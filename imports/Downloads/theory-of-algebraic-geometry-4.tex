\section{Construction of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

\subsection*{Problem statement}

We check the details of the construction of \(\Proj\) in lecture. Recall, in analogy with
\(\Spec A\), an affine scheme, we can define a projective scheme.

Let
\[
S=\bigoplus_{d=0}^{\infty}S_d
\]
be a graded ring. We will define \(\Proj S\).

\begin{enumerate}
	\item[(i)]
	We write
	\[
	S_+ = \bigoplus_{d=1}^{\infty} S_d,
	\]
	the irrelevant ideal. Define \(\Proj S\) to be the set of all homogeneous prime ideals
	of \(S\) not containing the irrelevant ideal.
	
	If \(I\subseteq S\) is a homogeneous ideal, let \(V(I)\) denote the set of all primes
	in \(\Proj S\) containing \(I\). Show these form the closed sets of a topology on
	\(\Proj S\).
	
	\item[(ii)]
	We define a sheaf \(\mathcal O\) on \(\Proj S\). For \(\mathfrak p\in \Proj S\), let
	\(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) be the set of elements of the localization \(S_{\mathfrak p}\)
	which are homogeneous of degree zero, that is, a ratio of two elements of \(S\) of
	the same degree.
	
	For \(U\subseteq \Proj S\) open, define \(\mathcal O(U)\) to be the set of functions
	\[
	s:U\longrightarrow \coprod_{\mathfrak p\in U} S_{(\mathfrak p)}
	\]
	such that \(s(\mathfrak p)\in S_{(\mathfrak p)}\), and every point
	\(\mathfrak p\in U\) has an open neighbourhood \(V\) for which there exist
	homogeneous \(f,g\in S\) of the same degree, with
	\[
	g\notin \mathfrak q
	\]
	for all \(\mathfrak q\in V\), such that
	\[
	s(\mathfrak q)=\frac{f}{g}
	\]
	for all \(\mathfrak q\in V\).
	
	Then show:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item[(a)]
		The stalk of \(\mathcal O\) at \(\mathfrak q\) is
		\[
		\mathcal O_{\mathfrak q}\cong S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
		\]
		
		\item[(b)]
		For any homogeneous \(f\in S_+\), let \(D_+(f)\) be the set of primes of
		\(\Proj S\) not containing \(f\). Show the sets \(D_+(f)\) cover \(\Proj S\),
		and for each such open set there is an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces
		\[
		\left(D_+(f),\mathcal O|_{D_+(f)}\right)
		\cong
		\Spec S_{(f)}.
		\]
		Here \(S_{(f)}\) denotes the subring of elements of degree \(0\) in the localization
		\(S_f\).
		
		\emph{Hint.} It is easy to define a map
		\[
		\psi:D_+(f)\longrightarrow \Spec S_{(f)}.
		\]
		However, constructing its inverse \(\theta\) is not so easy. Given
		\(\mathfrak q\in \Spec S_{(f)}\), set
		\[
		\mathfrak q_n
		:=
		\left\{
		s\in S_n :
		\frac{s^{\deg f}}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q
		\right\}.
		\]
		Show that \(\mathfrak q_n\) is closed under addition and that
		\[
		\theta(\mathfrak q)
		=
		\bigoplus_n \mathfrak q_n
		\]
		is a homogeneous prime ideal in \(D_+(f)\).
		
		\item[(c)]
		Conclude that \(\Proj S\) is a scheme.
		
		\item[(d)]
		Show that if \(k\) is an algebraically closed field, then the set of closed points
		of
		\[
		\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
		\]
		is in one-to-one correspondence with points of
		\[
		(k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*
		\]
		with the usual action of \(k^*\) by scalar multiplication.
		
		Show that if
		\[
		I\subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
		\]
		is a homogeneous ideal, then the closed points of
		\[
		\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
		\]
		are in one-to-one correspondence with equivalence classes of points
		\[
		(a_0,\dots,a_n)\in (k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*
		\]
		such that
		\[
		F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
		\]
		for all homogeneous \(F\in I\).
	\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Solution}

Let
\[
S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
\]
be a graded ring. The irrelevant ideal is
\[
S_+ = \bigoplus_{d\ge 1}S_d.
\]
We define
\[
\Proj S
=
\left\{
\mathfrak p\subset S :
\mathfrak p \text{ is homogeneous prime and } S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p
\right\}.
\]

\subsection{The topology on \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

For a homogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S\), define
\[
V(I)
=
\left\{
\mathfrak p\in \Proj S : I\subseteq \mathfrak p
\right\}.
\]

We prove that these subsets form the closed sets of a topology.

First,
\[
V(0)=\Proj S,
\]
because every prime ideal contains \(0\).

Also,
\[
V(S_+)=\varnothing,
\]
because by definition no point of \(\Proj S\) contains \(S_+\).

Now let \(\{I_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}\) be a family of homogeneous ideals. Then
\[
\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}V(I_\alpha)
=
V\left(\sum_{\alpha\in A}I_\alpha\right).
\]
Indeed, a prime ideal contains every \(I_\alpha\) if and only if it contains their sum.

Thus arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed.

Now let \(I,J\subseteq S\) be homogeneous ideals. Then
\[
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]
Indeed, if \(\mathfrak p\supseteq I\), then \(\mathfrak p\supseteq IJ\), and similarly if
\(\mathfrak p\supseteq J\).

Conversely, suppose
\[
IJ\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
If \(I\not\subseteq \mathfrak p\), choose \(a\in I\) with \(a\notin \mathfrak p\). Then for every
\(b\in J\),
\[
ab\in IJ\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime and \(a\notin \mathfrak p\), we get
\[
b\in \mathfrak p.
\]
Hence \(J\subseteq \mathfrak p\). Therefore
\[
\mathfrak p\in V(I)\cup V(J).
\]

So finite unions of closed sets are closed.

Hence the sets
\[
V(I)
\]
for homogeneous ideals \(I\subseteq S\) define the closed sets of a topology on
\(\Proj S\).

\subsection{Basic open subsets}

For a homogeneous element \(f\in S\), define
\[
D_+(f)
=
\Proj S\setminus V((f)).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
D_+(f)
=
\left\{
\mathfrak p\in \Proj S : f\notin \mathfrak p
\right\}.
\]

These are the projective analogues of the affine basic open sets
\[
D(f)\subseteq \Spec A.
\]

They satisfy
\[
D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg).
\]
Indeed,
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)
\]
if and only if
\[
f\notin \mathfrak p
\quad\text{and}\quad
g\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime, this is equivalent to
\[
fg\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Hence
\[
D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg).
\]

The sets \(D_+(f)\), where \(f\in S_+\) is homogeneous, cover \(\Proj S\). Indeed, if
\[
\mathfrak p\in \Proj S,
\]
then
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
So there exists a homogeneous element
\[
f\in S_+
\]
such that
\[
f\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Thus
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]

Therefore the \(D_+(f)\) cover \(\Proj S\).

\subsection{The rings \texorpdfstring{$S_{(\mathfrak p)}$}{S(p)}}

Let \(\mathfrak p\in \Proj S\). We define
\[
S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
to be the degree-zero part of the homogeneous localization of \(S\) at
\(\mathfrak p\).

Concretely,
\[
S_{(\mathfrak p)}
=
\left\{
\frac{a}{b} :
a,b\in S \text{ homogeneous},\ \deg a=\deg b,\ b\notin \mathfrak p
\right\}.
\]

This is a local ring. Its maximal ideal is
\[
\mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}
=
\left\{
\frac{a}{b}\in S_{(\mathfrak p)} : a\in \mathfrak p
\right\}.
\]

Indeed, if
\[
\frac{a}{b}\notin \mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)},
\]
then
\[
a\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Since also \(b\notin \mathfrak p\), the inverse
\[
\frac{b}{a}
\]
is defined in \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\). Therefore every element outside
\(\mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}\) is invertible. Hence \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) is local.

\subsection{The structure sheaf}

For an open subset \(U\subseteq \Proj S\), the ring \(\mathcal O(U)\) consists of
functions
\[
s:U\longrightarrow \coprod_{\mathfrak p\in U}S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
such that
\[
s(\mathfrak p)\in S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
for each \(\mathfrak p\in U\), and such that \(s\) is locally represented by a fraction
\[
\frac{f}{g}
\]
where \(f,g\in S\) are homogeneous of the same degree.

This is exactly the projective analogue of the structure sheaf on \(\Spec A\), except
that only degree-zero homogeneous fractions are allowed.

\subsection{The stalk of \texorpdfstring{$\mathcal O$}{O} at a point}

We prove that
\[
\mathcal O_{\mathfrak q}\cong S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
\]

Define
\[
\Phi:\mathcal O_{\mathfrak q}\longrightarrow S_{(\mathfrak q)}
\]
by evaluation at \(\mathfrak q\). If a germ is represented by a section \(s\in\mathcal O(U)\),
with \(\mathfrak q\in U\), define
\[
\Phi(s_{\mathfrak q})=s(\mathfrak q).
\]

This is a ring homomorphism.

We show that \(\Phi\) is surjective. Let
\[
\frac{a}{b}\in S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
\]
Then \(a,b\) are homogeneous of the same degree and
\[
b\notin \mathfrak q.
\]
Hence
\[
\mathfrak q\in D_+(b).
\]
Define a section on \(D_+(b)\) by
\[
s(\mathfrak p)=\frac{a}{b}.
\]
This is regular by definition. Its germ at \(\mathfrak q\) maps to \(a/b\). Therefore
\(\Phi\) is surjective.

Now suppose
\[
\Phi(s_{\mathfrak q})=0.
\]
Since \(s\) is locally a homogeneous fraction, after shrinking around \(\mathfrak q\) we may
write
\[
s(\mathfrak p)=\frac{a}{b}
\]
on an open neighbourhood \(V\) of \(\mathfrak q\), where \(a,b\) are homogeneous of the
same degree and \(b\notin\mathfrak p\) for all \(\mathfrak p\in V\).

The equality
\[
s(\mathfrak q)=0
\]
means
\[
\frac{a}{b}=0
\]
in \(S_{(\mathfrak q)}\). Hence there exists a homogeneous element
\[
h\notin \mathfrak q
\]
such that
\[
ha=0.
\]
Now restrict to
\[
V\cap D_+(h).
\]
On this smaller neighbourhood, \(h\notin \mathfrak p\), so
\[
\frac{a}{b}=0
\]
in \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) for every \(\mathfrak p\in V\cap D_+(h)\). Hence the germ
\(s_{\mathfrak q}\) is zero.

Therefore \(\Phi\) is injective and surjective. Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\mathcal O_{\mathfrak q}\cong S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
}
\]

\subsection{The affine open pieces}

Let \(f\in S_+\) be homogeneous and set
\[
d=\deg f.
\]
The localization \(S_f\) is naturally graded. Let
\[
S_{(f)}
=
(S_f)_0
\]
be its degree-zero part.

Explicitly,
\[
S_{(f)}
=
\left\{
\frac{a}{f^m} :
a\in S_{md}
\right\}.
\]

We prove that
\[
\left(D_+(f),\mathcal O|_{D_+(f)}\right)
\cong
\Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

\subsection{The map \texorpdfstring{$D_+(f)\to \Spec S_{(f)}$}{D+(f) to Spec S(f)}}

Let
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]
Then \(f\notin \mathfrak p\), so we can localize \(\mathfrak p\) in \(S_f\). Define
\[
\psi(\mathfrak p)
=
\mathfrak pS_f\cap S_{(f)}.
\]

This is a prime ideal of \(S_{(f)}\), because \(\mathfrak pS_f\) is prime in \(S_f\), and
the contraction of a prime ideal is prime.

Therefore we have a map
\[
\psi:D_+(f)\longrightarrow \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

\subsection{The inverse map}

Now let
\[
\mathfrak q\in \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]
We construct a homogeneous prime ideal of \(S\).

For \(n\ge 0\), define
\[
\mathfrak q_n
=
\left\{
s\in S_n :
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q
\right\}.
\]
This makes sense because if \(s\in S_n\), then
\[
\deg(s^d)=dn
\]
and
\[
\deg(f^n)=dn,
\]
so
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}
\]
has degree zero and belongs to \(S_{(f)}\).

Now set
\[
\theta(\mathfrak q)
=
\bigoplus_{n\ge 0}\mathfrak q_n.
\]

We show that \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is a homogeneous prime ideal.

First, by construction,
\[
\mathfrak q_n\subseteq S_n.
\]
Therefore \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is homogeneous.

Now we prove that each \(\mathfrak q_n\) is closed under addition. Let
\[
s,t\in \mathfrak q_n.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q,
\qquad
\frac{t^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
We need to show that
\[
s+t\in \mathfrak q_n,
\]
that is,
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q.
\]

By the binomial theorem,
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}
=
\sum_{i=0}^d
\binom di
\frac{s^i t^{d-i}}{f^n}.
\]
Each summand has degree zero.

For each \(i\), consider
\[
\frac{s^i t^{d-i}}{f^n}.
\]
Its \(d\)-th power is
\[
\left(\frac{s^i t^{d-i}}{f^n}\right)^d
=
\left(\frac{s^d}{f^n}\right)^i
\left(\frac{t^d}{f^n}\right)^{d-i}.
\]
Since
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n},\frac{t^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q,
\]
the right-hand side lies in \(\mathfrak q\). Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, it is radical.
Therefore
\[
\frac{s^i t^{d-i}}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
Hence every term in the binomial expansion lies in \(\mathfrak q\), so
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
Thus
\[
s+t\in \mathfrak q_n.
\]

Now we show that \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is an ideal. Let
\[
s\in \mathfrak q_n
\]
and let
\[
r\in S_m
\]
be homogeneous. Then
\[
rs\in S_{m+n}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\frac{(rs)^d}{f^{m+n}}
=
\frac{r^d}{f^m}\cdot \frac{s^d}{f^n}.
\]
The first factor belongs to \(S_{(f)}\), and the second factor belongs to \(\mathfrak q\).
Therefore
\[
\frac{(rs)^d}{f^{m+n}}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
Hence
\[
rs\in \mathfrak q_{m+n}.
\]
So \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is a homogeneous ideal.

Now we prove that \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is prime. Let \(a\in S_m\) and \(b\in S_n\) be
homogeneous, and suppose
\[
ab\in \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]
Then
\[
ab\in \mathfrak q_{m+n}.
\]
Hence
\[
\frac{(ab)^d}{f^{m+n}}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
But
\[
\frac{(ab)^d}{f^{m+n}}
=
\frac{a^d}{f^m}\cdot \frac{b^d}{f^n}.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, either
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^m}\in \mathfrak q
\]
or
\[
\frac{b^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
Thus either
\[
a\in \mathfrak q_m
\]
or
\[
b\in \mathfrak q_n.
\]
Therefore either
\[
a\in \theta(\mathfrak q)
\]
or
\[
b\in \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]
So \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is prime.

Finally,
\[
f\notin \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]
Indeed, \(f\in S_d\), and
\[
\frac{f^d}{f^d}=1\notin \mathfrak q.
\]
So
\[
f\notin \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]
Therefore
\[
\theta(\mathfrak q)\in D_+(f).
\]

Thus we have constructed a map
\[
\theta:\Spec S_{(f)}\longrightarrow D_+(f).
\]

\subsection{The two maps are inverse}

We now show that
\[
\psi\circ\theta=\operatorname{id}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\theta\circ\psi=\operatorname{id}.
\]

Let
\[
\mathfrak q\in \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]
An element of \(S_{(f)}\) has the form
\[
\frac{a}{f^m},
\]
where \(a\in S_{md}\). Then
\[
\frac{a}{f^m}\in \psi(\theta(\mathfrak q))
\]
if and only if
\[
a\in \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]
By definition of \(\theta\), this is equivalent to
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^{md}}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
But
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^{md}}
=
\left(\frac{a}{f^m}\right)^d.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, hence radical, this is equivalent to
\[
\frac{a}{f^m}\in \mathfrak q.
\]
Therefore
\[
\psi(\theta(\mathfrak q))=\mathfrak q.
\]

Conversely, let
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]
For a homogeneous element \(s\in S_n\), we have
\[
s\in \theta(\psi(\mathfrak p))
\]
if and only if
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in \psi(\mathfrak p).
\]
This is equivalent to
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in \mathfrak pS_f.
\]
Since \(f\notin \mathfrak p\), this is equivalent to
\[
s^d\in \mathfrak p.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime, this is equivalent to
\[
s\in \mathfrak p.
\]
Hence
\[
\theta(\psi(\mathfrak p))=\mathfrak p.
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}
\]
as topological spaces.

\subsection{Compatibility with the structure sheaves}

The homeomorphism above also identifies the structure sheaves.

Indeed, on \(D_+(f)\), sections of \(\mathcal O_{\Proj S}\) are locally represented by
fractions
\[
\frac{a}{b}
\]
where \(a,b\) are homogeneous of the same degree.

On \(\Spec S_{(f)}\), sections of the affine structure sheaf are locally represented by
fractions in localizations of \(S_{(f)}\).

These two descriptions agree under the correspondence
\[
D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

More explicitly, if \(g\in S\) is homogeneous, then
\[
D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)
\]
corresponds to the affine principal open subset
\[
D\left(\frac{g^d}{f^{\deg g}}\right)
\subseteq \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]
On these basic opens, both sheaves are described by the same degree-zero localized
fractions.

Therefore we obtain an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces
\[
\boxed{
	\left(D_+(f),\mathcal O|_{D_+(f)}\right)
	\cong
	\Spec S_{(f)}.
}
\]

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S} is a scheme}

The open subsets
\[
D_+(f),
\qquad f\in S_+ \text{ homogeneous},
\]
cover \(\Proj S\).

For each such \(f\), we have shown that
\[
D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}
\]
as locally ringed spaces.

Therefore \(\Proj S\) is covered by affine schemes. Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj S \text{ is a scheme.}
}
\]

\section{Closed points of projective space}

Now let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
with the standard grading, and assume that \(k\) is algebraically closed.

Then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
is projective \(n\)-space over \(k\), denoted
\[
\mathbb P_k^n.
\]

We show that the closed points of \(\mathbb P_k^n\) are in one-to-one correspondence with
\[
(k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*.
\]

\subsection{From a projective point to a homogeneous prime}

Let
\[
a=(a_0,\dots,a_n)\in k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}.
\]
The corresponding projective point is written
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n].
\]

Define
\[
\mathfrak p_a
=
\left\{
F\in k[x_0,\dots,x_n] \text{ homogeneous} :
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\right\}.
\]

Equivalently, \(\mathfrak p_a\) is the kernel of the graded ring homomorphism
\[
k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\longrightarrow k[t]
\]
given by
\[
x_i\longmapsto a_i t.
\]

The kernel is homogeneous. It is prime because \(k[t]\) is an integral domain.

Also, since \(a\neq 0\), some \(a_i\neq 0\). Hence
\[
x_i\notin \mathfrak p_a.
\]
Therefore
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p_a.
\]
So
\[
\mathfrak p_a\in \Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n].
\]

If \(b=\lambda a\) for some \(\lambda\in k^*\), then for every homogeneous polynomial
\(F\) of degree \(m\),
\[
F(b)=F(\lambda a)=\lambda^m F(a).
\]
Thus
\[
F(a)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F(b)=0.
\]
Therefore
\[
\mathfrak p_a=\mathfrak p_b.
\]
So the homogeneous prime depends only on the equivalence class
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n].
\]

\subsection{From a closed point to a projective point}

Conversely, let
\[
\mathfrak p\in \Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
be a closed point.

Since
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p,
\]
there exists some \(i\) such that
\[
x_i\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Thus
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(x_i).
\]

But
\[
D_+(x_i)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_i},
\dots,
\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
\dots,
\frac{x_n}{x_i}
\right].
\]
This affine scheme is isomorphic to
\[
\mathbb A_k^n.
\]

Since \(k\) is algebraically closed, the closed points of \(\mathbb A_k^n\) are exactly
the \(k\)-rational points. Therefore \(\mathfrak p\) corresponds to a maximal ideal of
the form
\[
\left(
\frac{x_0}{x_i}-b_0,
\dots,
\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}-1},
\dots,
\frac{x_n}{x_i}-b_n
\right).
\]

This gives the projective point
\[
[b_0:\cdots:b_{i-1}:1:b_{i+1}:\cdots:b_n].
\]

Hence every closed point of \(\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\) arises from a projective point.

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	\left|\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\right|_{\mathrm{closed}}
	\cong
	(k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*.
}
\]

\section{Closed points of projective varieties}

Let
\[
I\subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
be a homogeneous ideal. Consider
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I.
\]

The closed points of this scheme are the closed points of projective space satisfying
the equations in \(I\).

Indeed, a point
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in \mathbb P_k^n
\]
corresponds to the homogeneous prime
\[
\mathfrak p_a
=
\left\{
F\in k[x_0,\dots,x_n] \text{ homogeneous} :
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\right\}.
\]

It lies in
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\]
if and only if
\[
I\subseteq \mathfrak p_a.
\]
This is equivalent to saying that
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\]
for every homogeneous polynomial
\[
F\in I.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	\left|
	\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
	\right|_{\mathrm{closed}}
	\cong
	\left\{
	[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in \mathbb P_k^n :
	F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
	\text{ for all homogeneous }F\in I
	\right\}.
}
\]

Equivalently, the closed points of
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\]
are exactly the projective equivalence classes of nonzero points
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\in k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
\]
satisfying all homogeneous equations in \(I\).

\section{Summary}

The construction of \(\Proj S\) is the projective analogue of the construction of
\(\Spec A\).

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
	\text{Affine case} & \text{Projective case} \\
	\hline
	\Spec A & \Proj S \\
	\text{prime ideals} & \text{homogeneous prime ideals not containing }S_+ \\
	V(I) & V(I)\subseteq \Proj S \\
	D(f) & D_+(f) \\
	A_f & S_{(f)} \\
	D(f)\cong \Spec A_f & D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}
\end{array}
\]

The key local fact is
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}.
}
\]

Since the sets \(D_+(f)\) cover \(\Proj S\), this proves that
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj S \text{ is a scheme.}
}
\]







\section{Theory of the Construction of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

The construction of \(\Proj S\) is the projective analogue of the construction of
\(\Spec A\).

In affine algebraic geometry, one begins with an ordinary commutative ring \(A\) and
forms
\[
\Spec A,
\]
the set of all prime ideals of \(A\).

In projective algebraic geometry, one begins with a graded ring
\[
S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
\]
and forms
\[
\Proj S,
\]
the set of homogeneous prime ideals of \(S\) which do not contain the irrelevant ideal.

Thus the basic analogy is:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
	\textbf{Affine geometry} & \textbf{Projective geometry} \\
	\hline
	A \text{ a ring} & S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d \text{ a graded ring} \\
	\Spec A & \Proj S \\
	\text{prime ideals} & \text{homogeneous prime ideals} \\
	\text{ordinary localization} & \text{degree-zero homogeneous localization}
\end{array}
\]

The key new features in the projective case are:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item the grading of the ring,
	\item the irrelevant ideal \(S_+\),
	\item the restriction to homogeneous prime ideals,
	\item the use of degree-zero fractions in the structure sheaf.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Graded Rings}

\begin{definition}
	A ring \(S\) is called a \emph{graded ring} if it is written as a direct sum of abelian
	groups
	\[
	S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
	\]
	such that multiplication respects degree:
	\[
	S_mS_n\subseteq S_{m+n}.
	\]
\end{definition}

An element \(s\in S_d\) is called \emph{homogeneous of degree \(d\)}.

Every element \(s\in S\) can be written uniquely as a finite sum
\[
s=s_0+s_1+\cdots+s_N,
\]
where
\[
s_i\in S_i.
\]

The elements \(s_i\) are called the homogeneous components of \(s\).

\begin{example}
	Let
	\[
	S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n].
	\]
	The standard grading is given by
	\[
	S_d=\{\text{homogeneous polynomials of degree }d\}.
	\]
	Thus
	\[
	S_0=k,
	\]
	\[
	S_1=\text{the vector space of linear forms},
	\]
	\[
	S_2=\text{the vector space of quadratic forms},
	\]
	and so on.
\end{example}

\subsection{The Irrelevant Ideal}

\begin{definition}
	Let
	\[
	S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
	\]
	be a graded ring. The \emph{irrelevant ideal} of \(S\) is
	\[
	S_+
	=
	\bigoplus_{d\ge 1}S_d.
	\]
\end{definition}

So \(S_+\) consists of all elements of positive degree.

In the standard graded polynomial ring
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n],
\]
we have
\[
S_+=(x_0,\dots,x_n).
\]

This ideal is called the irrelevant ideal because it corresponds to the forbidden origin
\[
(0,\dots,0)
\]
in affine space.

Projective space is built from nonzero vectors modulo scalar multiplication:
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\sim(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n),
\qquad \lambda\in k^*.
\]
The zero vector is not allowed. Therefore the ideal corresponding to the origin must be
excluded.

\subsection{Definition of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

\begin{definition}
	Let
	\[
	S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
	\]
	be a graded ring. Then
	\[
	\Proj S
	\]
	is the set of all homogeneous prime ideals \(\mathfrak p\subset S\) such that
	\[
	S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p.
	\]
	Thus
	\[
	\Proj S
	=
	\left\{
	\mathfrak p\subset S:
	\mathfrak p \text{ is homogeneous prime and } S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p
	\right\}.
	\]
\end{definition}

The condition
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p
\]
means that not every positive-degree homogeneous element lies in \(\mathfrak p\).

Equivalently, there exists some homogeneous element
\[
f\in S_+
\]
such that
\[
f\notin \mathfrak p.
\]

This is the algebraic version of the projective condition that at least one homogeneous
coordinate is nonzero.

\subsection{Why Only Homogeneous Prime Ideals?}

Projective geometry is invariant under scalar multiplication.

A point of projective \(n\)-space over \(k\) is an equivalence class
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n],
\]
where
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\in k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\},
\]
and
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\sim(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n)
\]
for every
\[
\lambda\in k^*.
\]

Homogeneous polynomials behave well under this equivalence relation.

If \(F\in k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\) is homogeneous of degree \(d\), then
\[
F(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n)
=
\lambda^dF(a_0,\dots,a_n).
\]

Hence
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\]
if and only if
\[
F(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n)=0.
\]

Therefore homogeneous equations define well-defined subsets of projective space.

Non-homogeneous equations generally do not define well-defined projective subsets.

For example,
\[
x_0+1=0
\]
is not homogeneous, and the condition is not invariant under scaling.

On the other hand,
\[
x_0^2+x_1x_2=0
\]
is homogeneous, and hence defines a projective hypersurface.

Therefore the geometry of projective schemes must be built from homogeneous ideals and
homogeneous prime ideals.

\subsection{Closed Sets in \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

Let \(I\subseteq S\) be a homogeneous ideal.

Define
\[
V_+(I)
=
\{\mathfrak p\in \Proj S : I\subseteq \mathfrak p\}.
\]

These are the projective closed sets.

\begin{proposition}
	The sets
	\[
	V_+(I),
	\]
	where \(I\subseteq S\) is a homogeneous ideal, define the closed sets of a topology on
	\(\Proj S\).
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Proof.}
First,
\[
V_+(0)=\Proj S,
\]
because every prime ideal contains \(0\).

Also,
\[
V_+(S_+)=\varnothing,
\]
because by definition no point of \(\Proj S\) contains \(S_+\).

Let \(\{I_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}\) be a family of homogeneous ideals. Then
\[
\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}V_+(I_\alpha)
=
V_+\left(\sum_{\alpha\in A}I_\alpha\right).
\]
Indeed, a prime ideal contains every \(I_\alpha\) if and only if it contains their sum.

Thus arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed.

Now let \(I,J\subseteq S\) be homogeneous ideals. Then
\[
V_+(I)\cup V_+(J)=V_+(IJ).
\]

If \(\mathfrak p\in V_+(I)\cup V_+(J)\), then either
\[
I\subseteq \mathfrak p
\]
or
\[
J\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
In either case,
\[
IJ\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
Thus
\[
\mathfrak p\in V_+(IJ).
\]

Conversely, suppose
\[
IJ\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
If
\[
I\not\subseteq \mathfrak p,
\]
choose
\[
a\in I
\]
with
\[
a\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
For every \(b\in J\), we have
\[
ab\in IJ\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime and \(a\notin \mathfrak p\), it follows that
\[
b\in \mathfrak p.
\]
Hence
\[
J\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
Therefore
\[
\mathfrak p\in V_+(I)\cup V_+(J).
\]

Thus finite unions of closed sets are closed.

Therefore the sets \(V_+(I)\) define the closed sets of a topology on \(\Proj S\).
\(\square\)

\subsection{Basic Open Sets}

For a homogeneous element \(f\in S\), define
\[
D_+(f)
=
\Proj S\setminus V_+((f)).
\]

Equivalently,
\[
D_+(f)
=
\{\mathfrak p\in \Proj S : f\notin \mathfrak p\}.
\]

These sets are the projective analogues of the affine basic open sets
\[
D(f)\subseteq \Spec A.
\]

\begin{proposition}
	If \(f,g\in S\) are homogeneous, then
	\[
	D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg).
	\]
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Proof.}
Let \(\mathfrak p\in \Proj S\). Then
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)
\]
if and only if
\[
f\notin\mathfrak p
\quad\text{and}\quad
g\notin\mathfrak p.
\]
Since \(\mathfrak p\) is prime, this is equivalent to
\[
fg\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Therefore
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(fg).
\]
Hence
\[
D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)=D_+(fg).
\]
\(\square\)

\begin{proposition}
	The sets \(D_+(f)\), where \(f\in S_+\) is homogeneous, cover \(\Proj S\).
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Proof.}
Let
\[
\mathfrak p\in \Proj S.
\]
By definition,
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]
Therefore there exists some homogeneous element
\[
f\in S_+
\]
such that
\[
f\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Hence
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]
Therefore the sets \(D_+(f)\) cover \(\Proj S\).
\(\square\)

\subsection{Degree-Zero Localizations}

The essential difference between \(\Spec A\) and \(\Proj S\) appears in the local rings.

In affine geometry, if \(\mathfrak p\in\Spec A\), then the local ring is
\[
A_{\mathfrak p}.
\]

In projective geometry, if \(\mathfrak p\in\Proj S\), we do not take all of
\(S_{\mathfrak p}\). Instead, we take only the degree-zero part.

\begin{definition}
	Let \(\mathfrak p\in\Proj S\). Define
	\[
	S_{(\mathfrak p)}
	\]
	to be the degree-zero part of the homogeneous localization of \(S\) at \(\mathfrak p\).
	
	Concretely,
	\[
	S_{(\mathfrak p)}
	=
	\left\{
	\frac{a}{b}:
	a,b\in S \text{ homogeneous},\
	\deg a=\deg b,\
	b\notin \mathfrak p
	\right\}.
	\]
\end{definition}

The condition
\[
\deg a=\deg b
\]
ensures that the fraction
\[
\frac{a}{b}
\]
has degree zero.

\subsection{Why Degree Zero?}

The degree-zero condition is the algebraic form of projective invariance.

A regular function on projective space should be independent of the chosen representative
of a projective point.

If
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\sim(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n),
\]
then a well-defined projective function should have the same value on both representatives.

Let \(F,G\in k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\) be homogeneous polynomials of the same degree \(d\).
Then
\[
F(\lambda a)=\lambda^dF(a)
\]
and
\[
G(\lambda a)=\lambda^dG(a).
\]
Therefore
\[
\frac{F(\lambda a)}{G(\lambda a)}
=
\frac{\lambda^dF(a)}{\lambda^dG(a)}
=
\frac{F(a)}{G(a)}.
\]

Thus ratios of homogeneous polynomials of the same degree are invariant under scalar
multiplication.

This is why regular functions on projective schemes are locally represented by
degree-zero homogeneous fractions.

\subsection{The Local Ring \texorpdfstring{$S_{(\mathfrak p)}$}{S(p)}}

\begin{proposition}
	For every \(\mathfrak p\in \Proj S\), the ring \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) is local.
	Its maximal ideal is
	\[
	\mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}
	=
	\left\{
	\frac{a}{b}\in S_{(\mathfrak p)}:
	a\in\mathfrak p
	\right\}.
	\]
\end{proposition}

\textbf{Proof.}
Let
\[
\frac{a}{b}\in S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
with
\[
a,b
\]
homogeneous of the same degree and
\[
b\notin \mathfrak p.
\]

Suppose
\[
\frac{a}{b}\notin \mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}.
\]
Then
\[
a\notin \mathfrak p.
\]
Since also
\[
b\notin \mathfrak p,
\]
the fraction
\[
\frac{b}{a}
\]
is defined in \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\). Moreover,
\[
\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{b}{a}=1.
\]
Thus every element outside \(\mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}\) is invertible.

Therefore \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) is a local ring, with maximal ideal
\[
\mathfrak p_{(\mathfrak p)}.
\]
\(\square\)

\section{The Structure Sheaf on \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

The structure sheaf on \(\Proj S\) is defined using local degree-zero fractions.

Let
\[
U\subseteq \Proj S
\]
be open.

A section
\[
s\in\mathcal O_{\Proj S}(U)
\]
is a function
\[
s:U\longrightarrow \coprod_{\mathfrak p\in U}S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
such that
\[
s(\mathfrak p)\in S_{(\mathfrak p)}
\]
for every
\[
\mathfrak p\in U,
\]
and such that \(s\) is locally represented by a homogeneous fraction of degree zero.

More explicitly, for every point
\[
\mathfrak p\in U,
\]
there exists an open neighbourhood
\[
V\subseteq U
\]
of \(\mathfrak p\), and homogeneous elements
\[
a,b\in S
\]
of the same degree, such that
\[
b\notin \mathfrak q
\]
for every
\[
\mathfrak q\in V,
\]
and
\[
s(\mathfrak q)=\frac{a}{b}
\]
in \(S_{(\mathfrak q)}\) for all
\[
\mathfrak q\in V.
\]

Thus locally regular functions on \(\Proj S\) are exactly degree-zero homogeneous
fractions.

\subsection{The Stalks of the Structure Sheaf}

The projective analogue of the affine identity
\[
\mathcal O_{\Spec A,\mathfrak p}\cong A_{\mathfrak p}
\]
is the following.

\begin{theorem}
	For every point
	\[
	\mathfrak q\in \Proj S,
	\]
	there is a natural isomorphism
	\[
	\mathcal O_{\Proj S,\mathfrak q}
	\cong
	S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
	\]
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Proof.}
Define
\[
\Phi:\mathcal O_{\Proj S,\mathfrak q}\longrightarrow S_{(\mathfrak q)}
\]
by evaluation at \(\mathfrak q\).

If a germ is represented by a section
\[
s\in \mathcal O_{\Proj S}(U),
\qquad \mathfrak q\in U,
\]
then define
\[
\Phi(s_{\mathfrak q})=s(\mathfrak q).
\]

This is a well-defined ring homomorphism.

We show that \(\Phi\) is surjective.

Let
\[
\frac{a}{b}\in S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
\]
Then \(a,b\in S\) are homogeneous of the same degree and
\[
b\notin\mathfrak q.
\]
Therefore
\[
\mathfrak q\in D_+(b).
\]
Define a section on \(D_+(b)\) by
\[
s(\mathfrak p)=\frac{a}{b}.
\]
This is a regular section by definition. Its germ at \(\mathfrak q\) maps to \(a/b\).
Thus \(\Phi\) is surjective.

Now we prove injectivity.

Suppose
\[
\Phi(s_{\mathfrak q})=0.
\]
Since \(s\) is locally represented by a homogeneous fraction, after shrinking around
\(\mathfrak q\), we may assume that
\[
s(\mathfrak p)=\frac{a}{b}
\]
on an open neighbourhood \(V\) of \(\mathfrak q\), where \(a,b\) are homogeneous of the
same degree and
\[
b\notin\mathfrak p
\]
for every \(\mathfrak p\in V\).

The equality
\[
s(\mathfrak q)=0
\]
means that
\[
\frac{a}{b}=0
\]
in \(S_{(\mathfrak q)}\).

Therefore there exists a homogeneous element
\[
h\notin\mathfrak q
\]
such that
\[
ha=0.
\]

Now restrict to the smaller neighbourhood
\[
V\cap D_+(h).
\]
On this neighbourhood,
\[
h\notin\mathfrak p.
\]
Since
\[
ha=0,
\]
we get
\[
\frac{a}{b}=0
\]
in \(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\) for all \(\mathfrak p\in V\cap D_+(h)\).

Hence \(s\) is zero on a neighbourhood of \(\mathfrak q\), so the germ \(s_{\mathfrak q}\)
is zero.

Thus \(\Phi\) is injective.

Therefore
\[
\mathcal O_{\Proj S,\mathfrak q}
\cong
S_{(\mathfrak q)}.
\]
\(\square\)

\subsection{Affine Charts of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

Let \(f\in S_+\) be homogeneous, and set
\[
d=\deg f.
\]

The localization \(S_f\) is naturally graded. Define
\[
S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0.
\]

Thus \(S_{(f)}\) is the degree-zero part of \(S_f\).

Concretely,
\[
S_{(f)}
=
\left\{
\frac{a}{f^m}:
a\in S_{md}
\right\}.
\]

The key local theorem is:

\begin{theorem}
	For every homogeneous \(f\in S_+\), there is an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces
	\[
	\left(D_+(f),\mathcal O_{\Proj S}|_{D_+(f)}\right)
	\cong
	\Spec S_{(f)}.
	\]
\end{theorem}

This theorem is the projective analogue of the affine identity
\[
D(f)\cong \Spec A_f.
\]

In the projective case, however, the ring is not the whole localization \(S_f\), but only
its degree-zero part:
\[
S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0.
\]

\subsection{The Map \texorpdfstring{$D_+(f)\to\Spec S_{(f)}$}{D+(f) to Spec S(f)}}

Let
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]
Then
\[
f\notin\mathfrak p.
\]
Therefore we can localize \(\mathfrak p\) in \(S_f\).

Define
\[
\psi(\mathfrak p)
=
\mathfrak pS_f\cap S_{(f)}.
\]

This is a prime ideal of \(S_{(f)}\), because \(\mathfrak pS_f\) is a prime ideal of \(S_f\),
and the contraction of a prime ideal is prime.

Thus we obtain a map
\[
\psi:D_+(f)\longrightarrow \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

\subsection{The Inverse Map}

The inverse construction is more subtle.

Let
\[
\mathfrak q\in \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

For each \(n\ge 0\), define
\[
\mathfrak q_n
=
\left\{
s\in S_n:
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q
\right\},
\]
where
\[
d=\deg f.
\]

This definition makes sense because if
\[
s\in S_n,
\]
then
\[
s^d\in S_{dn}
\]
and
\[
f^n\in S_{dn}.
\]
Thus
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}
\]
has degree zero, so it lies in \(S_{(f)}\).

Now define
\[
\theta(\mathfrak q)
=
\bigoplus_{n\ge 0}\mathfrak q_n.
\]

\begin{lemma}
	Each \(\mathfrak q_n\) is closed under addition.
\end{lemma}

\textbf{Proof.}
Let
\[
s,t\in\mathfrak q_n.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q
\]
and
\[
\frac{t^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

We must show that
\[
s+t\in \mathfrak q_n.
\]
That is, we must show
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

By the binomial theorem,
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}
=
\sum_{i=0}^d
\binom di
\frac{s^it^{d-i}}{f^n}.
\]

Each summand has degree zero.

Now observe that
\[
\left(
\frac{s^it^{d-i}}{f^n}
\right)^d
=
\left(\frac{s^d}{f^n}\right)^i
\left(\frac{t^d}{f^n}\right)^{d-i}.
\]

Since
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n},\frac{t^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q,
\]
the right-hand side lies in \(\mathfrak q\).

Because \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, it is radical. Therefore
\[
\frac{s^it^{d-i}}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

Hence every term in the binomial expansion lies in \(\mathfrak q\). Therefore
\[
\frac{(s+t)^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q.
\]
Thus
\[
s+t\in\mathfrak q_n.
\]
\(\square\)

\begin{lemma}
	The set
	\[
	\theta(\mathfrak q)=\bigoplus_{n\ge 0}\mathfrak q_n
	\]
	is a homogeneous ideal of \(S\).
\end{lemma}

\textbf{Proof.}
By construction,
\[
\mathfrak q_n\subseteq S_n.
\]
Therefore
\[
\theta(\mathfrak q)
\]
is homogeneous.

It remains to show that it is an ideal.

Let
\[
s\in\mathfrak q_n
\]
and let
\[
r\in S_m
\]
be homogeneous.

Then
\[
rs\in S_{m+n}.
\]

We compute:
\[
\frac{(rs)^d}{f^{m+n}}
=
\frac{r^d}{f^m}
\cdot
\frac{s^d}{f^n}.
\]

The first factor
\[
\frac{r^d}{f^m}
\]
has degree zero, so it belongs to \(S_{(f)}\).

The second factor belongs to \(\mathfrak q\), because \(s\in\mathfrak q_n\).

Therefore
\[
\frac{(rs)^d}{f^{m+n}}\in\mathfrak q.
\]
Hence
\[
rs\in \mathfrak q_{m+n}.
\]

Therefore \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is a homogeneous ideal of \(S\).
\(\square\)

\begin{lemma}
	The homogeneous ideal
	\[
	\theta(\mathfrak q)
	\]
	is prime.
\end{lemma}

\textbf{Proof.}
Let
\[
a\in S_m,
\qquad
b\in S_n
\]
be homogeneous elements, and suppose
\[
ab\in \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]

Then
\[
ab\in\mathfrak q_{m+n}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\frac{(ab)^d}{f^{m+n}}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

But
\[
\frac{(ab)^d}{f^{m+n}}
=
\frac{a^d}{f^m}
\cdot
\frac{b^d}{f^n}.
\]

Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, either
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^m}\in\mathfrak q
\]
or
\[
\frac{b^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

Thus either
\[
a\in\mathfrak q_m
\]
or
\[
b\in\mathfrak q_n.
\]

Therefore either
\[
a\in\theta(\mathfrak q)
\]
or
\[
b\in\theta(\mathfrak q).
\]

Hence \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) is prime.
\(\square\)

\begin{lemma}
	The prime ideal \(\theta(\mathfrak q)\) belongs to \(D_+(f)\).
\end{lemma}

\textbf{Proof.}
We must show that
\[
f\notin \theta(\mathfrak q).
\]

Since
\[
f\in S_d,
\]
we would have
\[
f\in\mathfrak q_d
\]
only if
\[
\frac{f^d}{f^d}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

But
\[
\frac{f^d}{f^d}=1,
\]
and no prime ideal contains \(1\).

Therefore
\[
f\notin\theta(\mathfrak q).
\]

Hence
\[
\theta(\mathfrak q)\in D_+(f).
\]
\(\square\)

Thus we obtain a map
\[
\theta:\Spec S_{(f)}\longrightarrow D_+(f).
\]

\subsection{The Maps Are Inverse}

We now show that
\[
\psi\circ\theta=\operatorname{id}
\]
and
\[
\theta\circ\psi=\operatorname{id}.
\]

Let
\[
\mathfrak q\in\Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

An arbitrary element of \(S_{(f)}\) has the form
\[
\frac{a}{f^m},
\]
where
\[
a\in S_{md}.
\]

Then
\[
\frac{a}{f^m}\in \psi(\theta(\mathfrak q))
\]
if and only if
\[
a\in\theta(\mathfrak q).
\]

By definition of \(\theta\), this is equivalent to
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^{md}}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

But
\[
\frac{a^d}{f^{md}}
=
\left(\frac{a}{f^m}\right)^d.
\]

Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime, hence radical, this is equivalent to
\[
\frac{a}{f^m}\in\mathfrak q.
\]

Therefore
\[
\psi(\theta(\mathfrak q))=\mathfrak q.
\]

Conversely, let
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f).
\]

Let
\[
s\in S_n
\]
be homogeneous.

Then
\[
s\in\theta(\psi(\mathfrak p))
\]
if and only if
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in\psi(\mathfrak p).
\]

By definition of \(\psi\), this is equivalent to
\[
\frac{s^d}{f^n}\in\mathfrak pS_f.
\]

Since
\[
f\notin\mathfrak p,
\]
the denominator \(f^n\) is invertible after localization. Therefore this is equivalent to
\[
s^d\in\mathfrak p.
\]

Because \(\mathfrak p\) is prime, this is equivalent to
\[
s\in\mathfrak p.
\]

Thus every homogeneous element belongs to \(\theta(\psi(\mathfrak p))\) if and only if it
belongs to \(\mathfrak p\).

Since both ideals are homogeneous,
\[
\theta(\psi(\mathfrak p))=\mathfrak p.
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}
\]
as topological spaces.

\subsection{Compatibility with the Structure Sheaves}

The homeomorphism
\[
D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}
\]
also identifies the structure sheaves.

On \(D_+(f)\), sections of \(\mathcal O_{\Proj S}\) are locally represented by fractions
\[
\frac{a}{b},
\]
where \(a,b\in S\) are homogeneous of the same degree.

On \(\Spec S_{(f)}\), sections of the affine structure sheaf are locally represented by
fractions in localizations of \(S_{(f)}\).

These descriptions agree.

More concretely, if \(g\in S\) is homogeneous, then
\[
D_+(f)\cap D_+(g)
\]
corresponds to the affine principal open subset
\[
D\left(\frac{g^d}{f^{\deg g}}\right)
\subseteq
\Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

Indeed, for
\[
\mathfrak p\in D_+(f),
\]
we have
\[
g\notin\mathfrak p
\]
if and only if
\[
\frac{g^d}{f^{\deg g}}\notin \psi(\mathfrak p).
\]

On such basic opens, both structure sheaves are described by the same degree-zero
fractions.

Therefore there is an isomorphism of locally ringed spaces
\[
\left(D_+(f),\mathcal O_{\Proj S}|_{D_+(f)}\right)
\cong
\Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S} is a Scheme}

\begin{theorem}
	For every graded ring
	\[
	S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d,
	\]
	the locally ringed space \(\Proj S\) is a scheme.
\end{theorem}

\textbf{Proof.}
The open subsets
\[
D_+(f),
\qquad
f\in S_+ \text{ homogeneous},
\]
cover \(\Proj S\).

For each such \(f\), we have shown that
\[
D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}
\]
as locally ringed spaces.

Therefore \(\Proj S\) is covered by affine schemes.

Hence
\[
\Proj S
\]
is a scheme.
\(\square\)

\subsection{Projective Space as a \texorpdfstring{$\Proj$}{Proj}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
with the standard grading.

Then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
is projective \(n\)-space over \(k\), denoted
\[
\mathbb P_k^n.
\]

Thus
\[
\mathbb P_k^n
=
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n].
\]

\subsection{Affine Charts of Projective Space}

Consider
\[
\mathbb P_k^n
=
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n].
\]

For each \(i\), the open subset
\[
D_+(x_i)
\]
is the set of projective points where
\[
x_i\neq 0.
\]

Since \(x_i\neq 0\), we may divide all coordinates by \(x_i\).

The coordinate ring of this affine chart is
\[
k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_i},
\dots,
\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
\dots,
\frac{x_n}{x_i}
\right].
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(x_i)
\cong
\Spec
k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_i},
\dots,
\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
\dots,
\frac{x_n}{x_i}
\right]
\cong
\mathbb A_k^n.
\]

Thus projective space is covered by \(n+1\) affine charts:
\[
\mathbb P_k^n
=
D_+(x_0)\cup D_+(x_1)\cup\cdots\cup D_+(x_n).
\]

Each chart is affine \(n\)-space.

\subsection{Example: The Projective Line}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1].
\]

Then
\[
\Proj S=\mathbb P_k^1.
\]

There are two standard affine charts:
\[
D_+(x_0)
\]
and
\[
D_+(x_1).
\]

On \(D_+(x_0)\), define
\[
t=\frac{x_1}{x_0}.
\]
Then
\[
D_+(x_0)\cong \Spec k[t].
\]

On \(D_+(x_1)\), define
\[
u=\frac{x_0}{x_1}.
\]
Then
\[
D_+(x_1)\cong \Spec k[u].
\]

On the overlap
\[
D_+(x_0)\cap D_+(x_1),
\]
we have
\[
u=\frac{1}{t}.
\]

Thus \(\mathbb P_k^1\) is obtained by gluing two affine lines
\[
\mathbb A_k^1
\]
along the open subsets where \(t\neq 0\) and \(u\neq 0\), using the transition rule
\[
u=t^{-1}.
\]

\subsection{Example: The Projective Plane}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2].
\]

Then
\[
\Proj S=\mathbb P_k^2.
\]

The standard affine charts are
\[
D_+(x_0),
\qquad
D_+(x_1),
\qquad
D_+(x_2).
\]

On \(D_+(x_0)\),
\[
D_+(x_0)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
\right]
\cong
\mathbb A_k^2.
\]

On \(D_+(x_1)\),
\[
D_+(x_1)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\frac{x_2}{x_1}
\right]
\cong
\mathbb A_k^2.
\]

On \(D_+(x_2)\),
\[
D_+(x_2)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_2},
\frac{x_1}{x_2}
\right]
\cong
\mathbb A_k^2.
\]

Thus projective \(2\)-space is obtained by gluing three affine planes.

\subsection{Closed Points of Projective Space}

Assume now that \(k\) is algebraically closed.

The closed points of
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
are exactly the usual projective points
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n].
\]

Equivalently,
\[
\left|\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\right|_{\mathrm{closed}}
\cong
(k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*.
\]

The equivalence relation is
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\sim(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n),
\qquad
\lambda\in k^*.
\]

A point
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n]
\]
corresponds to the homogeneous prime ideal
\[
\mathfrak p_a
=
\left\{
F\in k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\text{ homogeneous}:
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\right\}.
\]

This ideal depends only on the projective class.

Indeed, if
\[
b=\lambda a,
\qquad \lambda\in k^*,
\]
and \(F\) is homogeneous of degree \(d\), then
\[
F(b)=F(\lambda a)=\lambda^dF(a).
\]
Therefore
\[
F(a)=0
\]
if and only if
\[
F(b)=0.
\]
Thus
\[
\mathfrak p_a=\mathfrak p_b.
\]

Conversely, every closed point of projective space lies in some affine chart
\[
D_+(x_i)\cong\mathbb A_k^n.
\]
Since \(k\) is algebraically closed, the closed points of affine \(n\)-space are the
usual \(k\)-rational points. Therefore every closed point of projective space comes from
a projective point
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n].
\]

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\left|\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]\right|_{\mathrm{closed}}
	\cong
	(k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/k^*.
}
\]

\subsection{Closed Points of Projective Varieties}

Let
\[
I\subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
be a homogeneous ideal.

Then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\]
is the projective scheme cut out by the homogeneous equations in \(I\).

Its closed points are precisely the projective points
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in\mathbb P_k^n
\]
such that
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\]
for every homogeneous polynomial
\[
F\in I.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\left|
	\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
	\right|_{\mathrm{closed}}
	\cong
	\left\{
	[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in\mathbb P_k^n:
	F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
	\text{ for all homogeneous }F\in I
	\right\}.
}
\]

Equivalently, the closed points of
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\]
are projective equivalence classes of nonzero points
\[
(a_0,\dots,a_n)\in k^{n+1}\setminus\{0\}
\]
satisfying all homogeneous equations in \(I\).

\subsection{Conceptual Summary}

The construction of \(\Proj S\) does three things.

First, it keeps only homogeneous data, because projective geometry is invariant under
scaling.

Second, it removes the irrelevant ideal \(S_+\), because \(S_+\) corresponds to the
forbidden origin.

Third, it defines local affine charts by inverting homogeneous elements and taking
degree-zero fractions:
\[
D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj S
	=
	\text{the scheme obtained by gluing the affine charts }
	\Spec S_{(f)}
	\text{ for homogeneous }f\in S_+.
}
\]

\subsection{Conceptual Diagram}

\[
\begin{array}{c}
	\text{graded ring }S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	\text{homogeneous prime ideals }\mathfrak p
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	\text{remove primes containing }S_+
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	\Proj S
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	\text{basic opens }D_+(f)
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}
	\\[6pt]
	\Downarrow
	\\[6pt]
	\Proj S\text{ is a scheme}
\end{array}
\]

\subsection{Final Dictionary}

\[
\begin{array}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6cm}
		|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7cm}}
	\toprule
	\textbf{Affine construction} & \textbf{Projective construction} \\
	\midrule
	\(A\) is a ring
	&
	\(S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d\) is a graded ring
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Spec A\)
	&
	\(\Proj S\)
	\\
	\midrule
	prime ideals
	&
	homogeneous prime ideals not containing \(S_+\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(V(I)\)
	&
	\(V_+(I)\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D(f)\)
	&
	\(D_+(f)\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(A_f\)
	&
	\(S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D(f)\cong\Spec A_f\)
	&
	\(D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(A_{\mathfrak p}\)
	&
	\(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\mathcal O_{\Spec A,\mathfrak p}\cong A_{\mathfrak p}\)
	&
	\(\mathcal O_{\Proj S,\mathfrak p}\cong S_{(\mathfrak p)}\)
	\\
	\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]

The most important local formula is
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}.
}
\]

The most important conceptual idea is
\[
\boxed{
	\text{projective regular functions are degree-zero homogeneous fractions.}
}
\]

\section{Examples of the Construction of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

In this section we give examples illustrating the construction of
\[
\Proj S
\]
for graded rings. The main ideas are:

\[
\Proj S
=
\{\text{homogeneous prime ideals of }S\text{ not containing }S_+\},
\]
where
\[
S_+=\bigoplus_{d\ge 1}S_d
\]
is the irrelevant ideal.

The most important local fact is
\[
D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)},
\]
where
\[
S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0
\]
is the degree-zero part of the localization \(S_f\).

Thus, in examples, one usually studies \(\Proj S\) by looking at affine charts
\[
D_+(f).
\]

\subsection{The Basic Example: \texorpdfstring{$\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]$}{Proj k[x0,...,xn]}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
with the standard grading:
\[
S_d=\{\text{homogeneous polynomials of degree }d\}.
\]

Then
\[
S_+=(x_0,\dots,x_n).
\]

By definition,
\[
\Proj S
\]
is the set of homogeneous prime ideals of \(S\) not containing \(S_+\).

The fundamental example is
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]=\PP_k^n.
}
\]

Thus ordinary projective \(n\)-space is the \(\Proj\) of the standard graded polynomial ring.

The basic affine charts are
\[
D_+(x_i)
=
\{\mathfrak p\in \Proj S:x_i\notin\mathfrak p\}.
\]

Each chart is affine \(n\)-space:
\[
D_+(x_i)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_i},
\dots,
\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
\dots,
\frac{x_n}{x_i}
\right]
\cong
\AA_k^n.
\]

Therefore
\[
\PP_k^n
=
D_+(x_0)\cup D_+(x_1)\cup\cdots\cup D_+(x_n),
\]
and each \(D_+(x_i)\) is an affine chart.

\subsection{Example: \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^1$}{P1}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1].
\]

Then
\[
\Proj S=\PP_k^1.
\]

There are two standard affine charts:
\[
D_+(x_0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
D_+(x_1).
\]

On \(D_+(x_0)\), we invert \(x_0\). The degree-zero part of \(S_{x_0}\) is
\[
S_{(x_0)}
=
k\left[\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right].
\]

Set
\[
t=\frac{x_1}{x_0}.
\]

Then
\[
D_+(x_0)
\cong
\Spec k[t]
\cong
\AA_k^1.
\]

On the other chart,
\[
S_{(x_1)}
=
k\left[\frac{x_0}{x_1}\right].
\]

Set
\[
u=\frac{x_0}{x_1}.
\]

Then
\[
D_+(x_1)
\cong
\Spec k[u]
\cong
\AA_k^1.
\]

On the overlap,
\[
D_+(x_0)\cap D_+(x_1),
\]
we have
\[
u
=
\frac{x_0}{x_1}
=
\left(\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right)^{-1}
=
t^{-1}.
\]

So
\[
\PP_k^1
\]
is obtained by gluing two affine lines along the open subsets
\[
t\neq 0
\]
and
\[
u\neq 0
\]
using the transition rule
\[
u=t^{-1}.
\]

Geometrically,
\[
\boxed{
	\PP_k^1
	=
	\AA_k^1\cup\{\infty\}.
}
\]

\subsection{Example: Closed Points of \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^1$}{P1}}

Assume \(k\) is algebraically closed.

A closed point of
\[
\PP_k^1=\Proj k[x_0,x_1]
\]
is a projective equivalence class
\[
[a_0:a_1],
\]
where
\[
(a_0,a_1)\neq (0,0),
\]
and
\[
[a_0:a_1]=[\lambda a_0:\lambda a_1]
\]
for all
\[
\lambda\in k^*.
\]

If
\[
a_0\neq 0,
\]
then
\[
[a_0:a_1]=[1:a_1/a_0].
\]

These points lie in the affine chart \(D_+(x_0)\).

If
\[
a_0=0,
\]
then since the point is nonzero, we must have
\[
a_1\neq 0.
\]
Hence
\[
[0:a_1]=[0:1].
\]

This is the point at infinity.

Therefore
\[
\PP_k^1(k)
=
\{[1:t]:t\in k\}\cup\{[0:1]\}.
\]

So
\[
\boxed{
	\PP_k^1(k)=k\cup\{\infty\}.
}
\]

\subsection{Example: \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^2$}{P2}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2].
\]

Then
\[
\Proj S=\PP_k^2.
\]

There are three standard affine charts:
\[
D_+(x_0),\qquad D_+(x_1),\qquad D_+(x_2).
\]

On \(D_+(x_0)\),
\[
S_{(x_0)}
=
k\left[
\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
\right].
\]

So
\[
D_+(x_0)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
\right]
\cong
\AA_k^2.
\]

Similarly,
\[
D_+(x_1)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\frac{x_2}{x_1}
\right]
\cong
\AA_k^2,
\]
and
\[
D_+(x_2)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_0}{x_2},
\frac{x_1}{x_2}
\right]
\cong
\AA_k^2.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\PP_k^2
	=
	D_+(x_0)\cup D_+(x_1)\cup D_+(x_2),
}
\]
where each chart is an affine plane.

\subsection{Example: A Projective Line Inside \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^2$}{P2}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2],
\]
and consider the homogeneous ideal
\[
I=(x_2).
\]

Then
\[
\Proj S/I
=
\Proj k[x_0,x_1,x_2]/(x_2).
\]

But
\[
k[x_0,x_1,x_2]/(x_2)
\cong
k[x_0,x_1].
\]

Therefore
\[
\Proj S/I
\cong
\Proj k[x_0,x_1]
=
\PP_k^1.
\]

Geometrically, the equation
\[
x_2=0
\]
defines a projective line in \(\PP_k^2\).

So
\[
\boxed{
	V_+(x_2)\subseteq \PP_k^2
	\text{ is a projective line.}
}
\]

Its closed points are
\[
[a_0:a_1:0].
\]

\subsection{Example: A Projective Conic}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2],
\]
and consider the homogeneous polynomial
\[
F=x_0x_2-x_1^2.
\]

Let
\[
I=(F).
\]

Then
\[
X=\Proj S/I
\]
is the projective conic
\[
x_0x_2=x_1^2
\]
inside
\[
\PP_k^2.
\]

Its closed points are projective classes
\[
[a_0:a_1:a_2]\in \PP_k^2
\]
such that
\[
a_0a_2-a_1^2=0.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	X(k)
	=
	\{[a_0:a_1:a_2]\in\PP_k^2:a_0a_2=a_1^2\}.
}
\]

Now look at the affine chart \(D_+(x_0)\). There we set
\[
u=\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\qquad
v=\frac{x_2}{x_0}.
\]

Divide the equation
\[
x_0x_2=x_1^2
\]
by \(x_0^2\). We get
\[
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
=
\left(\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right)^2.
\]

Thus on this chart,
\[
v=u^2.
\]

Therefore the conic locally looks like the affine parabola
\[
v=u^2.
\]

This shows how a homogeneous projective equation becomes an affine equation on a chart.

\subsection{Example: A Projective Hypersurface}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n],
\]
and let
\[
F\in S_d
\]
be homogeneous of degree \(d\).

Then
\[
X=\Proj S/(F)
\]
is the projective hypersurface
\[
F=0
\]
inside
\[
\PP_k^n.
\]

The closed points of \(X\) are
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in \PP_k^n
\]
such that
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0.
\]

So
\[
\boxed{
	X(k)
	=
	\{[a_0:\cdots:a_n]\in\PP_k^n:F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0\}.
}
\]

For example, if
\[
F=x_0^2+x_1^2+x_2^2,
\]
then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,x_1,x_2]/(F)
\]
is a projective conic.

If
\[
F=x_0^3+x_1^3+x_2^3,
\]
then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,x_1,x_2]/(F)
\]
is a plane cubic curve.

\subsection{Example: Why Non-Homogeneous Equations Do Not Work}

Consider
\[
f=x_0+1\in k[x_0,x_1].
\]

This is not homogeneous.

If we try to define a projective equation
\[
x_0+1=0,
\]
it does not make sense on projective points.

Suppose
\[
(a_0,a_1)
\]
satisfies
\[
a_0+1=0.
\]

Then
\[
a_0=-1.
\]

But the same projective point can be represented by
\[
(\lambda a_0,\lambda a_1).
\]

Then the equation becomes
\[
\lambda a_0+1=0.
\]

This condition is not equivalent to
\[
a_0+1=0.
\]

So the vanishing condition is not invariant under scaling.

That is why projective closed subsets must be defined by homogeneous equations.

By contrast, if
\[
F=x_0^2+x_1^2
\]
is homogeneous, then
\[
F(\lambda a_0,\lambda a_1)
=
\lambda^2F(a_0,a_1).
\]

Therefore
\[
F(a_0,a_1)=0
\]
if and only if
\[
F(\lambda a_0,\lambda a_1)=0.
\]

So homogeneous equations are well-defined on projective space.

\subsection{Example: Degree-Zero Fractions as Functions}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1].
\]

On
\[
\PP_k^1=\Proj S,
\]
the expression
\[
\frac{x_1}{x_0}
\]
is a valid regular function on the chart
\[
D_+(x_0).
\]

Indeed, \(x_1\) and \(x_0\) are homogeneous of the same degree:
\[
\deg x_1=\deg x_0=1.
\]

Hence
\[
\frac{x_1}{x_0}
\]
has degree zero.

It is also invariant under scaling:
\[
\frac{\lambda x_1}{\lambda x_0}
=
\frac{x_1}{x_0}.
\]

However,
\[
\frac{x_1^2}{x_0}
\]
is not degree zero, because
\[
\deg x_1^2=2,
\qquad
\deg x_0=1.
\]

So
\[
\frac{x_1^2}{x_0}
\]
is not a regular function on projective space.

The corresponding degree-zero expression would be
\[
\frac{x_1^2}{x_0^2}.
\]

Thus projective regular functions are locally represented by fractions
\[
\frac{F}{G}
\]
where
\[
\deg F=\deg G.
\]

\subsection{Example: Computing \texorpdfstring{$S_{(x_0)}$}{S(x0)}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2].
\]

We compute
\[
S_{(x_0)}.
\]

The localization is
\[
S_{x_0}
=
k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_0^{-1}].
\]

The degree-zero part consists of fractions
\[
\frac{F}{x_0^m},
\]
where \(F\) is homogeneous of degree \(m\).

Every such fraction can be written in terms of
\[
\frac{x_1}{x_0}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{x_2}{x_0}.
\]

Indeed, if
\[
F=x_1^a x_2^b x_0^c
\]
is a monomial of degree \(m\), then
\[
a+b+c=m.
\]

Therefore
\[
\frac{x_1^a x_2^b x_0^c}{x_0^m}
=
\left(\frac{x_1}{x_0}\right)^a
\left(\frac{x_2}{x_0}\right)^b.
\]

Hence
\[
S_{(x_0)}
=
k\left[
\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
\right].
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(x_0)
\cong
\Spec k\left[
\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\frac{x_2}{x_0}
\right].
\]

\subsection{Example: \texorpdfstring{$\Proj k[x]$}{Proj k[x]} is One Point}

Let
\[
S=k[x],
\]
with the standard grading
\[
\deg x=1.
\]

Then
\[
S_+=(x).
\]

The homogeneous prime ideals of \(k[x]\) are
\[
(0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(x).
\]

The ideal
\[
(x)
\]
contains \(S_+\), so it is excluded from \(\Proj S\).

The ideal
\[
(0)
\]
does not contain \(S_+\), so it remains.

Hence
\[
\Proj k[x]
\]
has one point.

Indeed,
\[
\Proj k[x]\cong \Spec k.
\]

The affine chart is
\[
D_+(x)=\Proj k[x],
\]
and
\[
S_{(x)}
=
(k[x]_x)_0
=
k.
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(x)\cong \Spec k.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj k[x]\cong \Spec k=\PP_k^0.
}
\]

\subsection{Example: \texorpdfstring{$\Proj k$}{Proj k} is Empty}

Let
\[
S=k
\]
with grading
\[
S_0=k,
\qquad
S_d=0
\quad\text{for }d>0.
\]

Then
\[
S_+=0.
\]

A point of \(\Proj S\) is a homogeneous prime ideal \(\mathfrak p\) such that
\[
S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p.
\]

But
\[
S_+=0
\]
is contained in every prime ideal.

Therefore there are no points.

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj k=\varnothing.
}
\]

This example shows why the irrelevant ideal condition matters.

\subsection{Example: Quotient by the Irrelevant Ideal Gives Empty \texorpdfstring{$\Proj$}{Proj}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n].
\]

Consider the quotient
\[
S/S_+
=
k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/(x_0,\dots,x_n)
\cong k.
\]

Then
\[
\Proj(S/S_+)
=
\Proj k
=
\varnothing.
\]

So killing all positive-degree elements destroys projective geometry.

Geometrically, this corresponds to keeping only the forbidden origin, which is removed in projective space.

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj(S/S_+)=\varnothing.
}
\]

\subsection{Example: A Non-Reduced Projective Scheme}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1],
\]
and let
\[
I=(x_1^2).
\]

Then
\[
X=\Proj S/I
=
\Proj k[x_0,x_1]/(x_1^2).
\]

The underlying closed subset is defined by
\[
x_1^2=0.
\]

Set-theoretically, this is the same as
\[
x_1=0.
\]

Thus the underlying topological space is one point:
\[
[1:0].
\]

But scheme-theoretically, \(X\) is not reduced.

On the chart
\[
D_+(x_0),
\]
set
\[
t=\frac{x_1}{x_0}.
\]

The equation
\[
x_1^2=0
\]
becomes
\[
t^2=0.
\]

So the affine chart is
\[
\Spec k[t]/(t^2).
\]

Therefore \(X\) is a doubled point in projective space.

This example shows that \(\Proj\) remembers scheme structure, not only the set of closed points.

\subsection{Example: A Projective Scheme with No Real Points}

Let
\[
k=\mathbb R.
\]

Consider
\[
X=\Proj \mathbb R[x_0,x_1]/(x_0^2+x_1^2).
\]

The real points would be
\[
[a_0:a_1]\in \PP_{\mathbb R}^1
\]
such that
\[
a_0^2+a_1^2=0.
\]

Over \(\mathbb R\), this equation has no nonzero solutions.

So
\[
X(\mathbb R)=\varnothing.
\]

But \(X\) is not necessarily the empty scheme. Over \(\mathbb C\), the equation factors:
\[
x_0^2+x_1^2
=
(x_0+ix_1)(x_0-ix_1).
\]

So after extending scalars to \(\mathbb C\), the scheme has two complex points.

This example shows the difference between the set of \(k\)-rational points
\[
X(k)
\]
and the scheme \(X\) itself.

\subsection{Example: The Veronese Embedding}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1].
\]

The degree-two homogeneous polynomials are generated by
\[
x_0^2,\qquad x_0x_1,\qquad x_1^2.
\]

Define a map
\[
\PP_k^1\longrightarrow \PP_k^2
\]
by
\[
[a_0:a_1]
\longmapsto
[a_0^2:a_0a_1:a_1^2].
\]

The image satisfies the equation
\[
Y_0Y_2-Y_1^2=0.
\]

Thus the image is the conic
\[
Y_0Y_2=Y_1^2
\]
in
\[
\PP_k^2.
\]

In scheme language, this is related to the graded subring
\[
k[x_0^2,x_0x_1,x_1^2]\subseteq k[x_0,x_1].
\]

The \(\Proj\) construction captures this projective embedding.

Thus the conic
\[
\Proj k[Y_0,Y_1,Y_2]/(Y_0Y_2-Y_1^2)
\]
is isomorphic to
\[
\PP_k^1.
\]

\subsection{Example: Projective Closure of an Affine Curve}

Start with the affine parabola
\[
y=x^2
\]
inside
\[
\AA_k^2.
\]

Its affine coordinate equation is
\[
y-x^2=0.
\]

To take the projective closure in \(\PP_k^2\), introduce homogeneous coordinates
\[
[X:Y:Z].
\]

On the affine chart \(Z\neq 0\), we have
\[
x=\frac{X}{Z},
\qquad
y=\frac{Y}{Z}.
\]

The equation
\[
y=x^2
\]
becomes
\[
\frac{Y}{Z}
=
\frac{X^2}{Z^2}.
\]

Multiplying by \(Z^2\), we get
\[
YZ=X^2.
\]

Thus the projective closure is
\[
\Proj k[X,Y,Z]/(YZ-X^2).
\]

On the affine chart \(Z\neq 0\), set
\[
x=\frac{X}{Z},
\qquad
y=\frac{Y}{Z}.
\]

Then
\[
YZ=X^2
\]
becomes
\[
y=x^2.
\]

The points at infinity satisfy
\[
Z=0.
\]

Plugging \(Z=0\) into
\[
YZ=X^2
\]
gives
\[
X^2=0.
\]

Thus
\[
X=0.
\]

So the point at infinity is
\[
[0:1:0].
\]

Therefore the projective closure of the affine parabola adds one point at infinity.

\subsection{Example: \texorpdfstring{$\Proj$}{Proj} of a Ring with an Unusual Grading}

Let
\[
S=k[x,y],
\]
but give it the grading
\[
\deg x=1,
\qquad
\deg y=2.
\]

Then
\[
S_+=(x,y).
\]

This is not the standard grading of a polynomial ring in two variables.

The scheme
\[
\Proj S
\]
is a weighted projective scheme, usually written
\[
\PP(1,2).
\]

Its points are homogeneous prime ideals not containing \((x,y)\), but the affine charts behave differently because \(x\) and \(y\) have different degrees.

For example,
\[
D_+(x)
\]
has coordinate ring
\[
S_{(x)}.
\]

Since
\[
\deg x=1,
\]
degree-zero fractions include
\[
\frac{y}{x^2}.
\]

Thus
\[
S_{(x)}
=
k\left[\frac{y}{x^2}\right].
\]

On the other hand, the chart \(D_+(y)\) involves degree-zero expressions such as
\[
\frac{x^2}{y}.
\]

This example shows that \(\Proj\) is sensitive to the grading, not only to the underlying ring.

\subsection{Example: Same Ring, Different Grading}

Let
\[
S=k[x,y].
\]

With the standard grading
\[
\deg x=\deg y=1,
\]
we get
\[
\Proj S=\PP_k^1.
\]

But with the grading
\[
\deg x=1,
\qquad
\deg y=2,
\]
we get the weighted projective scheme
\[
\Proj S=\PP(1,2).
\]

Therefore the symbol
\[
\Proj S
\]
depends not only on the ring \(S\), but also on its grading.

This is very important:
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj \text{ is a construction for graded rings, not merely for rings.}
}
\]

\subsection{Example: The Empty Open \texorpdfstring{$D_+(0)$}{D+(0)}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1].
\]

Take
\[
f=0.
\]

Then
\[
D_+(0)
=
\{\mathfrak p\in \Proj S:0\notin\mathfrak p\}.
\]

But every prime ideal contains \(0\). Hence
\[
D_+(0)=\varnothing.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(0)=\varnothing.
}
\]

This is the projective analogue of the affine fact
\[
D(0)=\varnothing.
\]

\subsection{Example: The Whole Space \texorpdfstring{$D_+(1)$}{D+(1)}}

Since
\[
1\in S_0,
\]
we have
\[
D_+(1)
=
\{\mathfrak p\in\Proj S:1\notin\mathfrak p\}.
\]

No proper prime ideal contains \(1\), so
\[
D_+(1)=\Proj S.
\]

However, \(1\) has degree zero, so \(D_+(1)\) is usually not one of the positive-degree basic opens used to cover \(\Proj S\).

The important affine charts are usually
\[
D_+(f),
\]
where
\[
f\in S_+
\]
is homogeneous.

\subsection{Example: Intersection of Charts in \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^2$}{P2}}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2].
\]

Consider
\[
D_+(x_0)\cap D_+(x_1).
\]

By the general formula,
\[
D_+(x_0)\cap D_+(x_1)
=
D_+(x_0x_1).
\]

On the chart \(D_+(x_0)\), use coordinates
\[
u=\frac{x_1}{x_0},
\qquad
v=\frac{x_2}{x_0}.
\]

The condition
\[
x_1\neq 0
\]
becomes
\[
u\neq 0.
\]

Therefore
\[
D_+(x_0)\cap D_+(x_1)
\]
corresponds to
\[
\Spec k[u,v]_u.
\]

On the chart \(D_+(x_1)\), use coordinates
\[
u'=\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\qquad
v'=\frac{x_2}{x_1}.
\]

The transition functions are
\[
u'=\frac{1}{u},
\]
and
\[
v'
=
\frac{x_2/x_0}{x_1/x_0}
=
\frac{v}{u}.
\]

So the gluing rule is
\[
\boxed{
	u'=u^{-1},
	\qquad
	v'=\frac{v}{u}.
}
\]

This is the usual coordinate transition between affine charts of projective space.

\subsection{Recommended Example Set for Lecture Notes}

For a clean lecture-note progression, the best examples are:

\[
\begin{array}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5cm}
		|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8cm}}
	\toprule
	\textbf{Example} & \textbf{Main point} \\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x]\)
	&
	Gives \(\PP_k^0\), a single point.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x_0,x_1]\)
	&
	Gives \(\PP_k^1\), two affine charts.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x_0,x_1,x_2]\)
	&
	Gives \(\PP_k^2\), three affine charts.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(V_+(x_2)\subseteq \PP_k^2\)
	&
	A projective line as a closed subscheme.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x_0,x_1,x_2]/(x_0x_2-x_1^2)\)
	&
	A projective conic.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x_0,x_1]/(x_1^2)\)
	&
	A non-reduced projective scheme.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k\)
	&
	The empty scheme.
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Proj k[x,y]\) with \(\deg x=1,\deg y=2\)
	&
	A weighted projective example.
	\\
	\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]

The most important computational example is:
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(x_i)
	\cong
	\Spec k\left[
	\frac{x_0}{x_i},
	\dots,
	\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
	\dots,
	\frac{x_n}{x_i}
	\right].
}
\]

The most important projective closed-subscheme example is:
\[
\boxed{
	\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I.
}
\]

Its closed points are
\[
[a_0:\cdots:a_n]
\]
satisfying
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\]
for every homogeneous polynomial
\[
F\in I.
\]

\subsection{Final Summary}

The examples above show the main uses of the \(\Proj\) construction.

First,
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
=
\PP_k^n.
\]

Second, each standard affine chart is
\[
D_+(x_i)
\cong
\AA_k^n.
\]

Third, homogeneous ideals define projective closed subschemes:
\[
I\subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\subseteq
\PP_k^n.
\]

Fourth, the grading matters. The same underlying ring with different gradings can give different projective schemes.

Finally, projective functions are locally represented by degree-zero homogeneous fractions:
\[
\frac{F}{G},
\qquad
\deg F=\deg G.
\]


\section{Charts, Diagrams, and Tables for the Construction of \texorpdfstring{$\Proj S$}{Proj S}}

This section collects visual and tabular summaries of the construction of
\[
\Proj S.
\]
The goal is to make the main ideas easy to remember:

\[
\Proj S
=
\left\{
\text{homogeneous prime ideals of }S
\text{ not containing }S_+
\right\},
\]
where
\[
S_+=\bigoplus_{d\ge 1}S_d
\]
is the irrelevant ideal.

The central local formula is
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(f)\cong \Spec S_{(f)}
}
\]
where
\[
S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0.
\]

Thus \(\Proj S\) is studied by covering it with affine opens \(D_+(f)\).

\subsection{Main Construction Diagram}

The construction begins with a graded ring and ends with a scheme covered by affine charts.

\[

	node distance=1.2cm,

	},

	]

	\node[box, below=of irr] (primes) {homogeneous prime ideals \(\mathfrak p\subset S\)};
	\node[box, below=of primes] (condition) {keep only those satisfying \(S_+\not\subseteq \mathfrak p\)};
	\node[box, below=of condition] (proj) {\(\Proj S\)};

The most important step is
\[
D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}.
\]
This is what makes \(\Proj S\) locally affine.

\subsection{Affine Versus Projective Dictionary}

The construction of \(\Proj S\) is parallel to the construction of \(\Spec A\).

\[
\begin{array}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{6cm}
		|>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7cm}}
	\toprule
	\textbf{Affine case} & \textbf{Projective case} \\
	\midrule
	\(A\) is a ring
	&
	\(S=\bigoplus_{d\ge 0}S_d\) is a graded ring
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\Spec A\)
	&
	\(\Proj S\)
	\\
	\midrule
	prime ideals
	&
	homogeneous prime ideals
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\mathfrak p\subset A\)
	&
	\(\mathfrak p\subset S\), with \(S_+\not\subseteq\mathfrak p\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(V(I)\)
	&
	\(V_+(I)\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D(f)\)
	&
	\(D_+(f)\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(A_f\)
	&
	\(S_{(f)}=(S_f)_0\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D(f)\cong\Spec A_f\)
	&
	\(D_+(f)\cong\Spec S_{(f)}\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(A_{\mathfrak p}\)
	&
	\(S_{(\mathfrak p)}\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\mathcal O_{\Spec A,\mathfrak p}\cong A_{\mathfrak p}\)
	&
	\(\mathcal O_{\Proj S,\mathfrak p}\cong S_{(\mathfrak p)}\)
	\\
	\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]

The main difference is that in the projective case we only use homogeneous information and degree-zero fractions.

\subsection{Chart Table for Projective Space}

Let
\[
S=k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
with the standard grading. Then
\[
\Proj S=\PP_k^n.
\]

The standard affine charts are
\[
D_+(x_i)=\{\mathfrak p\in\Proj S:x_i\notin\mathfrak p\}.
\]

\[
\begin{array}{>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{3cm}
		|>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{3cm}
		|>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{7cm}}
	\toprule
	\textbf{Chart} & \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Coordinate ring} \\
	\midrule
	\(D_+(x_0)\)
	&
	\(x_0\neq 0\)
	&
	\[
	k\left[
	\frac{x_1}{x_0},\dots,\frac{x_n}{x_0}
	\right]
	\]
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D_+(x_1)\)
	&
	\(x_1\neq 0\)
	&
	\[
	k\left[
	\frac{x_0}{x_1},
	\frac{x_2}{x_1},
	\dots,
	\frac{x_n}{x_1}
	\right]
	\]
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\vdots\)
	&
	\(\vdots\)
	&
	\(\vdots\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D_+(x_i)\)
	&
	\(x_i\neq 0\)
	&
	\[
	k\left[
	\frac{x_0}{x_i},
	\dots,
	\widehat{\frac{x_i}{x_i}},
	\dots,
	\frac{x_n}{x_i}
	\right]
	\]
	\\
	\midrule
	\(\vdots\)
	&
	\(\vdots\)
	&
	\(\vdots\)
	\\
	\midrule
	\(D_+(x_n)\)
	&
	\(x_n\neq 0\)
	&
	\[
	k\left[
	\frac{x_0}{x_n},\dots,\frac{x_{n-1}}{x_n}
	\right]
	\]
	\\
	\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	D_+(x_i)\cong \AA_k^n
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
	\PP_k^n
	=
	D_+(x_0)\cup D_+(x_1)\cup\cdots\cup D_+(x_n).
}
\]

\subsection{Gluing Diagram for \texorpdfstring{$\PP_k^1$}{P1}}

For
\[
\PP_k^1=\Proj k[x_0,x_1],
\]
there are two standard charts:
\[
D_+(x_0)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
D_+(x_1).
\]

On \(D_+(x_0)\), set
\[
t=\frac{x_1}{x_0}.
\]
Then
\[
D_+(x_0)\cong\Spec k[t].
\]

On \(D_+(x_1)\), set
\[
u=\frac{x_0}{x_1}.
\]
Then
\[
D_+(x_1)\cong\Spec k[u].
\]

On the overlap,
\[
u=t^{-1}.
\]

\[

	node distance=1.6cm and 2.2cm,

\[
\boxed{
	\PP_k^1

	\AA_k^1\cup\{\infty\}.
}
\]

\subsection{Diagram for Projective Closed Subschemes}

Let
\[
I\subseteq k[x_0,\dots,x_n]
\]
be a homogeneous ideal.

Then
\[
\Proj k[x_0,\dots,x_n]/I
\]
is a closed subscheme of
\[
\PP_k^n.
\]

\[

\]

\subsection{Homogeneous Versus Non-Homogeneous Equations}

Projective equations must be homogeneous.

\[
\begin{array}{>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{5cm}
		|>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{3cm}
		|>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{5cm}}
	\toprule
	\textbf{Equation} & \textbf{Homogeneous?} & \textbf{Defines projective subset?} \\
	\midrule
	\(x_0+x_1=0\)
	&
	yes
	&
	yes
	\\
	\midrule
	\(x_0^2+x_1x_2=0\)
	&
	yes
	&
	yes
	\\
	\midrule
	\(x_0x_2-x_1^2=0\)
	&
	yes
	&
	yes
	\\
	\midrule
	\(x_0+1=0\)
	&
	no
	&
	no
	\\
	\midrule
	\(x_0^2+x_1+1=0\)
	&
	no
	&
	no
	\\
	\midrule
	\(x_0^3+x_1^3+x_2^3=0\)
	&
	yes
	&
	yes
	\\
	\bottomrule
\end{array}
\]

The reason is that if \(F\) is homogeneous of degree \(d\), then
\[
F(\lambda a_0,\dots,\lambda a_n)
=
\lambda^dF(a_0,\dots,a_n).
\]

Therefore
\[
F(a_0,\dots,a_n)=0
\]
is invariant under scaling.

\subsection{Diagram for Projective Closure of an Affine Curve}

Start with the affine parabola
\[
y=x^2
\]
inside
\[
\AA_k^2.
\]

Homogenize the equation:
\[
y-x^2=0
\]
to get
\[
YZ-X^2=0
\]
in projective coordinates \([X:Y:Z]\).

\[

	node distance=1.2cm,

	\node[box, below=of substitution] (fraction) {\(\dfrac{Y}{Z}=\dfrac{X^2}{Z^2}\)};
	\node[box, below=of fraction] (homogeneous) {\(YZ=X^2\)};

\[

\]
Then
\[

becomes

\]
Hence
\[
X=0.
\]

Therefore the added point is
\[
[0:1:0].
\]
