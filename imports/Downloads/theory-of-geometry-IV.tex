\subsection*{Key definitions}

\section*{The groups $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$}

In this section we collect some basic and slightly deeper facts about the
projective special linear groups $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ and
$\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$, viewed both algebraically and geometrically.

\subsection*{1. Definitions and basic structure}

Let
\[
\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)
= \left\{
A\in M_{2\times 2}(\Bbbk) : \det A = 1
\right\},
\qquad
\Bbbk\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}.
\]
This is a Lie group (a smooth manifold with a compatible group structure).
Its centre is
\[
Z(\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk))
= \{\pm I\}
= \left\{
\begin{pmatrix}
	1 & 0\\ 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}
	-1 & 0\\ 0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
\]
The projective special linear group is the quotient
\[
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\Bbbk)
:= \mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)/\{\pm I\}.
\]
An element of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\Bbbk)$ is thus an equivalence class
$[A] = \{\pm A\}$ with $A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)$; the group law is
$[A]\cdot[B] = [AB]$.

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is a connected complex Lie group of
	complex dimension $3$ (real dimension $6$).
	\item $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is a connected real Lie group of real
	dimension $3$.
	\item The covering map
	$\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,\Bbbk)$ is a double covering
	and a local isomorphism of Lie groups.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{2. Projective lines and the Möbius action}

The projective line over $\Bbbk$ is the set of $1$–dimensional subspaces of
$\Bbbk^{2}$:
\[
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Bbbk)
= \{ \Bbbk\cdot (z_{0},z_{1}) : (z_{0},z_{1})\neq (0,0)\}.
\]
Each nonzero vector $(z_{0},z_{1})$ represents a point $[z_{0}:z_{1}]$ in
$\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Bbbk)$, and
\[
[z_{0}:z_{1}] = [\lambda z_{0}:\lambda z_{1}],\qquad \lambda\in\Bbbk^\times.
\]

The group $\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)$ acts linearly on $\Bbbk^2$, and this action
descends to a projective action on $\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Bbbk)$:
\[
A\cdot[z_{0}:z_{1}] := [A(z_{0},z_{1})].
\]
If $A$ is replaced by $-A$, the image subspace is the same, so the action
factors through the quotient and we obtain a faithful action
\[
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\Bbbk)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Diff}\bigl(\mathbb{P}^{1}(\Bbbk)\bigr).
\]

\paragraph{Complex case.}
For $\Bbbk=\mathbb{C}$ we can identify
\[
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})
\cong \widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
\]
by sending $[z:1]\mapsto z$ and $[1:0]\mapsto\infty$.  If
\[
A =
\begin{pmatrix}
	a & b\\
	c & d
\end{pmatrix},
\]
then
\[
A\cdot[z:1] = [az+b:cz+d]
\]
corresponds exactly to the Möbius transformation
\[
f_A(z) = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}.
\]
Thus we have an isomorphism of groups
\[
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
\;\cong\;
\{\text{Möbius transformations on }\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\}.
\]

\paragraph{Real case.}
For $\Bbbk=\mathbb{R}$ we obtain the real projective line
$\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R})$.  The map
\[
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\} \longrightarrow \mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R}),
\qquad
x\mapsto [x:1],\quad \infty\mapsto[1:0],
\]
identifies $\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R})$ with the extended real line
$\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$.  The action of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is
given again by real Möbius transformations
\[
z\longmapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d},
\qquad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R},\quad ad-bc=1.
\]

\subsection*{3. Geometric meaning of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$}

The Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ can be identified with the unit
sphere $S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$ via stereographic projection.  Equipped
with its standard complex structure, $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is denoted
$\mathbb{CP}^{1}$.

\begin{itemize}
	\item Every Möbius transformation $f_A$ is a biholomorphism of
	$\mathbb{CP}^{1}$.
	\item Conversely, every biholomorphic self-map of $\mathbb{CP}^{1}$ is a
	Möbius transformation.
\end{itemize}

Thus
\[
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
\cong \mathrm{Aut}_{\mathrm{hol}}(\mathbb{CP}^{1}),
\]
the group of holomorphic automorphisms of the Riemann sphere.

If we equip $S^{2}$ with the standard round metric (of constant Gauss
curvature $K=1$), then every Möbius transformation is, up to a conformal
factor, an angle-preserving map of $S^{2}$.  The subgroup of Möbius
transformations that actually preserve the round metric (i.e.\ are genuine
isometries of $S^{2}$) is the compact real form
\[
\mathrm{PSU}(2) \subset \mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}),
\]
which is isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.

A deeper fact from hyperbolic geometry is that:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is isomorphic to the group of
	orientation–preserving isometries of hyperbolic $3$–space
	$\mathbb{H}^{3}$ (in the upper half--space or ball model), acting
	by conformal transformations on the sphere at infinity
	$\partial\mathbb{H}^{3} \cong S^{2} \cong \widehat{\mathbb{C}}$.
\end{itemize}

In this sense, $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ simultaneously encodes the
complex-analytic symmetries of the Riemann sphere and the metric symmetries
of hyperbolic $3$--space.

\subsection*{4. Geometric meaning of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$}

Consider the upper half–plane
\[
\mathbb{H}
= \{ z\in\mathbb{C} : \Im z > 0\}
\]
equipped with the Poincaré metric
\[
ds^{2} = \frac{dx^{2} + dy^{2}}{y^{2}},
\qquad z=x+iy.
\]
This is a complete Riemannian metric of constant negative curvature
$K=-1$, giving $\mathbb{H}$ the structure of the hyperbolic plane.

The group $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ acts on $\mathbb{H}$ by real Möbius
transformations:
\[
[A]\cdot z = \frac{az+b}{cz+d},
\qquad
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
	a & b\\ c & d
\end{pmatrix}
\in \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}).
\]
One verifies directly that this action preserves the Poincaré metric and
orientation, and any orientation–preserving isometry of $(\mathbb{H},ds^{2})$
arises in this way.  Thus
\[
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
\cong \mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{H}),
\]
the full group of orientation–preserving isometries of the hyperbolic plane.

The boundary of $\mathbb{H}$ in the conformal compactification
$\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}$ is the extended real line
$\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, and
$\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ acts on this boundary by real Möbius
transformations as well.

\subsection*{5. Classification of elements via the trace}

Let $A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ and denote by $[A]$ its class in
$\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$.  The trace $\mathrm{tr}(A)$ is invariant under
conjugation, and for real matrices also under $A\mapsto -A$, so it gives an
invariant of conjugacy classes in $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$.

For $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ one has the classical classification:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Elliptic:}
	$|\mathrm{tr}(A)|<2$.  The corresponding Möbius transformation has two
	complex conjugate fixed points and acts as a rotation in hyperbolic
	geometry.
	\item \textbf{Parabolic:}
	$|\mathrm{tr}(A)|=2$.  There is exactly one fixed point on the boundary
	of $\mathbb{H}$, and the transformation acts as a parabolic isometry
	(e.g.\ a translation).
	\item \textbf{Hyperbolic:}
	$|\mathrm{tr}(A)|>2$.  There are two distinct fixed points on the
	boundary, and the transformation acts as a hyperbolic translation along
	a geodesic in $\mathbb{H}$.
\end{itemize}

In $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ one has a similar classification into
elliptic, parabolic and loxodromic elements, now using the complex trace.
The fixed point picture generalizes to the action on the Riemann sphere and
on hyperbolic $3$--space.

\subsection*{6. Discrete subgroups: Fuchsian and Kleinian groups}

Discrete subgroups of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ and
$\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ play a central role in geometry.

\begin{itemize}
	\item A \emph{Fuchsian group} is a discrete subgroup
	$\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$.  The quotient
	$\mathbb{H}/\Gamma$ carries a natural hyperbolic metric and often
	has the structure of a Riemann surface of finite type.
	\item A \emph{Kleinian group} is a discrete subgroup
	$\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$.  Such a group acts on
	$\mathbb{H}^{3}$ by hyperbolic isometries and on the Riemann sphere
	by Möbius transformations; the quotient geometry
	$\mathbb{H}^{3}/\Gamma$ and the induced action on the limit set on
	the sphere at infinity are fundamental objects in $3$--dimensional
	hyperbolic geometry and complex dynamics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{7. Summary}

\begin{itemize}
	\item Algebraically, $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\Bbbk)$ is the quotient of
	$\mathrm{SL}(2,\Bbbk)$ by its centre $\{\pm I\}$.
	\item Geometrically, $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the group of Möbius
	transformations of the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ and
	the group of orientation–preserving isometries of hyperbolic
	$3$--space $\mathbb{H}^{3}$.
	\item $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ is the group of real Möbius
	transformations of the extended real line and the group of
	orientation–preserving isometries of the hyperbolic plane
	$(\mathbb{H},ds^{2})$.
	\item The trace of a matrix in $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ (or
	$\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$) controls the qualitative dynamical
	behaviour of the corresponding Möbius transformation (elliptic,
	parabolic, hyperbolic/loxodromic).
\end{itemize}

These groups form the basic symmetry groups in low–dimensional geometry:
they sit at the intersection of complex analysis, hyperbolic geometry,
Riemann surface theory, and geometric group theory.

\subsection*{Examples for the Poincaré metric on the upper half--plane}

We work on the upper half--plane
\[
\mathbb H = \{ z=x+iy \in \mathbb C : y>0\}
\]
equipped with the Poincaré metric
\[
ds^{2} = \frac{dx^{2} + dy^{2}}{y^{2}},
\qquad z = x + i y.
\]

\subsubsection*{1. Distances along a vertical geodesic}

Consider two points lying on the same vertical line,
\[
z_{1} = x_{0} + i y_{1}, \qquad
z_{2} = x_{0} + i y_{2}, \qquad
0 < y_{1} < y_{2}.
\]
Parametrise the vertical segment between them by
\[
\gamma(t) = x_{0} + i t, \qquad t\in[y_{1},y_{2}].
\]
Along this curve we have
\[
dx = 0, \qquad dy = dt, \qquad y = t,
\]
so the metric becomes
\[
ds^{2} = \frac{0 + dt^{2}}{t^{2}} = \frac{dt^{2}}{t^{2}},
\qquad ds = \frac{dt}{t}.
\]
The hyperbolic length of the vertical segment is therefore
\[
L(\gamma)
= \int_{y_{1}}^{y_{2}} \frac{dt}{t}
= \log\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}}.
\]
Vertical lines are geodesics for the Poincaré metric, so this gives the
hyperbolic distance between $z_{1}$ and $z_{2}$:
\[
d_{\mathbb H}(z_{1},z_{2}) = \log\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}}.
\]

\paragraph{Example A: from $i$ to $2i$.}
Here $x_{0}=0$, $y_{1}=1$, $y_{2}=2$, hence
\[
d_{\mathbb H}(i,2i) = \log\frac{2}{1} = \log 2.
\]

\paragraph{Example B: from $1+i$ to $1+3i$.}
Here $x_{0}=1$, $y_{1}=1$, $y_{2}=3$, so
\[
d_{\mathbb H}(1+i,1+3i) = \log\frac{3}{1} = \log 3.
\]

\subsubsection*{2. Length of a horizontal segment}

Consider the horizontal segment from $z_{1} = 0 + i$ to $z_{2} = 1 + i$.
Parametrise it by
\[
\gamma(t) = t + i,\qquad t\in[0,1].
\]
Then
\[
dx = dt,\qquad dy = 0,\qquad y = 1,
\]
so along $\gamma$ the metric reduces to
\[
ds^{2} = \frac{dt^{2} + 0}{1^{2}} = dt^{2},
\qquad ds = dt.
\]
Thus the hyperbolic length of this horizontal segment is
\[
L(\gamma) = \int_{0}^{1} dt = 1.
\]
This curve is not a geodesic (geodesics are vertical lines or semicircles
orthogonal to the real axis), but it illustrates directly how to compute
lengths from the metric.

\subsubsection*{3. General distance formula between two points}

There is a useful closed formula for the hyperbolic distance between two
points $z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb H$:
\[
d_{\mathbb H}(z_{1},z_{2})
= \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(
1 + \frac{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}{2\,\Im z_{1}\,\Im z_{2}}
\right),
\]
where $\Im z$ denotes the imaginary part of $z$ and
$\operatorname{arcosh}$ is the inverse hyperbolic cosine.

\paragraph{Example C: distance between $i$ and $1+i$.}
Take
\[
z_{1} = i, \qquad z_{2} = 1+i.
\]
Then
\[
\Im z_{1} = \Im z_{2} = 1,
\qquad
|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}
= |\,i - (1+i)\,|^{2}
= |-1|^{2}
= 1.
\]
Hence
\[
d_{\mathbb H}(i,1+i)
= \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(
1 + \frac{1}{2\cdot 1\cdot 1}
\right)
= \operatorname{arcosh}\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right).
\]

These examples illustrate how the Poincaré metric is used in practice:
one parametrises a curve, substitutes $dx$ and $dy$ into
$ds^{2}=(dx^{2}+dy^{2})/y^{2}$, takes $ds$, and integrates to obtain
hyperbolic lengths and distances.

\subsection*{Why we cannot use $z_{2}-z_{1}$ directly in the Poincaré metric}

Recall that on the upper half--plane
\[
\mathbb H = \{ z = x+iy \in \mathbb C : y>0\}
\]
the Poincaré metric is
\[
ds^{2} = \frac{dx^{2} + dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.
\]
Here $dx$ and $dy$ are \emph{infinitesimal} changes along a curve, not
finite differences between two fixed points.

\subsubsection*{1. Metric as a local length formula}

Let $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb H$ be a smooth curve,
\[
\gamma(t) = (x(t),y(t)),\qquad y(t)>0.
\]
Along $\gamma$ one has
\[
dx = x'(t)\,dt,
\qquad
dy = y'(t)\,dt,
\]
so the metric gives
\[
ds^{2}
= \frac{x'(t)^{2} + y'(t)^{2}}{y(t)^{2}}\,dt^{2},
\qquad
ds = \frac{\sqrt{x'(t)^{2} + y'(t)^{2}}}{y(t)}\,dt.
\]
Hence the hyperbolic length of $\gamma$ is
\[
L(\gamma)
= \int_{a}^{b} \frac{\sqrt{x'(t)^{2} + y'(t)^{2}}}{y(t)}\,dt.
\]
This shows that $dx,dy$ in the metric are \emph{differentials along the
	curve}, i.e.\ they depend on the choice of parametrisation
$\gamma(t)$ and on the point $t$.

\subsubsection*{2. Why finite differences $z_{2}-z_{1}$ do not belong in $ds^{2}$}

Given two points $z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb H$, we can form the finite
differences
\[
\Delta x = \Re(z_{2}-z_{1}),
\qquad
\Delta y = \Im(z_{2}-z_{1}),
\]
but these are \emph{global} quantities: they only record the overall
horizontal and vertical displacement between the endpoints.

Different curves $\gamma$ from $z_{1}$ to $z_{2}$ can have the same
endpoints and hence the same $(\Delta x,\Delta y)$, but very different
functions $x(t),y(t)$ and derivatives $x'(t),y'(t)$, so their lengths
\[
L(\gamma) = \int \frac{\sqrt{x'(t)^{2}+y'(t)^{2}}}{y(t)}\,dt
\]
may be completely different.  The Poincaré metric is position--dependent
because of the factor $1/y^{2}$; what matters is the value of $y(t)$
\emph{along the curve}, not merely at the endpoints.

If one naively tried to ``plug in'' the finite differences into the metric,
for example
\[
ds^{2} \stackrel{\text{wrong}}{\approx}
\frac{(\Delta x)^{2} + (\Delta y)^{2}}{y^{2}},
\]
one would implicitly be treating the entire path as a single infinitesimal
step taken at some fixed height $y$, which is not what the Riemannian
metric means and would ignore the curvature encoded in the $1/y^{2}$ factor.

\subsubsection*{3. A concrete example}

Consider the vertical segment from $z_{1}=i$ to $z_{2}=2i$.

\paragraph{Correct computation.}
Parametrise
\[
\gamma(t) = it, \qquad t\in[1,2].
\]
Then $x(t)=0$, $y(t)=t$, $x'(t)=0$, $y'(t)=1$, so
\[
ds = \frac{1}{t}\,dt, \qquad
L(\gamma) = \int_{1}^{2} \frac{1}{t}\,dt = \log 2.
\]

\paragraph{Incorrect ``finite difference'' intuition.}
The finite differences are
\[
\Delta x = 0,\qquad \Delta y = 1.
\]
If one tried to interpret $dx=\Delta x$, $dy=\Delta y$ and plug some
``average height'' $y_{\text{avg}}$ into the metric, one might write
\[
L \stackrel{\text{wrong}}{\approx}
\frac{\sqrt{0^{2}+1^{2}}}{y_{\text{avg}}}
= \frac{1}{y_{\text{avg}}},
\]
which depends arbitrarily on the choice of $y_{\text{avg}}$ and certainly
does not give $\log 2$.  This illustrates that finite endpoint differences
cannot replace the integral of $ds$ along the curve.

\subsubsection*{4. When endpoint differences do work (flat case)}

In Euclidean space with the flat metric
\[
ds^{2} = dx^{2} + dy^{2},
\]
the coefficients of the metric are constant, so the length of a straight
segment depends only on the endpoints:
\[
L = \sqrt{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2} + (y_{2}-y_{1})^{2}}.
\]
Here one \emph{can} express the distance using only finite differences,
because integrating a constant metric along the straight segment yields
exactly this formula.

In the Poincaré metric, however, the coefficients depend on $y$, and
therefore the length of a curve depends on how it moves through regions of
different height.  This is why, given two points $z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb H$,
we compute distances by integrating along a path (typically a geodesic), or
by using a closed formula derived from such an integral, rather than by
substituting $z_{2}-z_{1}$ directly into $ds^{2}$.

\subsection*{E3. The product torus $S^{1}\times S^{1}$ and its geodesics}

\subsubsection*{Problem statement}

The first fundamental form makes sense for a surface $\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$
for any $n$.  Let $S^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}$ be the unit circle and let
\[
T := S^{1}\times S^{1} \subset \mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^{4}
\]
be the \emph{product torus}.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Show that the induced Riemannian metric on $T$ is locally Euclidean
	and hence flat.
	\item Show that through any point $p\in T$ there are infinitely many closed
	geodesics and also infinitely many non--closed geodesics (defined for
	all $t\in\mathbb{R}$).  (A closed geodesic is a geodesic
	$\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to T$ which is periodic, i.e.\ for some $L>0$,
	$\sigma(t+L)=\sigma(t)$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$.)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Theory: first fundamental form in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

Let $\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ be a smooth surface and let
$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denote the Euclidean inner product on
$\mathbb{R}^{n}$.  For $p\in\Sigma$, the tangent space $T_{p}\Sigma$ is a
$2$--dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, and the \emph{first fundamental
	form} at $p$ is the bilinear form
\[
g_{p}(u,v) := \langle u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{n}},
\qquad u,v\in T_{p}\Sigma.
\]
This defines a Riemannian metric $g$ on $\Sigma$, obtained simply by
restricting the ambient Euclidean inner product.

In local coordinates, if $\sigma:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\Sigma$ is a
parametrisation, then the coordinate vector fields
$\sigma_{u},\sigma_{v} \in T_{\sigma(u,v)}\Sigma$ satisfy
\[
E = \sigma_{u}\cdot\sigma_{u},\qquad
F = \sigma_{u}\cdot\sigma_{v},\qquad
G = \sigma_{v}\cdot\sigma_{v},
\]
and the metric is written as
\[
I = E\,du^{2} + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^{2}.
\]
This is completely analogous to the case $n=3$; the only difference is that
the dot product is taken in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ rather than $\mathbb{R}^{3}$.

\subsubsection*{Solution (1): the induced metric on the product torus}

Identify $S^{1}$ with the unit circle in $\mathbb{C}$ via
\[
e^{i\theta} = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.
\]
Define
\[
F : \mathbb{R}^{2} \longrightarrow T = S^{1}\times S^{1},\qquad
F(\theta,\phi) = \bigl(e^{i\theta},e^{i\phi}\bigr)
= (\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos\phi,\sin\phi)
\in \mathbb{R}^{4}.
\]
The map $F$ is $2\pi$--periodic in each variable:
\[
F(\theta+2\pi,\phi) = F(\theta,\phi),
\qquad
F(\theta,\phi+2\pi) = F(\theta,\phi),
\]
so it descends to a smooth map
\[
\bar F : \mathbb{R}^{2} / (2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2} \longrightarrow T,
\]
which is in fact a diffeomorphism.  Thus we may regard $T$ as the quotient
torus $\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ with the metric induced by $F$.

Compute the partial derivatives:
\[
F_{\theta}
= \frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}
= (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0,0),
\]
\[
F_{\phi}
= \frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi}
= (0,0,-\sin\phi,\cos\phi).
\]
Their Euclidean dot products in $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ are
\[
E := F_{\theta}\cdot F_{\theta}
= \sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1,
\]
\[
G := F_{\phi}\cdot F_{\phi}
= \sin^{2}\phi + \cos^{2}\phi = 1,
\]
\[
F := F_{\theta}\cdot F_{\phi}
= 0.
\]
Therefore, in the coordinates $(\theta,\phi)$, the first fundamental form
on $T$ is
\[
I = d\theta^{2} + d\phi^{2}.
\]

This is exactly the standard Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, and it
does not depend on the point $(\theta,\phi)$.  Hence $F$ is a local
isometry from $(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\theta^{2}+d\phi^{2})$ onto $(T,g)$, and the
Riemannian surface $(T,g)$ is \emph{flat} (its Gauss curvature is zero).

\subsubsection*{Solution (2): geodesics via straight lines in the covering plane}

Geodesics on $(\mathbb{R}^{2},d\theta^{2}+d\phi^{2})$ are precisely straight
lines.  Thus any geodesic in the covering space has the form
\[
\gamma(t) = (\theta(t),\phi(t))
= (\theta_{0},\phi_{0}) + t\,(a,b),
\]
where $(\theta_{0},\phi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and $(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$,
$(a,b)\neq (0,0)$, are constants.

Because $F$ is a local isometry and factors through the quotient by the
lattice $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$, the geodesics on $T$ are exactly the
projections of such straight lines:
\[
\sigma(t)
= \bar F\bigl(\theta_{0}+ta,\ \phi_{0}+tb\bigr)
\in T,
\]
where $\bar F:\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}\to T$ is the induced
diffeomorphism.

\subsubsection*{Closed vs non--closed geodesics}

Fix a point $p\in T$ and choose a lift $(\theta_{0},\phi_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$
with $\bar F(\theta_{0},\phi_{0})=p$.  For each nonzero direction
$(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ we obtain a geodesic
\[
\sigma_{a,b}(t) = \bar F\bigl(\theta_{0}+ta,\ \phi_{0}+tb\bigr).
\]

The geodesic $\sigma_{a,b}$ is \emph{closed} if and only if there exists
$T>0$ such that
\[
(\theta_{0}+Ta,\ \phi_{0}+Tb)
\equiv
(\theta_{0},\phi_{0})
\pmod{(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}},
\]
that is,
\[
Ta = 2\pi m,\qquad
Tb = 2\pi n
\]
for some integers $m,n\in\mathbb{Z}$.  This is possible exactly when the
slope $a/b$ is rational (understanding separately the case $b=0$, which
corresponds to a purely ``horizontal'' closed circle).  Thus:

\begin{itemize}
	\item If $\dfrac{a}{b}\in\mathbb{Q}$, then $\sigma_{a,b}$ is a closed
	geodesic.  There are infinitely many rational slopes, so there are
	infinitely many closed geodesics through $p$.
	\item If $\dfrac{a}{b}\notin\mathbb{Q}$, then there is no nonzero $T$
	with $(Ta,Tb)\in (2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$, so $\sigma_{a,b}$ is not
	periodic: it is a non--closed geodesic defined for all
	$t\in\mathbb{R}$ (indeed, its image is dense in $T$).  There are
	infinitely many irrational slopes, hence infinitely many such
	non--closed geodesics through $p$.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, for each point $p\in S^{1}\times S^{1}$ the flat product torus
contains infinitely many closed geodesics and infinitely many non--closed
geodesics (defined on the whole of $\mathbb{R}$) passing through $p$.


\subsection*{Understanding the quotient $\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$}

\subsubsection*{1. Warm--up: the quotient $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$}

Consider the real line $\mathbb{R}$ and the subgroup
\[
2\pi\mathbb{Z} = \{ 2\pi k : k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]
Define an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{R}$ by
\[
x \sim y
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x-y \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}.
\]
Thus $x$ and $y$ are equivalent exactly when they differ by an integer
multiple of $2\pi$.  The quotient
\[
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
\]
is the set of all equivalence classes
\[
[x] = \{ x + 2\pi k : k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]
Geometrically, we ``wrap'' the line $\mathbb{R}$ around a circle of
circumference $2\pi$: all points whose coordinates differ by $2\pi k$
collapse to a single point.  We can represent $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$
by the interval $[0,2\pi)$ with endpoints glued together.  This space is
canonically diffeomorphic to the unit circle $S^{1}$ via
\[
[\theta] \longmapsto e^{i\theta}.
\]

\subsubsection*{2. The lattice $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$}

Now pass to $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and consider the lattice
\[
(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
= \{ (2\pi m,\,2\pi n) : m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]
This is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.  We define an
equivalence relation on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ by
\[
(\theta,\phi) \sim (\theta',\phi')
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(\theta'-\theta,\ \phi'-\phi) \in (2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2},
\]
that is,
\[
(\theta',\phi') \sim (\theta,\phi)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\theta' = \theta + 2\pi m,\ \ \phi' = \phi + 2\pi n
\]
for some integers $m,n\in\mathbb{Z}$.

The quotient
\[
\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
\]
is then the set of equivalence classes
\[
[(\theta,\phi)]
= \{ (\theta+2\pi m,\ \phi+2\pi n) : m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.
\]

\subsubsection*{3. Geometric picture: from a square to a torus}

Geometrically, the lattice $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ defines a grid of
congruent squares of side length $2\pi$ in the $(\theta,\phi)$--plane.
A convenient \emph{fundamental domain} is the square
\[
Q = [0,2\pi) \times [0,2\pi).
\]
Every equivalence class $[(\theta,\phi)]$ contains exactly one point of
$Q$, so the quotient $\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ can be
represented by the square $Q$ with the following identifications:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the left edge $\{\theta=0\}$ is identified with the right edge
	$\{\theta=2\pi\}$ (points with the same $\phi$ coordinate),
	\item the bottom edge $\{\phi=0\}$ is identified with the top edge
	$\{\phi=2\pi\}$ (points with the same $\theta$ coordinate).
\end{itemize}
Gluing opposite edges of the square in this way produces a torus.  Thus
\[
\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
\]
is precisely the \emph{flat square torus}.

\subsubsection*{4. Identification with $S^{1}\times S^{1}$}

Define
\[
F : \mathbb{R}^{2} \longrightarrow S^{1}\times S^{1},\qquad
F(\theta,\phi) = (e^{i\theta},e^{i\phi}).
\]
Observe that
\[
F(\theta+2\pi,\phi) = F(\theta,\phi),
\qquad
F(\theta,\phi+2\pi) = F(\theta,\phi),
\]
so $F$ is constant on equivalence classes modulo $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$.
Therefore it induces a well--defined smooth map
\[
\bar F : \mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2} \longrightarrow S^{1}\times S^{1},
\qquad
\bar F\bigl([\theta,\phi]\bigr) = (e^{i\theta},e^{i\phi}).
\]
This map is bijective and a diffeomorphism, so we obtain a canonical
identification
\[
\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2} \;\cong\; S^{1}\times S^{1}.
\]

In summary, the quotient $\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$ is the
plane modulo integer translations by $(2\pi,0)$ and $(0,2\pi)$; it is
geometrically represented by a $2\pi\times 2\pi$ square with opposite
edges glued together, and it is naturally diffeomorphic to the product
torus $S^{1}\times S^{1}$.


\subsection*{Flat, locally Euclidean, and Euclidean metrics}

In Riemannian geometry several related but distinct notions appear:
\emph{locally Euclidean}, \emph{flat}, and (globally) \emph{Euclidean}.
We explain the differences and relate them to the product torus
$T = S^{1}\times S^{1}$.

\subsubsection*{1. Locally Euclidean}

A Riemannian metric $g$ on a smooth manifold $M$ is called
\emph{locally Euclidean} if around every point $p\in M$ there exists
a coordinate chart $(U;x^{1},\dots,x^{n})$ with $p\in U$ such that,
in these coordinates,
\[
g_{ij}(p) = \delta_{ij}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}(p) = 0.
\]
In such a chart, infinitesimally near $p$, lengths and angles are the
same as in the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.  These are the so--called
\emph{normal coordinates} at $p$.

Every smooth Riemannian manifold is locally Euclidean in this sense:
around each point one can choose normal coordinates so that $g_{ij}(p)
= \delta_{ij}$ and first derivatives vanish.

In our product torus $T = S^{1}\times S^{1}$, using covering coordinates
$(\theta,\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ with
\[
F(\theta,\phi) = (e^{i\theta},e^{i\phi}),
\]
we found that the induced metric is
\[
g = d\theta^{2} + d\phi^{2}.
\]
Thus in the $(\theta,\phi)$ coordinates the metric is exactly the
standard Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, not only at a point but
on all of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.  In particular, it is locally Euclidean.

\subsubsection*{2. Flat metrics}

A Riemannian metric $g$ on $M$ is called \emph{flat} if its curvature
tensor vanishes identically.  In dimension $2$ this means that the Gauss
curvature $K$ satisfies
\[
K(p) = 0
\quad\text{for all } p\in M,
\]
and in any dimension it means that the Riemann curvature tensor
$R_{ijkl}$ is zero everywhere.

Flatness is a \emph{curvature} condition; it does not directly say
anything about the global topology of $M$.  Important examples include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with the standard Euclidean metric,
	\item quotient spaces such as flat tori
	$\mathbb{R}^{n}/\Lambda$ for a lattice $\Lambda$.
\end{itemize}

In the case of the product torus $T = S^{1}\times S^{1}$, we have
identified $T$ with the quotient
\[
\mathbb{R}^{2}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}
\]
and shown that, in the covering space, the metric is
\[
g = d\theta^{2} + d\phi^{2},
\]
which is the standard flat metric on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.  Passing to the
quotient by a group of isometries preserves the local curvature
properties, so the resulting torus is a \emph{flat} Riemannian manifold
(of dimension $2$, with $K\equiv 0$).

Thus the product torus is both locally Euclidean and flat.

\subsubsection*{3. Euclidean (globally)}

The phrase \emph{Euclidean} is often used in two, related senses:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item As a metric: the \emph{Euclidean metric} on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is
	the standard inner product metric
	\[
	ds^{2} = dx_{1}^{2} + \cdots + dx_{n}^{2}.
	\]
	\item As a global geometry: a Riemannian manifold $(M,g)$ is sometimes
	called \emph{Euclidean} if it is globally isometric to
	$(\mathbb{R}^{n},\text{standard metric})$, i.e.\ if there exists
	a distance--preserving diffeomorphism
	\[
	\Phi : (M,g) \longrightarrow (\mathbb{R}^{n},dx_{1}^{2}+\cdots+dx_{n}^{2}).
	\]
\end{enumerate}

This second meaning is stronger than being flat.  A flat manifold has
zero curvature, but may have nontrivial topology and thus be \emph{not}
isometric to $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

For example:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $(\mathbb{R}^{2},dx^{2}+dy^{2})$ is flat, locally Euclidean, and
	globally Euclidean (it is literally the Euclidean plane).
	\item The product torus $T = S^{1}\times S^{1}$ with metric
	$g = d\theta^{2} + d\phi^{2}$ (modulo $(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{2}$) is
	flat and locally Euclidean, but \emph{not} globally Euclidean,
	because its topology is that of a torus, not of $\mathbb{R}^{2}$.
	It has nontrivial closed loops and nonzero fundamental group
	$\pi_{1}(T)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{4. Summary for the product torus}

For the product torus $T = S^{1}\times S^{1}$ with induced metric from
$\mathbb{R}^{4}$:

\begin{itemize}
	\item In covering coordinates $(\theta,\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ the metric
	is exactly the Euclidean metric $g = d\theta^{2}+d\phi^{2}$,
	hence $T$ is \emph{locally Euclidean}.
	\item The curvature of this metric is zero everywhere, so $T$ is a
	\emph{flat} Riemannian surface.
	\item Nevertheless, $T$ is not globally isometric to $\mathbb{R}^{2}$
	(its topology is different), so it is not globally Euclidean as a
	space, even though its metric is flat.
\end{itemize}

Thus the phrases ``locally Euclidean'', ``flat'', and ``Euclidean'' are
closely related but not synonymous: the product torus is a standard
example of a flat, locally Euclidean manifold that is not globally
Euclidean.

\subsection*{Problem 1: Fixed points and dynamics of a M\"obius map}

Let $T$ be a non–identity M\"obius transformation on the Riemann sphere
$\widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Show that $T$ has exactly one or two fixed points in
	$\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$.
	\item Suppose that $T$ corresponds, under stereographic projection, to a
	rotation of the round sphere $S^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}$.  Show that
	$T$ has two fixed points $z_{1},z_{2}\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ which
	satisfy
	\[
	z_{2} = -\frac{1}{\overline{z_{1}}}.
	\]
	\item Conversely, assume that $T$ has two fixed points $z_{1},z_{2}$ with
	$z_{2} = -1/\overline{z_{1}}$.  Prove that either
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $T$ corresponds to a rotation of $S^{2}$, or
		\item one of the fixed points (say $z_{1}$) is attractive, i.e.
		$T^{n}(z)\to z_{1}$ as $n\to\infty$ for all
		$z\neq z_{2}$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Theory: fixed points of a M\"obius map}

A M\"obius transformation has the form
\[
T(z) = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad-bc\neq 0.
\]
Finite fixed points satisfy $T(z)=z$, i.e.
\[
\frac{az+b}{cz+d} = z
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
az+b = z(cz+d)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
cz^{2} + (d-a)z - b = 0.
\]
Thus finite fixed points are roots of a quadratic polynomial, so there are
either two distinct finite fixed points or one double root.

The point at infinity behaves as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item If $c\neq 0$, then $T(\infty)=a/c\neq\infty$, so $\infty$ is not fixed.
	\item If $c=0$, then $T(z) = \dfrac{az+b}{d}$ and $T(\infty)=\infty$, so
	$\infty$ is a fixed point.  The equation $T(z)=z$ then reduces to
	$(a-d)z + b = 0$, which has exactly one finite solution unless
	$a=d$ and $b=0$, in which case $T$ is the identity.
\end{itemize}

Hence a non–identity M\"obius map can have at most two fixed points in
$\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$.  If it had three distinct fixed points, the
quadratic $cz^{2}+(d-a)z-b$ would have three distinct roots, forcing all its
coefficients to vanish and thus $T$ to be the identity.  Therefore a
non–identity M\"obius transformation has \emph{exactly} one or two fixed
points.

\subsubsection*{Theory: rotations and antipodal points}

Let $\Phi:S^{2}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ denote stereographic projection
from the north pole.  A rotation $R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ acts on $S^{2}$ by
isometries.  Its axis of rotation is a line through the origin, meeting
$S^{2}$ in two antipodal points $p$ and $-p$, which are precisely the fixed
points of $R$ on $S^{2}$.

On the level of the Riemann sphere, the corresponding M\"obius
transformation is
\[
T = \Phi\circ R\circ\Phi^{-1}.
\]
If $z_{1}=\Phi(p)$ and $z_{2}=\Phi(-p)$, then $z_{1},z_{2}$ are the fixed
points of $T$.  A standard computation with the explicit formula for
stereographic projection shows that antipodal points $p,-p$ correspond to
complex numbers related by
\[
z_{2} = -\frac{1}{\overline{z_{1}}}.
\]
Thus a rotation of $S^{2}$ gives a M\"obius map whose fixed points in
$\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ are of the form $z_{1}$ and
$z_{2} = -1/\overline{z_{1}}$.

\subsubsection*{Theory: conjugation and normal form}

Suppose a M\"obius map $T$ has two distinct fixed points $z_{1},z_{2}$.  Then
there exists a M\"obius map $\phi$ such that
\[
\phi(z_{1})=0,\qquad \phi(z_{2})=\infty.
\]
Conjugate $T$ by $\phi$:
\[
S := \phi\circ T\circ\phi^{-1}.
\]
Then $S$ has fixed points $0$ and $\infty$:
\[
S(0)=0,\qquad S(\infty)=\infty.
\]
A M\"obius map fixing $0$ and $\infty$ must be of the form
\[
S(w) = \lambda w,\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times},
\]
since imposing $S(0)=0$ and $S(\infty)=\infty$ on
$\frac{aw+b}{cw+d}$ forces $b=c=0$ and hence $S(w)=\frac{a}{d}w=\lambda w$.

The iterates of $S$ are
\[
S^{n}(w) = \lambda^{n}w.
\]
Therefore:
\begin{itemize}
	\item If $|\lambda|=1$, $S$ is a rotation of the plane and, interpreted on
	$\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$, corresponds to a rotation of the round
	sphere about the vertical axis.
	\item If $|\lambda|<1$, then $S^{n}(w)\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$ for all
	$w\neq\infty$, so $0$ is an \emph{attractive} fixed point and
	$\infty$ is \emph{repulsive}.
	\item If $|\lambda|>1$, then $S^{n}(w)\to\infty$ as $n\to\infty$ for all
	$w\neq 0$, so $\infty$ is attractive and $0$ is repulsive.
\end{itemize}
Conjugating back by $\phi^{-1}$ transfers this dynamical picture to the
original fixed points $z_{1},z_{2}$ of $T$.

\subsubsection*{Solution to (1)}

The argument in the first theory subsection shows that for a non–identity
M\"obius map $T$ the equation $T(z)=z$ is equivalent to a quadratic
equation in $z$, which has one (double) or two distinct roots, possibly
together with $\infty$ in the case $c=0$.  The possibility of three
distinct fixed points leads to a contradiction unless $T$ is the identity.
Hence a non–identity M\"obius transformation has exactly one or two fixed
points in $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$.

\subsubsection*{Solution to (2)}

Let $T$ correspond to a rotation $R$ of $S^{2}$:
\[
T = \Phi\circ R\circ\Phi^{-1}.
\]
Let $p\in S^{2}$ be a point on the rotation axis, and $-p$ its antipodal
point.  Then $R(p)=p$ and $R(-p)=-p$, so the points
\[
z_{1}=\Phi(p),\qquad z_{2}=\Phi(-p)
\]
are fixed by $T$.  Since $p$ and $-p$ are antipodal, the stereographic
projection formulas give
\[
z_{2} = -\frac{1}{\overline{z_{1}}}.
\]
Thus $T$ has two fixed points related by $z_{2}=-1/\overline{z_{1}}$.

\subsubsection*{Solution to (3)}

Now assume conversely that $T$ has two fixed points $z_{1},z_{2}$ satisfying
\[
z_{2} = -\frac{1}{\overline{z_{1}}}.
\]
The relation $z_{2}=-1/\overline{z_{1}}$ means that $(z_{1},z_{2})$ is an
antipodal pair for the round metric on $S^{2}$ (under stereographic
projection).  In particular, one can choose a M\"obius transformation
$\phi$ which maps $z_{1}\mapsto 0$, $z_{2}\mapsto\infty$, and preserves the
unit circle $\{|z|=1\}$ (for instance, $\phi$ can be chosen as a suitable
Blaschke factor).

Define $S = \phi\circ T\circ\phi^{-1}$.  Then $S$ fixes $0$ and $\infty$,
so $S(w)=\lambda w$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}$.  The dynamics
of $T$ are obtained from those of $S$ by conjugation:

\begin{itemize}
	\item If $|\lambda|=1$, then $S$ is a rotation $w\mapsto e^{i\theta}w$,
	whose extension to $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a rotation of the
	round sphere.  Since $\phi$ preserves the unit circle and sends the
	antipodal pair $\{z_{1},z_{2}\}$ to $\{0,\infty\}$, the map $T$ is
	conjugate to a rotation and therefore corresponds to a rotation of
	$S^{2}$.
	\item If $|\lambda|<1$, then $S^{n}(w)\to 0$ for all $w\neq\infty$.  Hence
	$0$ is an attractive fixed point of $S$.  Conjugating back,
	$z_{1}=\phi^{-1}(0)$ is an attractive fixed point of $T$, and
	$z_{2}=\phi^{-1}(\infty)$ is repulsive.  In this case
	$T^{n}(z)\to z_{1}$ as $n\to\infty$ for all $z\neq z_{2}$.
	\item If $|\lambda|>1$, then $S^{n}(w)\to\infty$ for all $w\neq 0$, so
	$\infty$ is attractive and $0$ is repulsive.  Conjugating back,
	$z_{2}$ is attractive and $z_{1}$ is repulsive; after relabelling
	the fixed points, this again yields the statement that one fixed
	point is attractive.
\end{itemize}


Let
\[
T(z) = \frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \qquad ad-bc \neq 0,
\]
be a M\"obius transformation on the extended complex plane
\(\widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\).

\begin{itemize}
	\item If \(c \neq 0\), then
	\[
	T(\infty)
	= \lim_{z\to\infty} \frac{az+b}{cz+d}
	= \frac{a}{c}.
	\]
	In this case the point
	\[
	z_{0} = -\frac{d}{c}
	\]
	satisfies \(cz_{0}+d=0\) and hence
	\[
	T(z_{0}) = \infty.
	\]
	
	\item If \(c = 0\), then
	\[
	T(z) = \frac{az+b}{d}
	\]
	is an affine transformation, and we define
	\[
	T(\infty) = \infty.
	\]
\end{itemize}


Thus, under the assumption $z_{2}=-1/\overline{z_{1}}$, either $T$ is
conjugate to a rotation (when $|\lambda|=1$) and hence corresponds to a
rotation of $S^{2}$, or one of its fixed points is attractive, with
$T^{n}(z)$ converging to that point for all $z$ different from the other
fixed point.

\subsection*{\textbf{Exercise 2. Inversion in the circle $\{|z-a|=r\}$ and Möbius maps}}

\paragraph{\textbf{Problem.}}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Show that inversion in the circle $\{|z-a|=r\}$ is given by
	\[
	z \longmapsto a+\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}.
	\]
	\item Show that every Möbius map is a composition of inversions.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\textbf{Solution.}}

\paragraph{\textbf{(i) Formula for inversion.}}
Let $C=\{\,|z-a|=r\,\}$ and let $z\neq a$. By definition, inversion in $C$ sends $z$ to the unique point $z^{*}$ such that
\[
a,z,z^{*} \text{ are collinear},\qquad |z^{*}-a|\,|z-a|=r^2,
\qquad \arg(z^{*}-a)=\arg(z-a).
\]
Set $w=z-a$. Then $C$ becomes the circle $\{|w|=r\}$ centered at $0$, and inversion must send $w\neq 0$ to $w^{*}$ with
\[
|w^{*}|\,|w|=r^2,\qquad \arg w^{*}=\arg w.
\]
Write $w=\rho e^{i\theta}$ with $\rho>0$. Then necessarily
\[
w^{*}=\frac{r^2}{\rho}e^{i\theta}.
\]
But $\overline{w}=\rho e^{-i\theta}$, hence
\[
\frac{r^2}{\overline{w}}=\frac{r^2}{\rho}e^{i\theta}=w^{*}.
\]
Therefore $w^{*}=\dfrac{r^2}{\overline{w}}$, and translating back,
\[
z^{*} \;=\; a+w^{*} \;=\; a+\frac{r^2}{\overline{z-a}}.
\]
In particular,
\[
|z^{*}-a|=\left|\frac{r^2}{\overline{z-a}}\right|=\frac{r^2}{|z-a|},
\quad\text{so}\quad
|z^{*}-a|\,|z-a|=r^2.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{(ii) Every Möbius map is a composition of inversions.}}
We use:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Inversion in a circle is \emph{anti-conformal} (it involves complex conjugation), and the composition of two inversions is conformal; in particular, any composition of an \emph{even} number of inversions is holomorphic (hence Möbius on $\widehat{\mathbb C}$).
	\item The Möbius group is generated by translations, similarities $z\mapsto \lambda z$ ($\lambda\neq 0$), and the reciprocal map $z\mapsto 1/z$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step A: Factor a general Möbius map into generators.}}
Let
\[
f(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\qquad ad-bc\neq 0.
\]
If $c=0$, then $f(z)=\alpha z+\beta$ is a similarity plus translation.

If $c\neq 0$, divide numerator and denominator by $c$ and set
\[
\alpha=\frac ac,\qquad \beta=\frac bc,\qquad \delta=\frac dc.
\]
Then
\[
f(z)=\frac{\alpha z+\beta}{z+\delta}
=\alpha+\frac{\beta-\alpha\delta}{z+\delta}.
\]
Hence, writing $T_\delta(z)=z+\delta$, $J(z)=1/z$, and $S_\lambda(z)=\lambda z$, we obtain
\[
f \;=\; T_\alpha \circ S_{\beta-\alpha\delta}\circ J \circ T_\delta.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{Step B: Each generator is a composition of inversions.}}
We allow \emph{line inversions}, i.e.\ reflections in straight lines (circles through $\infty$).

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Translations are compositions of two line inversions.}
	For translation by a real $t$ one may use reflections in the parallel vertical lines $x=0$ and $x=t/2$:
	\[
	(z\mapsto t-\overline{z})\circ(z\mapsto -\overline{z}) = z+t.
	\]
	
	\item \textbf{Dilations are compositions of two circle inversions.}
	Let $I_{0,r}(z)=\dfrac{r^2}{\overline{z}}$ be inversion in $|z|=r$. Then
	\[
	I_{0,r_2}\circ I_{0,r_1}(z)
	=\frac{r_2^2}{\overline{\,r_1^2/\overline{z}\,}}
	=\frac{r_2^2}{r_1^2}\,z,
	\]
	so a positive scaling is a composition of two inversions. Together with rotations this yields arbitrary complex scaling.
	
	\item \textbf{Rotations are compositions of two line inversions.}
	The composition of reflections in two lines through the origin meeting at angle $\theta/2$ is rotation by $\theta$.
	
	\item \textbf{The reciprocal map $J(z)=1/z$ is a composition of two inversions.}
	Inversion in the unit circle is $z\mapsto 1/\overline{z}$, and reflection in the real axis is $z\mapsto \overline{z}$. Hence
	\[
	(z\mapsto \overline{z})\circ(z\mapsto 1/\overline{z})=\frac{1}{z}.
	\]
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step C: Conclusion.}}
Since any Möbius map factors into translations, a similarity, and $z\mapsto 1/z$, and each of these factors is a composition of inversions, it follows that every Möbius map is a composition of inversions.

\subsection*{\textbf{Exercise 3. Vertical lines are length-minimizing in the upper half-plane model}}

\paragraph{\textbf{Problem.}}
Show from first principles that a vertical line segment is length-minimizing and hence defines a geodesic in the hyperbolic upper half-plane
\[
\mathbb H=\{x+iy:\;y>0\},\qquad
ds^2=\frac{dx^2+dy^2}{y^2}.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{Solution.}}
Let $\gamma:[t_0,t_1]\to\mathbb H$ be a piecewise $C^1$ curve written as $\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t))$ with $y(t)>0$. Its hyperbolic length is
\[
L(\gamma)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{\sqrt{x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2}}{y(t)}\,dt.
\]
Fix endpoints on the same vertical line:
\[
x(t_0)=x(t_1)=x_0,\qquad y(t_0)=y_0,\qquad y(t_1)=y_1.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{Lower bound for all curves.}}
Pointwise, $\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}\ge |y'|$, hence
\[
L(\gamma)\ge \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{|y'(t)|}{y(t)}\,dt
\ge \left|\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{y'(t)}{y(t)}\,dt\right|
= \left|\log y(t)\right|_{t_0}^{t_1}
= \left|\log\frac{y_1}{y_0}\right|.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{A vertical segment achieves the bound.}}
For the vertical curve $\gamma_v(t)=(x_0,y(t))$ with $y(t)$ monotone from $y_0$ to $y_1$ we have $x'(t)\equiv 0$, so
\[
L(\gamma_v)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{|y'(t)|}{y(t)}\,dt
=\left|\log\frac{y_1}{y_0}\right|.
\]
Thus $\gamma_v$ attains the universal lower bound and is globally length-minimizing. Hence it is (in particular) locally length-minimizing, i.e.\ a geodesic. Moreover,
\[
d_{\mathbb H}\big((x_0,y_0),(x_0,y_1)\big)=\left|\log\frac{y_1}{y_0}\right|.
\]

\subsection*{\textbf{Concrete examples for Step A and Step B (Möbius = composition of inversions)}}

\paragraph{\textbf{Step A (example): explicit factorization of a Möbius map.}}
Take
\[
f(z)=\frac{2z+3}{z+1}.
\]
Here $a=2,\ b=3,\ c=1,\ d=1$, so $ad-bc=2-3=-1\neq 0$. Since $c\neq 0$, set
\[
\alpha=\frac ac=2,\qquad \beta=\frac bc=3,\qquad \delta=\frac dc=1.
\]
Then the standard rearrangement gives
\[
f(z)=\alpha+\frac{\beta-\alpha\delta}{z+\delta}
=2+\frac{3-2\cdot 1}{z+1}
=2+\frac{1}{z+1}.
\]
So as a composition of generators,
\[
f = T_{2}\circ J \circ T_{1},
\qquad
T_{t}(z)=z+t,\quad J(z)=\frac1z.
\]
This is the cleanest kind of Step A: any nontrivial Möbius map reduces to “translate, invert, translate, then maybe scale”.

\medskip
\paragraph{\textbf{Step B (examples): write each generator as a composition of inversions.}}

\paragraph{\textbf{(B1) Translation as two line inversions (reflections).}}
Let $R_{x=c}$ be reflection in the vertical line $x=c$. In complex form,
\[
R_{x=c}(z)=2c-\overline{z}.
\]
Then for real $t$,
\[
R_{x=t/2}\circ R_{x=0}(z) = R_{x=t/2}(-\overline{z}) = t-\overline{(-\overline{z})}=z+t.
\]
So $T_t$ is a composition of two inversions in lines.

\paragraph{\textbf{(B2) Positive dilation as two circle inversions with the same center.}}
Let $I_{0,r}$ be inversion in the circle $|z|=r$:
\[
I_{0,r}(z)=\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z}}.
\]
Then
\[
I_{0,r_2}\circ I_{0,r_1}(z)
=\frac{r_2^2}{\overline{\,r_1^2/\overline{z}\,}}
=\frac{r_2^2}{r_1^2}\,z.
\]
So scaling by $\lambda>0$ is achieved by taking $r_2/r_1=\sqrt{\lambda}$.

\paragraph{\textbf{(B3) Rotation as two line inversions (reflections through lines through the origin).}}
Let $R_{\theta}$ denote reflection in the line through $0$ making angle $\theta$ with the real axis. Then
\[
R_{\theta/2}\circ R_{0}
\]
is rotation by angle $\theta$. (Classical Euclidean fact: the composition of two reflections in lines meeting at angle $\theta/2$ is the rotation by $\theta$.)

\paragraph{\textbf{(B4) Reciprocal map $J(z)=1/z$ as two inversions.}}
Inversion in the unit circle is $I_{0,1}(z)=1/\overline{z}$.
Reflection in the real axis is $R_{\mathbb R}(z)=\overline{z}$ (a line inversion).
Hence
\[
R_{\mathbb R}\circ I_{0,1}(z)=\overline{\left(\frac{1}{\overline{z}}\right)}=\frac{1}{z}.
\]

\paragraph{\textbf{(B5) Concrete Möbius example built from inversions.}}
For the earlier example $f(z)=2+\frac{1}{z+1}$ we have
\[
f = T_{2}\circ J \circ T_{1}.
\]
Now insert the explicit inversion decompositions:
\[
T_{1}=R_{x=1/2}\circ R_{x=0},
\qquad
J=R_{\mathbb R}\circ I_{0,1},
\qquad
T_{2}=R_{x=1}\circ R_{x=0}.
\]
Therefore one concrete “all-inversions” factorization is
\[
f
=
\bigl(R_{x=1}\circ R_{x=0}\bigr)
\circ
\bigl(R_{\mathbb R}\circ I_{0,1}\bigr)
\circ
\bigl(R_{x=1/2}\circ R_{x=0}\bigr),
\]
a composition of line inversions and one circle inversion.

\subsection*{\textbf{Geometric inversion vs holomorphic inversion (modulus inverted; angle preserved vs negated)}}

\paragraph{\textbf{Two maps that are both called “inversion”.}}
Fix a circle $C=\{|z-a|=r\}$.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Geometric (circle) inversion} in $C$:
	\[
	I_{a,r}(z)\;=\;a+\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}
	\qquad (z\neq a).
	\]
	This is the Euclidean inversion used in classical geometry (it is \emph{anti-holomorphic} because of $\overline{\phantom{z}}$).
	
	\item \textbf{Holomorphic (Möbius) “inversion”} about $a$ with radius $r$:
	\[
	J_{a,r}(z)\;=\;a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}
	\qquad (z\neq a).
	\]
	This is a Möbius transformation (holomorphic on $\C\setminus\{a\}$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\textbf{Polar form: what happens to modulus and argument.}}
Write
\[
z-a=\rho e^{i\theta}\qquad(\rho>0).
\]
Then

\begin{itemize}
	\item For \textbf{geometric inversion}:
	\[
	I_{a,r}(z)-a=\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{\rho e^{i\theta}}}
	=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho e^{-i\theta}}
	=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}\,e^{i\theta}.
	\]
	Hence
	\[
	|I_{a,r}(z)-a|=\frac{r^{2}}{|z-a|},\qquad
	\arg\bigl(I_{a,r}(z)-a\bigr)=\arg(z-a)=\theta.
	\]
	So the \textbf{modulus is inverted} but the \textbf{argument is preserved}.
	
	\item For \textbf{holomorphic inversion}:
	\[
	J_{a,r}(z)-a=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho e^{i\theta}}
	=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}\,e^{-i\theta}.
	\]
	Hence
	\[
	|J_{a,r}(z)-a|=\frac{r^{2}}{|z-a|},\qquad
	\arg\bigl(J_{a,r}(z)-a\bigr)=-\arg(z-a)=-\theta.
	\]
	So the \textbf{modulus is inverted} and the \textbf{argument is negated}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{\textbf{Relation between them: “reflection + geometric inversion”.}}
Define reflection across the line through $a$ parallel to the real axis:
\[
R_a(z)=a+\overline{z-a}.
\]
Then
\[
J_{a,r}=R_a\circ I_{a,r}.
\]
Indeed, for $z\neq a$,
\[
(R_a\circ I_{a,r})(z)
=a+\overline{\,I_{a,r}(z)-a\,}
=a+\overline{\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}}
=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}
=J_{a,r}(z).
\]

\subsection*{\textbf{Three common “inversions” in complex analysis and geometry}}

\paragraph{\textbf{(A) The basic Möbius inversion (unit-circle inversion).}}
\[
J(z)=\frac{1}{z}\qquad (z\neq 0).
\]
If $z=\rho e^{i\theta}$, then
\[
J(z)=\frac{1}{\rho}e^{-i\theta},
\qquad
|J(z)|=\frac{1}{|z|},\qquad \arg J(z)=-\arg z.
\]
It fixes the unit circle: if $|z|=1$ then $|J(z)|=1$.

\paragraph{\textbf{(B) Geometric inversion in the circle $|z-a|=r$ (anti-holomorphic).}}
\[
I_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}\qquad (z\neq a).
\]
Write $z-a=\rho e^{i\theta}$, then
\[
I_{a,r}(z)-a=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}e^{i\theta},
\qquad
|I_{a,r}(z)-a|=\frac{r^{2}}{|z-a|},\qquad
\arg\bigl(I_{a,r}(z)-a\bigr)=\arg(z-a).
\]
Thus it inverts the radius but \emph{preserves} the argument (same ray from $a$). It fixes the circle $|z-a|=r$ pointwise.

\paragraph{\textbf{(C) Holomorphic “circle inversion” (a Möbius map).}}
\[
H_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}\qquad (z\neq a).
\]
Write $z-a=\rho e^{i\theta}$, then
\[
H_{a,r}(z)-a=\frac{r^{2}}{\rho}e^{-i\theta},
\qquad
|H_{a,r}(z)-a|=\frac{r^{2}}{|z-a|},\qquad
\arg\bigl(H_{a,r}(z)-a\bigr)=-\arg(z-a).
\]
Thus it inverts the radius and \emph{negates} the argument (reflection of the ray). It preserves the circle $|z-a|=r$ as a set (not pointwise).

\paragraph{\textbf{Relationship between (B) and (C).}}
Let
\[
R_a(z)=a+\overline{z-a}
\]
be reflection across the horizontal line through $a$. Then
\[
H_{a,r}=R_a\circ I_{a,r}.
\]

	\subsection*{\textbf{Inversion as a Möbius transformation (and its geometric cousin)}}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Möbius transformations.}}
	A Möbius map is
	\[
	f(z)=\frac{Az+B}{Cz+D},\qquad AD-BC\neq 0,
	\]
	and it is represented (projectively) by the matrix
	\[
	\begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{pmatrix}
	\quad\text{in } PSL(2,\C).
	\]
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{1) Where is the simple inversion $1/z$ in Möbius form?}}
	Take
	\[
	f(z)=\frac{0\cdot z+1}{1\cdot z+0}=\frac{1}{z}.
	\]
	So $1/z$ corresponds to the matrix
	\[
	\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}
	\quad\text{(in } PSL(2,\C)\text{).}
	\]
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{2) Where is the “radius-$r$” holomorphic inversion $r^{2}/z$?}}
	Just scale:
	\[
	f(z)=\frac{0\cdot z+r^{2}}{1\cdot z+0}=\frac{r^{2}}{z},
	\qquad
	\begin{pmatrix}0 & r^{2}\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}.
	\]
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{3) Where is the shifted version $a+\dfrac{r^{2}}{z-a}$?}}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Conjugation by translations.}}
	Let $T_a(z)=z+a$. Starting from $r^{2}/z$ we obtain
	\[
	H_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}
	= T_a\circ\left(\frac{r^{2}}{z}\right)\circ T_{-a}(z).
	\]
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{As an explicit fraction $(Az+B)/(Cz+D)$.}}
	Compute:
	\[
	H_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}
	=\frac{a(z-a)+r^{2}}{z-a}
	=\frac{az+(r^{2}-a^{2})}{z-a}.
	\]
	Therefore, matching with $\dfrac{Az+B}{Cz+D}$, we read off
	\[
	A=a,\qquad B=r^{2}-a^{2},\qquad C=1,\qquad D=-a,
	\]
	and hence
	\[
	H_{a,r}(z)\ \longleftrightarrow\
	\begin{pmatrix}
		a & r^{2}-a^{2}\\[2pt]
		1 & -a
	\end{pmatrix},
	\qquad
	\det=
	a(-a)-(r^{2}-a^{2})\cdot 1=-r^{2}\neq 0.
	\]
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{4) What about geometric circle inversion $a+\dfrac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}$?}}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Not Möbius (anti-holomorphic).}}
	The geometric circle inversion is
	\[
	I_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}},
	\]
	and it is \emph{not} Möbius because it depends on $\overline{z}$ (it is anti-holomorphic).
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{But it becomes Möbius after composing with a reflection.}}
	Let
	\[
	R_a(z)=a+\overline{z-a}
	\]
	(reflection across the horizontal line through $a$). Then
	\[
	H_{a,r}=R_a\circ I_{a,r}.
	\]
	
	% =========================================================
	% Opposite direction: from (A,B,C,D) to factorization and to H_{a,r}-form
	% =========================================================
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{Opposite direction: starting from $(A,B,C,D)$}}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{(I) Universal factorization when $C\neq 0$.}}
	Assume $C\neq 0$. Define
	\[
	\alpha=\frac{A}{C},\qquad
	\delta=\frac{D}{C},\qquad
	\beta=\frac{BC-AD}{C^{2}}.
	\]
	Then the identity
	\[
	\frac{Az+B}{Cz+D}=\alpha+\frac{\beta}{z+\delta}
	\]
	holds. Consequently,
	\[
	f=T_{\alpha}\circ S_{\beta}\circ J\circ T_{\delta},
	\qquad
	T_t(z)=z+t,\quad S_\lambda(z)=\lambda z,\quad J(z)=\frac{1}{z}.
	\]
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{(II) The affine case $C=0$.}}
	If $C=0$, then
	\[
	f(z)=\frac{Az+B}{D}=\left(\frac{A}{D}\right)z+\left(\frac{B}{D}\right),
	\]
	so $f$ is just a similarity plus a translation.
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{(III) When is $f$ of the special form $H_{a,r}(z)=a+\dfrac{r^{2}}{z-a}$?}}
	Assume $C\neq 0$. The map $f(z)=\dfrac{Az+B}{Cz+D}$ is of the form
	\[
	H_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}
	\]
	(up to an overall nonzero scalar in the coefficients) exactly when
	\[
	A=-D.
	\]
	In that case one can set
	\[
	a=\frac{A}{C}=-\frac{D}{C},
	\qquad
	r^{2}=\frac{BC-AD}{C^{2}},
	\]
	and then indeed
	\[
	\frac{Az+B}{Cz+D}=a+\frac{r^{2}}{z-a}.
	\]
	
	
	% =========================================================
	% How the Möbius/inversion discussion relates to Exercise 2(ii)
	% =========================================================
	
	\subsection*{\textbf{How this relates to the exercise: why (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii)}}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Goal of (ii).}}
	We want to prove: every Möbius map
	\[
	f(z)=\frac{Az+B}{Cz+D}\qquad(AD-BC\neq 0)
	\]
	is a composition of inversions (in circles/lines).
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Step 1: (i) provides the explicit inversion map.}}
	From (i), inversion in the circle $|z-a|=r$ is the map
	\[
	I_{a,r}(z)=a+\frac{r^{2}}{\overline{z-a}}.
	\]
	This is a genuine Euclidean circle inversion: it sends $z$ to the unique point $z^{*}$ on the ray from $a$ through $z$
	such that $|z-a|\cdot|z^{*}-a|=r^{2}$.
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Step 2: reduce an arbitrary Möbius map to generators.}}
	If $C\neq 0$, define
	\[
	\alpha=\frac{A}{C},\qquad
	\delta=\frac{D}{C},\qquad
	\beta=\frac{BC-AD}{C^{2}}.
	\]
	Then one checks the identity
	\[
	\frac{Az+B}{Cz+D}=\alpha+\frac{\beta}{z+\delta}.
	\]
	Hence, writing
	\[
	T_t(z)=z+t,\qquad S_\lambda(z)=\lambda z,\qquad J(z)=\frac{1}{z},
	\]
	we obtain the factorization
	\[
	f \;=\; T_{\alpha}\circ S_{\beta}\circ J\circ T_{\delta}.
	\]
	If $C=0$, then $f(z)=\left(\frac{A}{D}\right)z+\left(\frac{B}{D}\right)$ is already a similarity plus translation.
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Step 3: realize each generator as a composition of inversions.}}
	We allow inversions in circles and also in lines (lines are circles through $\infty$, i.e.\ reflections).
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{The inversion $J(z)=1/z$ is a composition of inversions.}
		By (i), inversion in the unit circle is
		\[
		I_{0,1}(z)=\frac{1}{\overline{z}}.
		\]
Reflection in the real axis is the line inversion
\[
R_{\mathbb{R}}(z)=\overline{z}.
\]
Therefore
\[
R_{\mathbb{R}}\circ I_{0,1}(z)=\overline{\left(\frac{1}{\overline{z}}\right)}=\frac{1}{z}=J(z),
\]
so $J$ is the composition of a circle inversion and a line inversion
		
		\item \textbf{Translations are compositions of two line inversions.}
		Reflection in the vertical line $x=c$ is
		\[
		R_{x=c}(z)=2c-\overline{z}.
		\]
		Hence for real $t$,
		\[
		R_{x=t/2}\circ R_{x=0}(z)=z+t.
		\]
		Thus $T_t$ is a composition of two line inversions (and similarly for any complex translation by choosing parallel lines).
		
		\item \textbf{Positive scalings are compositions of two circle inversions.}
		Inversion in $|z|=r$ is $I_{0,r}(z)=r^{2}/\overline{z}$, so
		\[
		I_{0,r_2}\circ I_{0,r_1}(z)=\frac{r_2^2}{r_1^2}\,z.
		\]
		Thus $S_\lambda$ with $\lambda>0$ is a composition of two circle inversions. Rotations are compositions of two line inversions,
		so arbitrary nonzero complex scalings are obtained by combining these.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\paragraph{\textbf{Conclusion.}}
	Every Möbius map factors into translations, scalings/rotations, and $J(z)=1/z$, and each of these is a composition of inversions.
	Therefore every Möbius map is a composition of inversions, proving (ii).

	\subsection*{Problem 4 (Hyperbolic distance as a cross--ratio)}
	
	\paragraph{Problem statement.}
	Let $z_1,z_2$ be distinct points in the upper half plane $\H$.
	Suppose that the hyperbolic line through $z_1$ and $z_2$ meets the real axis
	at points $w_1$ and $w_2$, where $z_1$ lies on the hyperbolic line segment
	$w_1 z_2$ (and where one $w_i$ may be $\infty$).
	Show that the hyperbolic distance
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(z_1,z_2)=\log r,
	\]
	where $r$ is the cross-ratio of the four points $z_1,z_2,w_1,w_2$ taken in an
	appropriate order.
	
	\paragraph{Theory (what we use).}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item In the upper half-plane model, the hyperbolic metric is
		\[
		ds=\frac{|dz|}{\Im z},
		\qquad
		dA=\frac{dx\,dy}{y^2}\quad (z=x+iy).
		\]
		\item The orientation-preserving isometries of $(\H,ds)$ are exactly the Möbius
		transformations with real coefficients:
		\[
		T(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\qquad a,b,c,d\in\R,\ ad-bc=1,
		\]
		i.e.\ $T\in \PSL(2,\R)$.
		\item The cross-ratio of four (possibly extended) complex numbers is
		\[
		[a,b,c,d]=\frac{(a-c)(b-d)}{(a-d)(b-c)},
		\]
		and it is invariant under Möbius transformations:
		\[
		[T(a),T(b),T(c),T(d)]=[a,b,c,d].
		\]
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\paragraph{Solution.}
	Let $\gamma$ be the (unique) hyperbolic geodesic through $z_1$ and $z_2$.
	It is either a vertical line or a semicircle orthogonal to $\R$, and it meets
	$\R\cup\{\infty\}$ at the endpoints $w_1,w_2$.
	
	Choose an isometry $T\in \PSL(2,\R)$ such that
	\[
	T(w_1)=0,\qquad T(w_2)=\infty.
	\]
	Then $T(\gamma)$ is the imaginary axis, so we can write
	\[
	T(z_1)=i y_1,\qquad T(z_2)=i y_2
	\quad\text{for some }y_1,y_2>0.
	\]
	Because $z_1$ lies on the hyperbolic segment from $w_1$ to $z_2$ along $\gamma$,
	the order along $\gamma$ is $w_1,\ z_1,\ z_2,\ w_2$, hence on the imaginary axis
	we have $0,\ i y_1,\ i y_2,\ \infty$ with $0<y_1<y_2$.
	
	\medskip
	\noindent\textbf{Step 1: distance on the imaginary axis.}
	Along the curve $z(t)=it$ (so $dz=i\,dt$ and $\Im z=t$),
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(i y_1,i y_2)=\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{|dz|}{\Im z}
	=\int_{y_1}^{y_2}\frac{dt}{t}
	=\log\!\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right).
	\]
	
	\medskip
	\noindent\textbf{Step 2: identify the cross-ratio.}
	Consider the cross-ratio with the order
	\[
	r=[w_1,z_1,z_2,w_2]
	=\frac{(w_1-z_2)(z_1-w_2)}{(w_1-w_2)(z_1-z_2)}
	\quad\text{(any equivalent cross-ratio formula is fine)}.
	\]
	By Möbius invariance,
	\[
	r=[T(w_1),T(z_1),T(z_2),T(w_2)]=[0, i y_1, i y_2, \infty].
	\]
	Using the standard limiting rule for $\infty$, one finds
	\[
	[0, i y_1, i y_2, \infty]
	=\frac{i y_2}{i y_1}
	=\frac{y_2}{y_1}>1.
	\]
	Therefore
	\[
	\log r=\log\!\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right)=d_{\hyp}(i y_1,i y_2).
	\]
	
	\medskip
	\noindent\textbf{Step 3: return to the original points.}
	Since $T$ is an isometry,
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(z_1,z_2)=d_{\hyp}(T(z_1),T(z_2))=d_{\hyp}(i y_1,i y_2)=\log r.
	\]
	This proves the claim.
	
	\paragraph{Example.}
	Take $z_1=i$ and $z_2=2i$. The geodesic is the imaginary axis, with endpoints
	$w_1=0$ and $w_2=\infty$. Then
	\[
	r=[0,i,2i,\infty]=\frac{2i}{i}=2,
	\qquad
	d_{\hyp}(i,2i)=\log 2.
	\]

	% =========================================================
	\subsection*{Problem 5 (Circles in spherical and hyperbolic geometry)}
	
	\paragraph{Problem statement.}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Let $P\in S^2\subset\R^3$ be a point on the round sphere.
		The spherical circle with centre $P$ and radius $\rho$ is the set
		\[
		\{\,w\in S^2 \mid d_{\sph}(w,P)=\rho\,\},
		\]
		where $d_{\sph}$ is the spherical metric (induced by the first fundamental form
		of the embedding).
		Prove that a spherical circle of radius $\rho$ is a Euclidean circle.
		Prove that its circumference is $2\pi\sin(\rho)$ and that it bounds a disc on
		$S^2$ of area $2\pi(1-\cos(\rho))$.
		
		\item Let $C\subset\H$ be a hyperbolic circle with centre $p\in\H$ and radius $\rho$,
		i.e.\ the locus
		\[
		\{\,w\in\H \mid d_{\hyp}(w,p)=\rho\,\}
		\quad\text{for some }\rho>0.
		\]
		Show that $C$ is a Euclidean circle. If $p=ic$ for $c\in\R_{>0}$, find the centre
		and radius of $C$ as a Euclidean circle.
		Show the hyperbolic circumference of $C$ is $2\pi\sinh(\rho)$, and the hyperbolic
		area of the disc it bounds is $2\pi(\cosh(\rho)-1)$.
		Deduce that no hyperbolic triangle contains a hyperbolic circle of radius
		$>\cosh^{-1}(3/2)$.
		
		\item Show that there is some $\delta>0$ such that, in any hyperbolic triangle,
		the union of the $\delta$-neighbourhoods of two of the sides completely contains
		the third side. (Does such a $\delta$ exist for triangles in the Euclidean plane?)
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\paragraph{(a) Theory.}
	The round unit sphere $S^2\subset\R^3$ has constant curvature $+1$.
	Great circles are geodesics, and spherical distance is the angle between radius
	vectors. Rotations of $\R^3$ act by isometries of $(S^2,d_{\sph})$.
	
	\paragraph{(a) Solution.}
	By a rotation, assume $P$ is the north pole:
	\[
	P=(0,0,1).
	\]
	Write a point $w\in S^2$ in spherical coordinates as
	\[
	w=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta),
	\]
	where $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is the colatitude (angle from the north pole).
	On the unit sphere, the spherical distance from $P$ to $w$ equals $\theta$:
	\[
	d_{\sph}(w,P)=\theta.
	\]
	Hence the spherical circle of radius $\rho$ is given by $\theta=\rho$, i.e.
	\[
	w=(\sin\rho\cos\varphi,\ \sin\rho\sin\varphi,\ \cos\rho),
	\qquad \varphi\in[0,2\pi).
	\]
	This set is exactly the intersection of $S^2$ with the plane $z=\cos\rho$.
	Therefore it is a Euclidean circle of radius $\sin\rho$ in that plane.
	
	Its Euclidean circumference is
	\[
	\operatorname{length}=2\pi(\sin\rho).
	\]
	
	The region on $S^2$ bounded by this circle is the spherical cap
	\[
	\{\,(\theta,\varphi)\mid 0\le \theta\le \rho,\ 0\le \varphi<2\pi\,\}.
	\]
	Using the area element on $S^2$ in spherical coordinates,
	\[
	dA=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi,
	\]
	the area is
	\[
	\operatorname{area}
	=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\rho \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi
	=2\pi(1-\cos\rho).
	\]
	
	\paragraph{(a) Example.}
	For $\rho=\pi/2$ the set is the equator ($z=0$), a Euclidean circle of radius $1$.
	Its circumference is $2\pi\sin(\pi/2)=2\pi$ and the spherical cap area is
	$2\pi(1-\cos(\pi/2))=2\pi$ (a hemisphere).
	
	% ---------------------------------------------------------
	\paragraph{(b) Theory.}
	In the upper half-plane model, a useful distance formula is
	\[
	\cosh d_{\hyp}(z,w)
	=1+\frac{|z-w|^2}{2\,\Im z\,\Im w}.
	\]
	Geodesic polar coordinates around a point give the metric
	\[
	ds^2=d\rho^2+\sinh^2(\rho)\,d\theta^2,
	\]
	so circles of hyperbolic radius $\rho$ have length $2\pi\sinh\rho$ and disks have
	area $2\pi(\cosh\rho-1)$.
	
	\paragraph{(b) Solution: Euclidean centre/radius when $p=ic$.}
	Let $p=ic$ with $c>0$, and write $w=x+iy$ with $y>0$.
	The condition $d_{\hyp}(w,ic)=\rho$ is equivalent to
	\[
	\cosh\rho
	=1+\frac{|w-ic|^2}{2\,(\Im w)(\Im(ic))}
	=1+\frac{x^2+(y-c)^2}{2yc}.
	\]
	Rearrange:
	\[
	x^2+(y-c)^2 = 2yc(\cosh\rho-1).
	\]
	Expand and collect terms:
	\[
	x^2+y^2-2cy+c^2 = 2yc(\cosh\rho-1)
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	x^2+y^2-2c(\cosh\rho)\,y+c^2=0.
	\]
	Complete the square in $y$:
	\[
	x^2+\bigl(y-c\cosh\rho\bigr)^2
	= c^2\sinh^2\rho.
	\]
	Thus $C$ is a Euclidean circle with
	\[
	\text{Euclidean centre }(0,\ c\cosh\rho),
	\qquad
	\text{Euclidean radius }c\sinh\rho.
	\]
	
	\paragraph{(b) Hyperbolic circumference and area.}
	In hyperbolic polar coordinates $(\rho,\theta)$ around the centre, the metric is
	\[
	ds^2=d\rho^2+\sinh^2(\rho)\,d\theta^2.
	\]
	Fixing $\rho$ gives a circle with line element $ds=\sinh(\rho)\,d\theta$, hence
	\[
	\operatorname{length}(\partial D_\rho)
	=\int_0^{2\pi}\sinh(\rho)\,d\theta
	=2\pi\sinh(\rho).
	\]
	The area element is $dA=\sinh(t)\,dt\,d\theta$, so
	\[
	\operatorname{area}(D_\rho)
	=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\rho \sinh(t)\,dt\,d\theta
	=2\pi(\cosh\rho-1).
	\]
	
	\paragraph{(b) Deduction about triangles.}
	For a hyperbolic triangle with angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$, Gauss--Bonnet gives
	\[
	\operatorname{area}(\triangle)=\pi-(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)<\pi.
	\]
	If a triangle contained a hyperbolic disk of radius $r$, then
	\[
	\pi>\operatorname{area}(\triangle)\ge \operatorname{area}(D_r)=2\pi(\cosh r-1),
	\]
	hence $\cosh r-1<\tfrac12$, i.e.\ $\cosh r<\tfrac32$.
	Therefore
	\[
	r<\cosh^{-1}\!\left(\frac32\right),
	\]
	so no hyperbolic triangle contains a hyperbolic circle of radius
	$>\cosh^{-1}(3/2)$.
	
	\paragraph{(b) Example.}
	If $p=2i$ and $\rho=1$, then the Euclidean circle has centre
	$(0,2\cosh 1)$ and radius $2\sinh 1$.
	
	% ---------------------------------------------------------
	\paragraph{(c) Solution (thin triangles and a concrete constant).}
	There exists a universal constant $\delta>0$ such that every geodesic triangle in
	$\H$ is \emph{$\delta$-thin}: each side is contained in the $\delta$-neighbourhood
	of the union of the other two sides. Equivalently, for any hyperbolic triangle,
	the union of the $\delta$-neighbourhoods of two sides contains the third side.
	
	A sharp choice (for curvature $-1$) is
	\[
	\delta=\arsinh(1)=\log(1+\sqrt2).
	\]
	
	\medskip
	\noindent\textbf{Sketch of why this works.}
	Given any hyperbolic triangle, extend its sides to an \emph{ideal triangle} (with
	vertices on $\partial\H=\R\cup\{\infty\}$) whose sides contain the original ones.
	Distances to the union of the other sides can only increase in the ideal case,
	so it suffices to bound the thickness of ideal triangles.
	
	By an isometry in $\PSL(2,\R)$, any ideal triangle is congruent to the standard
	ideal triangle with vertices $0,1,\infty$.
	Its sides are the geodesics
	\[
	L_0:\ x=0,\qquad L_1:\ x=1,\qquad L_2:\ (x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\tfrac14.
	\]
	Consider points on $L_0$, namely $z=iy$ with $y>0$.
	One computes (using standard distance-to-geodesic formulas, or by mapping $L_2$
	to the imaginary axis via $z\mapsto \frac{z}{1-z}$) that
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(iy,L_1)=\arsinh\!\left(\frac1y\right),
	\qquad
	d_{\hyp}(iy,L_2)=\arsinh(y).
	\]
	Hence the distance from $iy$ to the union $L_1\cup L_2$ is
	\[
	d_{\hyp}\bigl(iy,\,L_1\cup L_2\bigr)
	=\min\!\left\{\arsinh\!\left(\frac1y\right),\ \arsinh(y)\right\}
	\le \arsinh(1).
	\]
	The maximum occurs at $y=1$, giving
	\[
	\sup_{y>0}\ d_{\hyp}\bigl(iy,\,L_1\cup L_2\bigr)=\arsinh(1)=\log(1+\sqrt2).
	\]
	By symmetry the same bound holds for the other sides, so the standard ideal
	triangle is $\delta$-thin with $\delta=\arsinh(1)$, and therefore \emph{every}
	hyperbolic triangle is $\delta$-thin.
	
	\medskip
	\noindent\textbf{Euclidean comparison.}
	No such universal $\delta$ exists in the Euclidean plane: scaling an equilateral
	triangle by a large factor scales all distances, so any fixed $\delta$ fails for
	sufficiently large triangles.

	\subsection*{Problem 6 (Hyperbolic law of sines)}
	
\subsubsection*{problem}
		Fix a hyperbolic triangle $\Delta\subset \HH^2$ with interior angles $A,B,C$ and side lengths
		(in the hyperbolic metric) $a,b,c$, where $a$ is opposite the vertex with angle $A$, etc.
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Suppose that $C$ is a right angle. By applying the \emph{hyperbolic cosine formula} in two different ways,
			prove that
			\[
			\sin(A)\,\sinh(c)=\sinh(a).
			\]
			\item Deduce that for a general hyperbolic triangle,
			\[
			\frac{\sin(A)}{\sinh(a)}=\frac{\sin(B)}{\sinh(b)}=\frac{\sin(C)}{\sinh(c)}.
			\]
		\end{enumerate}

	
	\subsubsection*{Theory: the cosine formulae in $\HH^2$}
	For a hyperbolic triangle with side lengths $a,b,c$ opposite angles $A,B,C$ one has:
	
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Cosine formula (sides):}
		\begin{align*}
			\cosh(a)&=\cosh(b)\cosh(c)-\sinh(b)\sinh(c)\cos(A),\\
			\cosh(b)&=\cosh(c)\cosh(a)-\sinh(c)\sinh(a)\cos(B),\\
			\cosh(c)&=\cosh(a)\cosh(b)-\sinh(a)\sinh(b)\cos(C).
		\end{align*}
		
		\item \textbf{Cosine formula (angles):}
		\begin{align*}
			\cos(A)&=-\cos(B)\cos(C)+\sin(B)\sin(C)\cosh(a),\\
			\cos(B)&=-\cos(C)\cos(A)+\sin(C)\sin(A)\cosh(b),\\
			\cos(C)&=-\cos(A)\cos(B)+\sin(A)\sin(B)\cosh(c).
		\end{align*}
	\end{itemize}
	
	(These are dual to each other; one clean way to see the duality is via the \emph{polar triangle}
	in constant curvature $-1$ geometry.)
	
	\subsubsection*{Solution (a): right triangle, $C=\pi/2$}
	Assume $C=\pi/2$ so $\cos(C)=0$ and $\sin(C)=1$.
	
	\paragraph{Step 1: apply the cosine formula (sides) to $c$.}
	From the side-cosine formula for $c$ we get
	\[
	\cosh(c)=\cosh(a)\cosh(b)-\sinh(a)\sinh(b)\cos(C)
	=\cosh(a)\cosh(b).
	\tag{1}
	\]
	
	\paragraph{Step 2: apply the cosine formula (sides) to $b$ and eliminate $\cosh(c)$.}
	Use the side-cosine formula for $b$:
	\[
	\cosh(b)=\cosh(c)\cosh(a)-\sinh(c)\sinh(a)\cos(B).
	\]
	Insert $\cosh(c)=\cosh(a)\cosh(b)$ from (1):
	\[
	\cosh(b)=\cosh(a)\cosh(b)\cosh(a)-\sinh(c)\sinh(a)\cos(B)
	=\cosh(b)\cosh^2(a)-\sinh(c)\sinh(a)\cos(B).
	\]
	Rearranging gives
	\[
	\sinh(c)\sinh(a)\cos(B)=\cosh(b)\bigl(\cosh^2(a)-1\bigr)=\cosh(b)\sinh^2(a).
	\]
	Hence
	\[
	\cos(B)=\cosh(b)\,\frac{\sinh(a)}{\sinh(c)}.
	\tag{2}
	\]
	
	\paragraph{Step 3: apply the cosine formula (angles) to $B$ (using $C=\pi/2$).}
	From the angle-cosine formula for $B$ and $C=\pi/2$ we obtain
	\[
	\cos(B)=-\cos(C)\cos(A)+\sin(C)\sin(A)\cosh(b)=\sin(A)\cosh(b).
	\tag{3}
	\]
	Comparing (2) and (3) and cancelling $\cosh(b)>0$ yields
	\[
	\sin(A)=\frac{\sinh(a)}{\sinh(c)}.
	\]
	Equivalently,
	\[
	\sin(A)\,\sinh(c)=\sinh(a),
	\]
	which is exactly the desired identity.
	
	\subsubsection*{Solution (b): the hyperbolic law of sines for a general triangle}
	Let $\Delta ABC$ be any hyperbolic triangle and drop the hyperbolic perpendicular from $C$ to the side
	$AB$; let $D\in AB$ be the foot, so $\angle ADC=\angle BDC=\pi/2$.
	
	In the right triangle $\triangle ACD$, the hypotenuse is $AC=b$ and the angle at $A$ is $A$.
	Applying part (a) to $\triangle ACD$ gives
	\[
	\sin(A)\,\sinh(b)=\sinh(CD).
	\tag{4}
	\]
	In the right triangle $\triangle BCD$, the hypotenuse is $BC=a$ and the angle at $B$ is $B$.
	Applying part (a) to $\triangle BCD$ gives
	\[
	\sin(B)\,\sinh(a)=\sinh(CD).
	\tag{5}
	\]
	Comparing (4) and (5) yields
	\[
	\sin(A)\,\sinh(b)=\sin(B)\,\sinh(a)
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	\frac{\sin(A)}{\sinh(a)}=\frac{\sin(B)}{\sinh(b)}.
	\]
	Now repeat the same argument by dropping a perpendicular from $A$ to $BC$ (or from $B$ to $CA$)
	to obtain equality with the remaining ratio, hence
	\[
	\frac{\sin(A)}{\sinh(a)}=\frac{\sin(B)}{\sinh(b)}=\frac{\sin(C)}{\sinh(c)}.
	\]

	\subsection*{Problem 7 (Ultraparallel lines and reflections)}
	\paragraph{Problem.}
	\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
		\item Show that two hyperbolic lines have a common perpendicular if and only if they are \emph{ultraparallel},
		and that in this case the common perpendicular is unique. Show that, up to isometry, for $t>0$ there is a unique
		configuration of ultraparallel lines for which the segment of the common perpendicular between the lines has length $t$.
		\item Let $l_1,l_2$ be ultraparallel hyperbolic lines, and let $r_{l_i}$ denote the hyperbolic isometry given by reflection in $l_i$.
		Prove that $r_{l_1}\circ r_{l_2}$ has infinite order.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	
	\subsubsection*{Theory}
	In $\HH^2$ (e.g. the upper half–plane model $\HH=\{x+iy:y>0\}$), two distinct complete geodesics fall into exactly one of:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Intersecting:} they meet in $\HH$.
		\item \textbf{Parallel (asymptotic):} disjoint in $\HH$ but share exactly one ideal endpoint on $\partial\HH=\R\cup\{\infty\}$.
		\item \textbf{Ultraparallel:} disjoint in $\HH$ with \emph{two distinct} ideal endpoints each (no shared ideal endpoint).
	\end{itemize}
	Reflection in a geodesic is an isometry (orientation-reversing). The composition of two reflections is:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item elliptic if the lines intersect,
		\item parabolic if they are asymptotically parallel,
		\item hyperbolic (a translation along an axis) if they are ultraparallel.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsubsection*{Solution (a): common perpendicular $\Longleftrightarrow$ ultraparallel; uniqueness; normal form with parameter $t$}
	
	\paragraph{($\Rightarrow$) If a common perpendicular exists, the lines are ultraparallel.}
	Suppose $l_1,l_2$ share a common perpendicular geodesic $m$ meeting each at right angles. Then $l_1$ and $l_2$
	cannot intersect (otherwise there would be a triangle with two right angles, impossible in $\HH^2$ since
	$\text{Area}=\pi-(A+B+C)>0$ forces $A+B+C<\pi$). They also cannot share an ideal endpoint: if they did, they would
	approach each other toward that cusp and no geodesic could meet both at right angles at finite points.
	Hence $l_1,l_2$ are disjoint and have no common ideal endpoint, i.e. they are ultraparallel.
	
	\paragraph{($\Leftarrow$) If $l_1,l_2$ are ultraparallel, a common perpendicular exists and is unique.}
	Let $g\in\Isom(\HH^2)$ be an isometry sending the pair $(l_1,l_2)$ to a convenient standard position.
	Because $\Isom(\HH^2)$ acts $3$–transitively on the boundary $\partial\HH$ (via Möbius maps),
	we may send the four ideal endpoints of $l_1$ and $l_2$ to symmetric positions so that
	\[
	g(l_1)=\{z\in\HH:|z|=1\},\qquad g(l_2)=\{z\in\HH:|z|=e^t\},
	\]
	for some $t>0$, i.e. two nested semicircular geodesics orthogonal to $\R$.
	Then the imaginary axis
	\[
	m=\{iy:y>0\}
	\]
	meets both semicircles orthogonally (at $i$ and $ie^t$) and therefore is a common perpendicular in this model.
	Pulling back by $g^{-1}$ gives a common perpendicular for the original pair.
	
	Uniqueness: in this normal form, the only geodesic perpendicular to both circles $|z|=1$ and $|z|=e^t$ is the
	imaginary axis (by symmetry and the fact that orthogonality at a point fixes the tangent direction).
	Hence the common perpendicular in the original configuration is unique.
	
	\paragraph{The length parameter $t$ and uniqueness up to isometry.}
	On the imaginary axis, the hyperbolic distance is
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(iy_1,iy_2)=\left|\log\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right|.
	\]
	Thus
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(i,ie^t)=\log(e^t)=t.
	\]
	Conversely, any ultraparallel pair with common perpendicular segment of length $t$ is isometric to this standard pair,
	so the configuration is unique up to isometry for each $t>0$.
	
	\subsubsection*{Solution (b): $r_{l_1}\circ r_{l_2}$ has infinite order}
	
	Continue in the standard position from (a):
	\[
	l_1:\ |z|=1,\qquad l_2:\ |z|=e^t.
	\]
	Reflection in the geodesic semicircle $|z|=r$ (orthogonal to $\R$) is given by the circle inversion with conjugation:
	\[
	r_{|z|=r}(z)=\frac{r^2}{\overline{z}}.
	\]
	Hence
	\[
	r_{l_1}(z)=\frac{1}{\overline{z}},\qquad r_{l_2}(z)=\frac{e^{2t}}{\overline{z}}.
	\]
	Therefore
	\[
	(r_{l_1}\circ r_{l_2})(z)
	= r_{l_1}\!\left(\frac{e^{2t}}{\overline{z}}\right)
	= \frac{1}{\overline{\,e^{2t}/\overline{z}\,}}
	= \frac{1}{e^{2t}/z}
	= e^{-2t}z.
	\]
	This is the hyperbolic translation along the imaginary axis (its invariant axis) by length $2t$ since
	\[
	d_{\hyp}(iy,\,i e^{-2t}y)=|{-2t}|=2t.
	\]
	Because $e^{-2t}\neq 1$, the iterates satisfy
	\[
	(r_{l_1}\circ r_{l_2})^n(z)=e^{-2nt}z\neq z\quad(n\neq 0),
	\]
	so the element has infinite order.

	\subsection*{Problem 8 (Ideal square and hyperbolic surfaces)}
	

		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Consider the \emph{ideal} hyperbolic square with vertices at $0,1,\infty,-1$ in the upper half–plane model.
			By gluing the edges of the square by isometries, or otherwise, prove that there is a \emph{complete} hyperbolic metric
			on the smooth surface $S^2\setminus\{p,q,r\}$ given by the complement of $3$ distinct points in the sphere.
			\item Construct a non-orientable compact hyperbolic surface.
		\end{enumerate}

	
	\subsubsection*{Solution (a): gluing an ideal square to obtain $S^2\setminus\{p,q,r\}$ (a pair of pants)}
	Let $Q\subset\HH$ be the ideal quadrilateral with vertices in cyclic order
	\[
	-1,\ 0,\ 1,\ \infty.
	\]
	Its sides are the geodesics:
	\[
	\ell_{-1}: x=-1,\qquad
	\ell_{1}: x=1,\qquad
	s_-:\text{ semicircle from }-1\text{ to }0,\qquad
	s_+:\text{ semicircle from }0\text{ to }1.
	\]
	
	\paragraph{Step 1: choose explicit side-pairing isometries.}
	Define the following isometries of $\HH$:
	\[
	T(z)=z+2\quad(\text{translation}),\qquad
	R(z)=-\overline{z}\quad(\text{reflection in the imaginary axis}).
	\]
	Then:
	\[
	T(\ell_{-1})=\ell_1,\quad T(-1)=1,\quad T(\infty)=\infty,
	\]
	and
	\[
	R(s_+)=s_-,\quad R(0)=0,\quad R(1)=-1.
	\]
	Thus we can glue the sides by identifying $\ell_{-1}\sim\ell_1$ via $T$ and $s_+\sim s_-$ via $R$.
	
	\paragraph{Step 2: identify the topology of the quotient.}
	After gluing:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item the ideal vertex $\infty$ is fixed by $T$ and becomes a \emph{cusp end} (a puncture),
		\item the ideal vertices $-1$ and $1$ are identified by $T$ (also a cusp),
		\item the ideal vertex $0$ is fixed by $R$ (a cusp).
	\end{itemize}
	Hence the quotient has exactly three cusp ends. The resulting surface is connected and has Euler characteristic
	\[
	\chi = 2-3=-1,
	\]
	so it is homeomorphic to $S^2\setminus\{p,q,r\}$ (a sphere with three punctures), i.e. a \emph{pair of pants}.
	
	\paragraph{Step 3: completeness of the induced hyperbolic metric.}
	The metric on $Q$ is the restriction of the complete hyperbolic metric on $\HH$.
	Gluing along geodesic sides by isometries produces a locally isometric quotient away from the ideal vertices.
	Near each ideal vertex one may take a horocyclic neighbourhood (a standard cusp neighbourhood); since the vertices are ideal,
	the distance to the vertex is infinite, and after gluing these neighbourhoods become standard cusp ends.
	Therefore the quotient metric is complete.
	
	\paragraph{(Optional) Area check (Gauss--Bonnet / ideal polygon formula).}
	An ideal $n$-gon in $\HH$ has area $(n-2)\pi$. Here $n=4$, so
	\[
	\Area(Q)=2\pi.
	\]
	For the thrice-punctured sphere, $\chi=-1$, so Gauss--Bonnet gives
	\[
	\Area = -2\pi\chi = 2\pi,
	\]
	consistent with the construction.

	\subsubsection*{Solution (b): a compact non-orientable hyperbolic surface}
	A closed non-orientable surface of genus $k$ (connected sum of $k$ projective planes) has Euler characteristic
	\[
	\chi = 2-k.
	\]
	It admits a hyperbolic metric exactly when $\chi<0$, i.e. $k\ge 3$.
	
	\paragraph{Concrete construction (genus $3$).}
	Take a hyperbolic hexagon $P$ with interior angles
	\[
	\alpha=\frac{\pi}{3}
	\]
	(for example, a \emph{regular} hyperbolic hexagon with all sides equal and all angles $\pi/3$; such a polygon exists since
	$\alpha<\frac{(6-2)\pi}{6}=\frac{2\pi}{3}$).
	Label the sides consecutively as
	\[
	a,\ a,\ b,\ b,\ c,\ c,
	\]
	and glue each pair of equally-labeled sides by an isometry that reverses orientation along the boundary
	(so endpoints match). Because all six vertices are identified in the quotient, the total angle around the unique
	vertex is
	\[
	6\cdot \frac{\pi}{3}=2\pi,
	\]
	so the quotient has no cone singularity and inherits a smooth hyperbolic metric from $P$.
	
	The resulting surface has fundamental polygon word
	\[
	a^2\,b^2\,c^2,
	\]
	hence it is the connected sum of three projective planes: a closed non-orientable surface of genus $3$.
	Since $P$ is compact and the side pairings identify its boundary, the quotient is compact.
	
	\paragraph{Area (Gauss--Bonnet check).}
	For a closed hyperbolic surface,
	\[
	\Area = -2\pi\chi = 2\pi(k-2).
	\]
	For $k=3$ this gives $\Area=2\pi>0$, consistent with a hyperbolic metric.

	\subsection*{Deep theory: Ideal hyperbolic triangles}
	
	\subsubsection*{Definition and basic picture}
	An \emph{ideal hyperbolic triangle} is a hyperbolic triangle whose three vertices lie on the \emph{ideal boundary}
	\[
	\partial \HH^2
	\]
	(e.g.\ in the upper half–plane model $\HH=\{x+iy:y>0\}$ one has $\partial\HH=\R\cup\{\infty\}$).
	Equivalently, it is a geodesic $3$--gon in $\HH$ whose vertices are \emph{ideal points}.
	Such a triangle has:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Angles} $A=B=C=0$ (each vertex is at infinity),
		\item \textbf{Finite area} equal to $\pi$ (in curvature $-1$ normalization),
		\item \textbf{Infinite side lengths} (each side is a complete geodesic extending to the boundary),
		\item \textbf{Cusp ends} at each vertex: neighbourhoods of the vertices are \emph{cusps}.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsubsection*{Uniqueness up to isometry}
	Every ideal triangle in $\HH^2$ is congruent to every other ideal triangle.
	
	\paragraph{Reason (boundary $3$--transitivity).}
	The full orientation-preserving isometry group of $\HH$ is
	\[
	\Isom^+(\HH)\cong \PSL(2,\R),
	\]
	acting by Möbius transformations
	\[
	z\longmapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d},\qquad a,b,c,d\in\R,\ ad-bc=1.
	\]
	This action extends to the boundary $\partial\HH=\R\cup\{\infty\}$ and is \emph{$3$--transitive} on distinct boundary points:
	given two triples of distinct boundary points $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ and $(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ there exists a unique Möbius map
	sending $u_i\mapsto v_i$ for $i=1,2,3$.
	Hence any ideal triangle can be mapped by an isometry to the \emph{standard ideal triangle} with vertices
	\[
	0,\ 1,\ \infty.
	\]
	
	\subsubsection*{Standard ideal triangle in the upper half–plane}
	Let $\Delta_{\mathrm{ideal}}$ be the triangle with vertices $0,1,\infty$.
	Its sides are the geodesics:
	\[
	\gamma_{0\infty}:\ x=0,\qquad
	\gamma_{1\infty}:\ x=1,\qquad
	\gamma_{01}:\ (x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)^2,\ y>0.
	\]
	Thus it is bounded by two vertical lines and one semicircle orthogonal to $\R$.
	
	\subsubsection*{Area of an ideal triangle}
	For a hyperbolic triangle in curvature $-1$, Gauss--Bonnet gives
	\[
	\Area(\Delta)=\pi-(A+B+C).
	\]
	For an \emph{ideal} triangle $A=B=C=0$, therefore
	\[
	\Area(\Delta_{\mathrm{ideal}})=\pi.
	\]
	In particular, \emph{all} ideal triangles have area $\pi$, since they are all isometric.
	
	\subsubsection*{Horocycles and cusp geometry}
	A \emph{horocycle} in $\HH$ is a curve orthogonal to all geodesics ending at a given ideal point.
	In the upper half–plane model:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item horocycles based at $\infty$ are horizontal lines $y=\text{const}$,
		\item horocycles based at a finite boundary point $a\in\R$ are Euclidean circles tangent to $\R$ at $a$.
	\end{itemize}
	
	If you cut an ideal triangle by horocycles near its vertices, you obtain a compact hexagon region.
	A fundamental and very useful fact is:
	
	\paragraph{All ideal triangles are ``equilateral'' in horocyclic length.}
	Choose one horocycle at each ideal vertex. Then the lengths of the three arcs of these horocycles inside the ideal triangle
	are all equal (with appropriate normalization of the horocycles). This expresses the strong symmetry of ideal triangles
	even though the vertices lie at infinity.

\paragraph{Horocycles at $a=\tfrac14$ and the red geodesic side.}
Consider the ideal triangle with vertices $\{\tfrac14,\tfrac34,\infty\}$ in the upper half--plane model.
Its ``red'' geodesic side (joining $\tfrac14$ to $\tfrac34$) is the semicircle
\[
(x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\Bigl(\tfrac14\Bigr)^2,\qquad (y>0).
\]
A horocycle based at $a=\tfrac14$ is a Euclidean circle tangent to $\R$ at $\tfrac14$, hence it has equation
\[
(x-\tfrac14)^2+(y-r)^2=r^2,\qquad r>0,
\]
with centre $\bigl(\tfrac14,r\bigr)$ and radius $r$.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{Key fact.}
A horocycle based at $a=\tfrac14$ \emph{always} meets the red geodesic side
\[
(x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\Bigl(\tfrac14\Bigr)^2.
\]
Thus there is no regime in which it ``does not cross the red boundary'' (except in the degenerate limiting sense as
the horocycle shrinks to the cusp point).

\subsubsection*{1) What changes when the Euclidean radius $r$ increases?}
As $r$ increases:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the circle becomes larger;
	\item its highest point is at $y=2r$ (directly above $x=\tfrac14$);
	\item near the real axis $y=0$ it becomes flatter (its curvature decreases), so parts of the circle may lie very close to $y=0$.
\end{itemize}
Hence ``larger radius'' does not mean ``the whole arc moves upward''; rather, the circle expands and flattens near the boundary.

\subsubsection*{2) Explicit intersection with the red semicircle}
Solve simultaneously
\[
(x-\tfrac14)^2+(y-r)^2=r^2,
\qquad
(x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2=\Bigl(\tfrac14\Bigr)^2.
\]
One intersection point is the cusp endpoint $(\tfrac14,0)$.
The second intersection point is
\[
x(r)=\frac{48r^2+1}{4(16r^2+1)},
\qquad
y(r)=\frac{2r}{16r^2+1}.
\]
Its limiting behaviour is:
\[
r\to 0:\quad (x(r),y(r))\to\Bigl(\tfrac14,0\Bigr),
\qquad
r\to\infty:\quad (x(r),y(r))\to\Bigl(\tfrac34,0\Bigr).
\]
So increasing $r$ makes the visible horocycle arc extend farther across the triangle, but the intersection height $y(r)$
eventually decreases back toward $0$.

\subsubsection*{3) Why the arc looks cut in the picture (clipping to the triangle interior)}
The interior of the ideal triangle $\{\tfrac14,\tfrac34,\infty\}$ is
\[
\operatorname{int}(\Delta)
=\Bigl\{\,x+iy\in\HH:\ \tfrac14<x<\tfrac34,\ (x-\tfrac12)^2+y^2>\Bigl(\tfrac14\Bigr)^2\,\Bigr\}.
\]
Geometrically: we keep the region between the two vertical sides and \emph{outside} (above) the red semicircle.
Therefore, whenever part of the horocycle lies below the red semicircle, that part is \emph{not} in $\operatorname{int}(\Delta)$
and is clipped away; only the portion lying in the triangle interior remains visible.

	\subsubsection*{Chat-style explanation (intuition, but still precise)}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Think of an ideal triangle as a triangle whose corners are ``pushed to infinity''. Each corner becomes a \emph{cusp}.
		\item Even though the sides run off to infinity, the triangle has a \emph{finite} area: exactly $\pi$.
		\item The reason everything is so rigid is that $\PSL(2,\R)$ can move any three boundary points to any other three:
		so there is only one ideal triangle shape.
		\item Ideal triangles are the basic building blocks of finite-area hyperbolic surfaces with cusps (like the thrice-punctured sphere).
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsection*{Deep theory: Ideal hyperbolic triangles in the Poincar\'e disk model}
	
	\paragraph{The Poincar\'e disk model.}
	Let
	\[
	\DD=\{z\in\C:\ |z|<1\}.
	\]
	The Poincar\'e metric (curvature $-1$) is
	\[
	ds^2_{\hyp}=\frac{4\,|dz|^2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}.
	\]
	The boundary circle $\partial\DD=S^1=\{|z|=1\}$ is not part of the space, but it represents the \emph{ideal boundary}:
	points of $S^1$ are the \emph{ideal points} (points ``at infinity'').
	
	\paragraph{Geodesics.}
	Hyperbolic geodesics in $(\DD,d_{\hyp})$ are exactly:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Euclidean diameters of the unit circle; and
		\item Euclidean circular arcs meeting $S^1$ \emph{orthogonally}.
	\end{itemize}
	(So the correct ``straight lines'' are those arcs that hit the boundary at right angles.)
	
	\paragraph{Ideal triangles.}
	An \emph{ideal hyperbolic triangle} is a region in $\DD$ bounded by three geodesics whose endpoints lie on $S^1$,
	so its three vertices are ideal points $u,v,w\in S^1$ (pairwise distinct).
	
	\paragraph{Uniqueness up to isometry.}
	Every ideal triangle in $\H^2$ is congruent to every other one. In the disk model the orientation-preserving isometry group
	is the group of M\"obius transformations preserving $\DD$ (often written $\mathrm{PSU}(1,1)\cong \mathrm{PSL}(2,\R)$),
	and it acts $3$-transitively on $S^1$: given two ordered triples of distinct boundary points, there is a unique isometry sending
	one triple to the other. Hence \emph{there are infinitely many ideal triangles in $\DD$ as subsets, but only one up to isometry.}
	
	\paragraph{Area.}
	For a hyperbolic triangle with interior angles $A,B,C$ in curvature $-1$ one has
	\[
	\operatorname{Area}(\Delta)=\pi-(A+B+C).
	\]
	For an ideal triangle, $A=B=C=0$, so
	\[
	\operatorname{Area}(\Delta_{\mathrm{ideal}})=\pi.
	\]
	(So all ideal triangles have the same area $\pi$.)
	
	\paragraph{Horocycles (horolines).}
	In the disk model, a horocycle based at an ideal point $\xi\in S^1$ is a curve of constant distance from $\xi$,
	and it appears as a Euclidean circle \emph{tangent} to $S^1$ at $\xi$ (or, in the limiting case, a diameter/line in a suitable model).
	This is the disk analogue of ``$y=c$'' horocycles in the upper half-plane model.

\section*{E1. Constellations of circles and M\"obius transformations}

\subsubsection*{Problem statement}
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Let $A,B$ be disjoint circles in $\C$. Show that there is a M\"obius transformation which takes $A$ and $B$ to two concentric circles.
	\item A collection of circles $X_i\subset \C$, $0\le i\le n-1$, for which
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $X_i$ is tangent to $A$, $B$ and $X_{i+1}$ (indices mod $n$), and
		\item the circles are disjoint away from tangency points,
	\end{enumerate}
	is called a \emph{constellation} on $(A,B)$.
	Prove that for any $n\ge 2$ there is some pair $(A,B)$ and a constellation on $(A,B)$ made of precisely $n$ circles.
	Draw a picture illustrating your answer.
	\item Given an $n$-circle constellation $\{X_i\}$ on $(A,B)$, prove that the tangency points
	\[
	p_i:=X_i\cap X_{i+1}\qquad (0\le i\le n-1)
	\]
	all lie on a circle. Now suppose $n>2$. Prove that if $Y_0$ is \emph{any} circle tangent to $A$ and $B$, and circles
	$Y_i$ are constructed inductively so that $Y_i$ is tangent to $A$, $B$ and $Y_{i-1}$, then necessarily $Y_n=Y_0$,
	so the chain closes up to form another constellation. Does the same result hold when $n=2$?
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Theory (inversive/M\"obius geometry facts)}
\begin{itemize}
	\item A M\"obius transformation $T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}$ maps (generalized) circles/lines to (generalized) circles/lines.
	\item M\"obius transformations preserve angles and tangency: if two circles are tangent, their images are tangent.
	\item Two disjoint circles generate a \emph{coaxial family} of circles; this family has two \emph{limiting points}
	(the two points with equal power w.r.t.\ both circles). Mapping these limiting points to $0$ and $\infty$ makes the family
	concentric.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Solution (a): mapping two disjoint circles to concentric circles}
Let $A$ and $B$ be disjoint circles. Consider their coaxial family; it has two distinct limiting points $\ell_1,\ell_2$
(geometrically: the two points having equal power with respect to $A$ and $B$; they exist and are distinct since $A,B$
do not meet).
Choose a M\"obius transformation $T$ with
\[
T(\ell_1)=0,\qquad T(\ell_2)=\infty.
\]
In the $w$-plane, the coaxial family becomes the family of circles \emph{centered at $0$} (because the limiting points are
$0$ and $\infty$), hence $T(A)$ and $T(B)$ are circles with the same center. Therefore $T(A)$ and $T(B)$ are concentric.

\subsubsection*{Solution (b): existence of an $n$-circle constellation}
By part (a) we may construct examples in the concentric model and then pull back by a M\"obius map.

\paragraph{Case $n\ge 3$ (explicit concentric construction).}
Let
\[
A:\ |z|=r,\qquad B:\ |z|=R,\qquad 0<r<R,
\]
concentric circles centered at $0$. A circle $X$ lying in the annulus and tangent to both boundaries must satisfy
\[
d+s=R,\qquad d-s=r,
\]
where $d$ is the distance of the center of $X$ from the origin and $s$ is the Euclidean radius of $X$.
Hence
\[
d=\frac{R+r}{2},\qquad s=\frac{R-r}{2}.
\]
So \emph{all} circles tangent to both $A$ and $B$ have the same radius $s$ and their centers lie on the circle of radius $d$.

Now arrange $n$ such circles with centers equally spaced on the circle $|z|=d$.
Two consecutive circles are tangent iff the chord length between consecutive centers equals $2s$:
\[
2d\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{n}\Bigr)=2s
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{n}\Bigr)=\frac{s}{d}=\frac{R-r}{R+r}.
\]
Given $n\ge 3$, pick any $r>0$ and define $R$ by
\[
\frac{R-r}{R+r}=\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{n}\Bigr)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R=r\,\frac{1+\sin(\pi/n)}{1-\sin(\pi/n)}.
\]
Then the $n$ equal circles tangent to both boundaries and to their neighbors form a constellation.

\paragraph{Case $n=2$ (a concrete non-concentric example).}
Let $A,B$ be the two unit circles centered at $(-a,0)$ and $(a,0)$, with $a>1$ (so they are disjoint).
Set
\[
R=\frac{a^2-1}{2}.
\]
Then the two circles
\[
X_0:\ (x-0)^2+(y-R)^2=R^2,
\qquad
X_1:\ (x-0)^2+(y+R)^2=R^2
\]
are each tangent to both $A$ and $B$, and also tangent to each other (by symmetry, their centers are distance $2R$ apart
and their radii sum to $2R$). Hence $\{X_0,X_1\}$ is a $2$-circle constellation.

\subsubsection*{Solution (c): tangency points are concyclic; the porism for $n>2$}
Let $\{X_i\}_{i=0}^{n-1}$ be a constellation on $(A,B)$ and let $p_i=X_i\cap X_{i+1}$ be the tangency points.

\paragraph{Step 1: reduce to concentric circles.}
By (a) there is a M\"obius map $T$ sending $A,B$ to concentric circles.
Since $T$ preserves tangency, $\{T(X_i)\}$ is a constellation between concentric circles, and the tangency points
$T(p_i)$ are concyclic iff the original $p_i$ are concyclic (images of circles are circles).

\paragraph{Step 2: concentric model computation.}
Assume $A:|z|=r$, $B:|z|=R$ are concentric. Then each $X_i$ tangent to $A$ and $B$ has the \emph{same} radius
\[
s=\frac{R-r}{2},
\quad\text{and its center lies on } |z|=d,\ \ d=\frac{R+r}{2}.
\]
In any constellation, consecutive circles $X_i,X_{i+1}$ are equal-radius tangent circles, so their tangency point is the
Euclidean midpoint of their centers. If the centers are $c_i,c_{i+1}$ with $|c_i|=|c_{i+1}|=d$ and
$\angle(c_i,c_{i+1})=\alpha$ constant along the chain, then
\[
p_i=\frac{c_i+c_{i+1}}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|p_i|=\Bigl|\frac{c_i+c_{i+1}}{2}\Bigr|=d\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigr),
\]
which is independent of $i$. Hence all $p_i$ lie on the circle centered at $0$ with radius $d\cos(\alpha/2)$.
Pulling back by $T^{-1}$, the original tangency points $p_i$ are concyclic.

\paragraph{Step 3: closure for $n>2$ (porism).}
In the concentric model, a circle tangent to both boundaries is determined by the angle $\theta$ of its center on $|z|=d$.
Fix the rule ``$Y_i$ is tangent to $A,B$ and $Y_{i-1}$'' choosing consistently one direction around the annulus.
Then each step advances the center angle by a fixed increment $\alpha$ determined by the tangency condition
\[
2d\sin(\alpha/2)=2s.
\]
So the sequence of centers is $\theta_i=\theta_0+i\alpha$, and therefore
\[
Y_n=Y_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\alpha\equiv 2\pi \pmod{2\pi}.
\]
If \emph{one} $n$-cycle exists (a constellation with $n>2$), then necessarily $\alpha=2\pi/n$ (up to orientation),
and then \emph{every} starting circle closes after $n$ steps. This is the circle-chain analogue of a Poncelet-type porism.

\paragraph{What about $n=2$?}
For $n=2$ the ``next circle tangent to $A,B$ and $Y_{i-1}$'' is not rigid in the same way (there can be branching choices),
and the closure phenomenon does \emph{not} hold in general: there are pairs $(A,B)$ for which some choices yield a 2-cycle,
while other starting circles (or other choices of the next tangent circle) generate non-closing chains.

\subsection{E2. A free subgroup generated by compositions of reflections}

\subsubsection*{Problem statement}
Let $l_1,l_2,l_3$ be pairwise ultraparallel hyperbolic lines whose endpoints on
$\partial\H^2$ are cyclically ordered
\[
l_1^+,\,l_1^-,\,l_2^+,\,l_2^-,\,l_3^+,\,l_3^- .
\]
Let $r_{l}$ denote reflection in the line $l$ and define
\[
T_a=r_{l_2}\circ r_{l_1},
\qquad
T_b=r_{l_3}\circ r_{l_2}.
\]
Prove that $T_a$ and $T_b$ generate a free subgroup of the group of
orientation-preserving hyperbolic isometries.
(\emph{Hint:} if $U$ is the region bounded by the $l_i$, and $V=U\cup r_{l_2}U$, consider a tiling of the plane by copies of $V$.)

\subsubsection*{Theory}
\begin{itemize}
	\item A reflection $r_l$ is an isometry reversing orientation; it fixes $l$ pointwise and exchanges the two half-planes bounded by $l$.
	\item If $l,m$ are ultraparallel, then $r_m\circ r_l$ is a \emph{hyperbolic translation} along the unique common perpendicular of $l$ and $m$
	(with translation length twice the perpendicular distance).
	\item \textbf{Ping--pong principle (geometric version).}
	If a group acts on a set $X$ and one can find disjoint nonempty subsets $A,B\subset X$ such that
	\[
	T_a^{\pm1}(X\setminus A)\subset A,
	\qquad
	T_b^{\pm1}(X\setminus B)\subset B,
	\]
	then $\langle T_a,T_b\rangle$ is free on $\{T_a,T_b\}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Solution (tiling / ping--pong via a fundamental domain)}
Work in the Poincar\'e disk model $\DD$ (the argument is model-independent).
The cyclic ordering of endpoints means: the six ideal endpoints cut $\partial\DD$ into six disjoint arcs, and each line $l_i$
connects its own pair of endpoints without intersecting the others. This ensures that the three ultraparallel geodesics bound a
simply connected region $U$.

Reflect $U$ across $l_2$ and set
\[
V:=U\cup r_{l_2}(U).
\]
Geometrically, $V$ is a ``two-sided'' region with boundary pieces lying on $l_1,l_2,l_3$ and their reflected images across $l_2$.

Now observe:
\[
T_a=r_{l_2}\circ r_{l_1}\quad\text{maps $V$ across the side $l_1$ into an adjacent copy of $V$},
\]
and
\[
T_b=r_{l_3}\circ r_{l_2}\quad\text{maps $V$ across the side $l_3$ into another adjacent copy of $V$}.
\]
Moreover, reduced words in $T_a^{\pm1},T_b^{\pm1}$ move $V$ across successive sides without immediate cancellation; hence each reduced word
sends $V$ into a copy of $V$ that is \emph{distinct} from $V$ itself. In particular no nontrivial reduced word can fix $V$, so
no nontrivial reduced word represents the identity isometry.

Equivalently, one can package the same idea as ping--pong on $\partial\DD$:
the cyclic order gives disjoint boundary arcs (``ping--pong tables'') associated to the two translations $T_a$ and $T_b$,
and the compositions push complementary arcs strictly inside the corresponding target arcs.
Therefore $\langle T_a,T_b\rangle$ is free on $\{T_a,T_b\}$.

\subsection*{E3. Simple closed geodesics and embedded subsurfaces}

\subsubsection*{Problem statement}
Let $\Sigma$ be an abstract compact hyperbolic surface.
Let $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ be simple closed geodesics in $\Sigma$ (images of smooth embeddings $S^1\to\Sigma$
satisfying the geodesic equation everywhere).

\begin{enumerate}
	
	\item Prove that $\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2$ cannot be the boundary of an embedded cylinder in $\Sigma$
	(i.e.\ the boundary of an embedded subsurface homeomorphic to $S^1\times[0,1]$).
	\item Construct a compact hyperbolic surface $\Sigma$ and disjoint simple closed geodesics $\gamma_1,\gamma_2\subset \Sigma$
	such that $\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2$ bounds an embedded subsurface $\Sigma'\subset \Sigma$ homeomorphic to the complement
	of two disjoint disks in a torus (i.e.\ genus $1$ with two boundary components).
	\item Can this happen when $\Sigma$ has genus $2$? Briefly justify your answer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Theory}
\begin{itemize}
	\item (Uniqueness of geodesics in a free homotopy class.)
	On a hyperbolic surface, each nontrivial free homotopy class of closed curves contains a \emph{unique} closed geodesic.
	\item If two disjoint simple closed curves bound an embedded cylinder (annulus), then they are freely homotopic (indeed homotopic).
	\item Euler characteristic bookkeeping for subsurfaces with boundary:
	if $\Sigma=\Sigma'\cup \Sigma''$ glued along boundary circles (each has $\chi=0$), then
	\[
	\chi(\Sigma)=\chi(\Sigma')+\chi(\Sigma'').
	\]
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Solution (i): two distinct disjoint geodesics cannot bound an embedded cylinder}
Assume for contradiction that $\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2$ is the boundary of an embedded cylinder $C\simeq S^1\times[0,1]$.
Then $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ represent the same free homotopy class in $\Sigma$ (the two boundary components of an annulus are homotopic).
But in a hyperbolic metric, each free homotopy class contains a \emph{unique} closed geodesic.
Hence the geodesic representatives of that class must coincide, i.e.\ $\gamma_1=\gamma_2$, contradicting that they are distinct and disjoint.
Therefore no such embedded cylinder can exist.

\subsubsection*{Solution (ii): a surface where $\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2$ bounds a torus-with-two-holes}
Let $\Sigma'$ be a compact surface of genus $1$ with two boundary components (a torus with two disks removed).
Its Euler characteristic is
\[
\chi(\Sigma')=2-2\cdot 1-2=-2<0,
\]
so $\Sigma'$ admits a hyperbolic metric with \emph{geodesic boundary}.
Let its boundary components be the geodesics $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$.

Now take the \emph{double} of $\Sigma'$ along its boundary:
glue a second copy of $\Sigma'$ to the first by identifying $\gamma_1$ to $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ to $\gamma_2$.
The resulting surface $\Sigma$ is closed and hyperbolic; its genus is
\[
g(\Sigma)=2g(\Sigma')+b(\Sigma')-1=2\cdot 1+2-1=3.
\]
In $\Sigma$, the curves $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ remain disjoint simple closed geodesics, and they bound (one copy of) the subsurface $\Sigma'$
embedded in $\Sigma$ exactly as required.

\subsubsection*{Solution (iii): can this happen in genus $2$?}
If $\Sigma$ has genus $2$, then $\chi(\Sigma)=2-2\cdot 2=-2$.
If $\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2$ bounded a subsurface $\Sigma'$ homeomorphic to a torus minus two disks, then $\chi(\Sigma')=-2$ as above.
Let $\Sigma''$ be the complementary subsurface (the closure of $\Sigma\setminus \Sigma'$).
Gluing along boundary circles does not change Euler characteristic, so
\[
\chi(\Sigma)=\chi(\Sigma')+\chi(\Sigma'') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-2=-2+\chi(\Sigma'') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \chi(\Sigma'')=0.
\]
But $\Sigma''$ has \emph{two} boundary components (the same $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$), and $\chi(\Sigma'')=0$ forces $\Sigma''$ to be an annulus.
That would mean $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ bound an embedded cylinder in $\Sigma$, which is impossible by part (i).
Hence this configuration \emph{cannot} occur in genus $2$.

\subsubsection*{Why the composition of two reflections rotates by $2\varphi$}

Let $l_1$ and $l_2$ be two hyperbolic lines (geodesics) in the Poincar\'e disk model
that intersect at a point $P$, and let
\[
\varphi=\angle(l_1,l_2)\in(0,\pi)
\]
be their (hyperbolic) angle at $P$.
Since the Poincar\'e disk model is conformal, this equals the Euclidean angle at $P$.

Consider an oriented tangent ray $\rho$ based at $P$.
Let
\[
\alpha=\angle(\rho,l_1)
\]
be the signed angle from $l_1$ to $\rho$ (measured in $(-\pi,\pi)$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: reflection in $l_1$.}
Reflection $r_{l_1}$ fixes $l_1$ pointwise and reverses orientation, hence it
\emph{negates} the signed angle with $l_1$:
\[
\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_1}(\rho),l_1\bigr)=-\alpha.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: express angles with respect to $l_2$.}
Because $l_2$ is obtained from $l_1$ by rotating by $\varphi$ at $P$, we have
\[
\angle(\rho,l_2)=\alpha-\varphi,
\qquad
\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_1}(\rho),l_2\bigr)
=\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_1}(\rho),l_1\bigr)-\varphi
=-\alpha-\varphi.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: reflection in $l_2$.}
Again reflection negates the signed angle with the mirror, so
\[
\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_2}r_{l_1}(\rho),\,l_2\bigr)
=
-\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_1}(\rho),\,l_2\bigr)
=
\alpha+\varphi.
\]

Finally convert back to angles with respect to $l_1$:
\[
\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_2}r_{l_1}(\rho),\,l_1\bigr)
=
\angle\!\bigl(r_{l_2}r_{l_1}(\rho),\,l_2\bigr)
+\varphi
=
(\alpha+\varphi)+\varphi
=
\alpha+2\varphi.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.}
For every tangent direction $\rho$ at $P$, the composition $r_{l_2}\circ r_{l_1}$
adds the constant angle $2\varphi$:
\[
\alpha \longmapsto \alpha+2\varphi.
\]
Therefore $r_{l_2}\circ r_{l_1}$ is a rotation about $P=l_1\cap l_2$ by angle $2\varphi$.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Remark (answer to the confusion).}
The expression $\alpha+2\varphi$ is the \emph{final} angle of the ray with respect to $l_1$.
The \emph{rotation amount} is the difference
\[
(\alpha+2\varphi)-\alpha=2\varphi,
\]
which does \emph{not} depend on $\alpha$.
