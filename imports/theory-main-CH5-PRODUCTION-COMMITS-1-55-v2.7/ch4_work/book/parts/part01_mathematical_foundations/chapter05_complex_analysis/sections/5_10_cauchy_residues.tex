\section{Cauchy Theory, Series, and Residues}
\label{sec:ch5-cauchy-residues}

The geometric and differential ideas developed earlier culminate in a remarkable fact: for holomorphic functions, local differentiability controls global integrals. This section establishes the core results needed for the exercise bank and for later applications in waves, Green functions, and quantum theory.

\subsection{Contours and complex line integrals}
A piecewise smooth contour is a map $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb C$. For a continuous function $f$,
\[
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\gamma'(t)\,dt.
\]
Reversing orientation changes the sign, and concatenating contours adds their integrals. If $F'=f$ on a domain containing the contour, then
\[
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=F(\gamma(b))-F(\gamma(a)).
\]
Thus every closed-contour integral of a function with a primitive vanishes.

\begin{keyidea}[Cauchy--Goursat theorem]
If $f$ is holomorphic on a simply connected domain $D$ and $\gamma$ is a closed piecewise smooth contour in $D$, then
\[
\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0.
\]
The theorem is stronger than its real-variable analogue: one complex derivative is enough to force integral rigidity throughout the domain.
\end{keyidea}

\subsection{Cauchy's integral formula}
Let $f$ be holomorphic on and inside a positively oriented simple closed contour $C$, and let $z_0$ lie inside $C$. Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{f(z_0)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{z-z_0}\,dz.}
\label{eq:cauchy-integral-formula}
\end{equation}
More generally,
\[
f^{(n)}(z_0)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}\,dz.
\]
A holomorphic function is therefore infinitely differentiable and its values inside a contour are determined by boundary data.

\begin{physicalbox}[Boundary data and field reconstruction]
Cauchy's formula is an exact reconstruction law. In two-dimensional electrostatics and ideal flow, analytic potentials encode fields whose interior behavior is constrained by values on a surrounding boundary. This anticipates Green-function methods used throughout mathematical physics.
\end{physicalbox}

\subsection{Taylor and Laurent expansions}
If $f$ is holomorphic in a disk centered at $z_0$, then
\[
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-z_0)^n,
\qquad a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}.
\]
If $f$ is holomorphic only in an annulus, negative powers may be required:
\[
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-z_0)^n.
\]
The negative-power part is the principal part. An isolated singularity is removable when the principal part vanishes, a pole when it contains finitely many negative powers, and essential when it contains infinitely many.

\subsection{Residues and the residue theorem}
The residue of $f$ at an isolated singularity $z_0$ is the coefficient of $(z-z_0)^{-1}$ in its Laurent expansion:
\[
\operatorname{Res}(f;z_0)=a_{-1}.
\]
For a simple pole of $f=g/h$ with $h(z_0)=0$ and $h'(z_0)\neq0$,
\[
\operatorname{Res}(f;z_0)=\frac{g(z_0)}{h'(z_0)}.
\]

\begin{keyidea}[Residue theorem]
If $f$ is holomorphic inside and on a positively oriented contour $C$ except for isolated singularities $z_1,\ldots,z_m$ inside $C$, then
\[
\boxed{\oint_C f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_{k=1}^{m}\operatorname{Res}(f;z_k).}
\]
\end{keyidea}

The theorem reduces a global contour integral to local coefficients near isolated singularities. It is one of the main computational engines of complex analysis.

\subsection{Branch points and analytic continuation}
Functions such as $\log z$ and $z^\alpha$ are multivalued unless a branch of the argument is selected. A branch cut removes a curve from the plane so that a single-valued analytic branch can be defined. Analytic continuation extends a locally defined analytic function along paths whenever compatible overlapping expansions exist. The result may depend on topology, foreshadowing monodromy, geometric phase, and the importance of multiply connected domains.
