\section{Volume Integrals and Densities}
\label{sec:volume-integrals}

A volume integral converts a local density into a total quantity:
\[
\boxed{Q=\iiint_V\rho(\mathbf r)\,dV.}
\]
Depending on the meaning of $\rho$, $Q$ may represent mass, charge, particle number, energy, or probability.

\subsection{Coordinate volume elements}
A coordinate transformation changes the infinitesimal volume by a Jacobian. In Cartesian coordinates,
\[
dV=dx\,dy\,dz.
\]
In cylindrical and spherical coordinates,
\[
\boxed{dV=s\,ds\,d\phi\,dz,}
\qquad
\boxed{dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi.}
\]

\begin{workedexample}
\textbf{Mass of a radially varying sphere.}\par
Let a sphere of radius $R$ have density $\rho(r)=\rho_0(1-r/R)$. Then
\[
M=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^R
\rho_0\left(1-\frac rR\right)r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi
=\frac{\pi}{3}\rho_0R^3.
\]
The angular integrals contribute $4\pi$, while the radial integral records the decreasing density.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{keyidea}[Connection to quantum mechanics]
A normalized wavefunction satisfies
\[
\iiint_{\mathbb R^3}|\psi(\mathbf r,t)|^2\,dV=1.
\]
The scalar field $|\psi|^2$ is a probability density; integration converts it into the probability of finding the particle in a finite region.
\end{keyidea}
