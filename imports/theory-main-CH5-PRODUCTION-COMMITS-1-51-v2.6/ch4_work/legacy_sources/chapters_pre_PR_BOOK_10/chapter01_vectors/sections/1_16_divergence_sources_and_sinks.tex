\section{Divergence---Measuring Sources and Sinks}
\label{sec:divergence}

When studying vector fields, we naturally ask not only how individual vectors
point, but also how the field behaves collectively in the neighbourhood of a
point.

Imagine rainwater flowing across a landscape. In some regions water appears to
emerge from underground springs and spread outward. In others it converges into
drains or sinkholes and disappears. Even without tracking every droplet, we can
identify places where the flow behaves locally like a source, a sink, or a
through-flow with no net creation or removal.

The mathematical quantity that measures this behaviour is the
\term{divergence}.

\subsection{Motivation}

Consider a fluid flowing through a small region of space. If exactly as much
fluid enters as leaves, the amount of fluid inside remains unchanged. If more
fluid leaves than enters, the region behaves as though fluid were being created
inside it. If more enters than leaves, the region behaves like a sink.

Divergence measures this local balance. It answers the question:

\begin{quote}
How strongly does the vector field spread outward from this point?
\end{quote}

\subsection{Flux Through a Closed Surface}

To understand divergence geometrically, imagine surrounding a point with a tiny
closed surface. Every vector crossing that surface contributes outward,
inward, or tangential flow.

If outward flow exceeds inward flow, the point behaves as a source. If inward
flow dominates, it behaves as a sink. If the two balance, there is no net source
or sink.

\subsection{Formal Definition}

For a differentiable vector field
\[
\mathbf F(x,y,z)
=
F_x(x,y,z)\,\mathbf e_x
+
F_y(x,y,z)\,\mathbf e_y
+
F_z(x,y,z)\,\mathbf e_z,
\]
the divergence is defined by
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}
+
\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.
}
\]

Divergence transforms a vector field into a scalar field:
\[
\boxed{
\text{vector field}
\longrightarrow
\text{scalar field}.
}
\]

At every point, the result is a single number describing the local net outflow.

\subsection{Why This Formula Works}

Suppose the $x$-component of the field increases as we move in the positive
$x$-direction. Then slightly more flow leaves one face of a tiny rectangular
box than enters through the opposite face. The quantity
\[
\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
\]
measures that imbalance per unit length in the $x$-direction.

The same reasoning applies independently to the $y$- and $z$-directions.
Adding the three contributions gives the total net outward flow per unit
volume:
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}
+
\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.
\]

Thus, divergence is the local outward flux density.

\subsection{Physical Meaning of the Sign}

The sign of the divergence immediately reveals the local behaviour of the
field.

\paragraph{Positive divergence}
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F>0.
\]
More field flows outward than inward. The point behaves like a source.

\paragraph{Negative divergence}
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F<0.
\]
More field flows inward than outward. The point behaves like a sink.

\paragraph{Zero divergence}
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0.
\]
Equal amounts enter and leave. There is no net local source or sink.

Zero divergence does not mean that the field itself vanishes. A field may be
strong, curved, or rotational while still having zero net outflow.

\subsection{Worked Example 1: Uniform Expansion}

Consider
\[
\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x,y,z).
\]
Then
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\frac{\partial x}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial y}{\partial y}
+
\frac{\partial z}{\partial z}
=
1+1+1.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3.
}
\]

The divergence is positive everywhere. The field expands uniformly, with
vectors pointing away from the origin.

\subsection{Worked Example 2: Rotational Flow}

Consider the rotational field
\[
\mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x).
\]
Its divergence is
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\frac{\partial(-y)}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}
=
0+0.
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0.
}
\]

The field circulates around the origin, yet it has no local expansion or
contraction. This example demonstrates that divergence measures sources and
sinks, not rotation.

\subsection{Worked Example 3: Incompressible Fluid Flow}

For a fluid with velocity field $\mathbf v(\mathbf r,t)$, local conservation of
mass is expressed by the continuity equation
\[
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}
+
\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v)
=
0,
\]
where $\rho$ is the mass density.

For a fluid of constant density, this reduces to
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.
}
\]

Such a flow is called incompressible. The fluid may curve, accelerate, or form
vortices, but it is not locally compressed or expanded.

\subsection{Worked Example 4: Electric Charge as a Source}

One of Maxwell's equations is Gauss's law in differential form:
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E
=
\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},
}
\]
where $\mathbf E$ is the electric field and $\rho$ is the charge density.

Positive charge produces positive divergence, negative charge produces negative
divergence, and in a region with no charge,
\[
\rho=0
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0.
\]

This equation gives a direct physical interpretation of electric charge:
charge is the source of the electric field.

\subsection{Integral Interpretation}

Let $V$ be a small volume surrounding a point, and let $\partial V$ denote its
closed boundary surface. The total outward flux is
\[
\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A.
\]

Dividing by the volume and shrinking the region to the point gives
\[
\boxed{
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\lim_{V\to 0}
\frac{1}{V}
\oint_{\partial V}
\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A.
}
\]

This expression shows that divergence is the net outward flux per unit volume.

For a finite region, the same idea becomes the divergence theorem:
\[
\boxed{
\iiint_V
(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\,dV
=
\oint_{\partial V}
\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A.
}
\]

The theorem connects local information inside a volume to the total flux
through its boundary.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation}

Divergence describes the local creation, removal, expansion, or compression of
a transported quantity. It appears in:

\begin{itemize}
\item mass conservation in fluid mechanics;
\item electric fields sourced by charge;
\item gravitational fields sourced by matter;
\item heat and energy transport;
\item particle transport in plasmas;
\item probability-current conservation in quantum mechanics.
\end{itemize}

These systems differ physically, but they share the same mathematical
structure: a local balance law expressed through divergence.

\subsection{Historical Note}

The modern concept of divergence emerged from nineteenth-century work on flux,
potential theory, and vector analysis. Carl Friedrich Gauss established the
integral relation now known as the divergence theorem. Maxwell later used
divergence as one of the central operators in the compact mathematical
formulation of electromagnetism.

\subsection{Common Mistakes}

Zero divergence does not imply
\[
\mathbf F=\mathbf 0.
\]
It means only that there is no net local source or sink.

Divergence measures expansion or contraction, not rotation. A field may have
zero divergence while exhibiting strong circulation.

Divergence acts on a vector field and produces a scalar field. It should not be
confused with the gradient, which acts on a scalar field and produces a vector
field.

Finally, derivatives must be matched with their corresponding components:
\[
\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x},
\qquad
\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y},
\qquad
\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.
\]

\subsection{Summary}

Divergence measures the local net outward flux of a vector field. Positive
divergence indicates source-like behaviour, negative divergence indicates
sink-like behaviour, and zero divergence indicates no net local creation or
removal. In Cartesian coordinates,
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=
\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}
+
\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}
+
\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.
\]

Its differential and integral forms connect local field behaviour to flux
through closed surfaces.

\subsection*{Looking Ahead}

Divergence detects local expansion and contraction, but it cannot detect
rotation. A stirred fluid may circulate strongly while maintaining
\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf v=0.
\]

To describe rotational behaviour, we introduce the curl. Curl measures the
local tendency of a vector field to circulate about a point and completes the
central trio of vector-calculus operators: gradient, divergence, and curl.

\subsection*{Building Toward Advanced Physics}

Divergence supplies the language of local conservation laws. It will reappear
in Maxwell's equations, fluid dynamics, gravitation, continuum mechanics, and
quantum probability conservation. Together with gradient and curl, it forms
part of the differential framework used throughout the remainder of the book.
