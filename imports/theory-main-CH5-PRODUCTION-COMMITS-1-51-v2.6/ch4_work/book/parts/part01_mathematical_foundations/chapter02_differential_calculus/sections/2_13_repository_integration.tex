\begin{bookrule}[Canonical derivative notation]
For a map $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$, the coordinate-independent derivative
at $\mathbf a$ is written $DF(\mathbf a)$.  Its matrix in the standard bases is
$J_F(\mathbf a)$.  For a scalar field $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$, the same
linear map may be written
\[
 df_{\mathbf a}(\mathbf h)=Df(\mathbf a)[\mathbf h]
 =\nabla f(\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf h.
\]
Thus $df_{\mathbf a}$ is a covector, while $\nabla f(\mathbf a)$ is the vector
that represents it through the Euclidean inner product.
\end{bookrule}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Object & Canonical notation & Physical reading\\
\midrule
ordinary derivative & $df/dx$ or $f'(x)$ & instantaneous response\\
partial derivative & $\partial f/\partial x^i$ & coordinate response\\
total differential & $df$ & first-order change\\
Jacobian & $J_F$ & local linear transformation\\
gradient & $\nabla f$ & steepest increase / field normal\\
directional derivative & $D_{\mathbf u}f$ & response along $\mathbf u$\\
Hessian & $H_f$ & local curvature / stability\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{bookinsight}[Dependencies carried into later physics]
Chapter~\ref{ch:vector-calculus} differentiates vector fields and integrates the
resulting local quantities.  Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics use partial
and total derivatives on configuration and phase space.  Electromagnetism uses
gradients of potentials and time derivatives of fields.  Quantum mechanics
uses derivatives as momentum and kinetic-energy operators.  The notation fixed
here is therefore a repository-wide contract, not a chapter-local convention.
\end{bookinsight}
