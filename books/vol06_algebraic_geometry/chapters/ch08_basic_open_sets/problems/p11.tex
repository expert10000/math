\begin{problem}[Crossing axes and component removal]
Let
\[
R=k[x,y]/(xy).
\]
Compute $R_{\bar x}$, $R_{\bar y}$, and $R_{\bar x+\bar y}$.  Describe geometrically
$D(\bar x)$, $D(\bar y)$, and $D(\bar x+\bar y)$.
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
This is a concrete demonstration that localization can remove entire irreducible components
and can even disconnect a reducible space after an intersection point is removed.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
If $\bar x$ is invertible, $\bar x\bar y=0$ forces $\bar y=0$.  For the third localization,
construct an idempotent using $\bar x/(\bar x+\bar y)$.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
In $R_{\bar x+\bar y}$ set
\[
e=\frac{\bar x}{\bar x+\bar y}.
\]
Show $e^2=e$ and $1-e=\bar y/(\bar x+\bar y)$.

\begin{solution}
In $R_{\bar x}$, the element $\bar x$ is a unit.  Since $\bar x\bar y=0$, multiplying by
$\bar x^{-1}$ gives $\bar y=0$.  Hence
\[
R_{\bar x}\cong k[x,x^{-1}].
\]
Similarly,
\[
R_{\bar y}\cong k[y,y^{-1}].
\]
Thus $D(\bar x)$ is the $x$-axis with the origin removed, and $D(\bar y)$ is the
$y$-axis with the origin removed.

Now localize at $s=\bar x+\bar y$.  Define
\[
e=\frac{\bar x}{s},
\qquad
1-e=\frac{\bar y}{s}.
\]
Because $\bar x\bar y=0$,
\[
e(1-e)=\frac{\bar x\bar y}{s^2}=0,
\]
and since $e+(1-e)=1$, it follows that $e^2=e$.  Therefore the localized ring decomposes
by the two complementary idempotents:
\[
R_s\cong eR_s\times(1-e)R_s.
\]
On the first factor $\bar x$ is invertible and $\bar y=0$, giving
$k[x,x^{-1}]$; on the second factor $\bar y$ is invertible and $\bar x=0$, giving
$k[y,y^{-1}]$.  Hence
\[
R_{\bar x+\bar y}
\cong
k[x,x^{-1}]\times k[y,y^{-1}].
\]
Geometrically $D(\bar x+\bar y)$ is the union of both axes with the origin removed, now a
disjoint union of two punctured components.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
Inverting a function can force some equations to collapse and can expose idempotent
splittings that were not global before localization.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Generalize to the union of $r$ coordinate axes and choose a function nonzero away from their
common intersection.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
New canonical problem combining the legacy localization intuition with the retained crossing
axes geometry from earlier affine chapters.
