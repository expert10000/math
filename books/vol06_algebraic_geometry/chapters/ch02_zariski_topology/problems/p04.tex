% Problem dossier: VI.02.P4
\subsection*{Problem VI.02.P4 --- Dense integer lattices}
\label{prob:vi02-p4}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Prove that \(\mathbb Z^n\) is Zariski dense in \(\mathbb A_{\mathbb C}^n\).  More generally,
if \(S_1,\dots,S_n\subseteq k\) are infinite subsets of an infinite field \(k\), prove that
\(S_1\times\cdots\times S_n\) is dense in \(\mathbb A_k^n\).

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
Metric thinness and algebraic thinness are different.  A countable lattice can be invisible
to no nonzero polynomial and is therefore dense in the Zariski sense.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
Induct on \(n\).  View a polynomial as a polynomial in \(x_n\) whose coefficients depend on
the first \(n-1\) variables.

\paragraph{Solution.}
Suppose \(f\) vanishes on the Cartesian product and write
\[
f=\sum_{j=0}^d a_j(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})x_n^j.
\]
Fix \((s_1,\dots,s_{n-1})\) in the first \(n-1\) factors.  The resulting one-variable
polynomial vanishes at every point of the infinite set \(S_n\), so it is zero.  Therefore
all coefficient values \(a_j(s_1,\dots,s_{n-1})\) vanish.  By induction each \(a_j\) is the
zero polynomial, hence \(f=0\).  The density criterion proves the claim.  Taking every
\(S_i=\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb C\) gives the lattice statement.

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
A polynomial cannot vanish independently along infinitely many values in each coordinate
unless every coefficient vanishes.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Replace the Cartesian product by other countable sets and ask which are Zariski dense.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
New canonical problem designed for VI/02.
\fi
