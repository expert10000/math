% Challenge dossier: VI.02.C3
\subsection*{Challenge VI.02.C3 --- Closure of the cusp parametrization}
\label{chal:vi02-c3}

\paragraph{Challenge.}
Assume \(k\) is infinite and let
\[
Y=\set{(t^2,t^3):t\in k}\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2.
\]
Prove directly that
\[
\overline Y^{\,\mathrm{Zar}}=V(y^2-x^3).
\]

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\paragraph{Guided hints.}
One inclusion is immediate.  For the reverse, divide any polynomial \(f(x,y)\) modulo
\(y^2-x^3\) to reduce to
\[
a(x)+y b(x).
\]
Then substitute \((x,y)=(t^2,t^3)\) and separate even and odd powers of \(t\).

\paragraph{Solution.}
Every point of \(Y\) satisfies \(y^2-x^3=0\), so \(\overline Y\subseteq V(y^2-x^3)\).
Modulo \(y^2-x^3\), every polynomial has a representative
\[
a(x)+yb(x).
\]
Suppose such a representative vanishes on all \((t^2,t^3)\).  Then
\[
a(t^2)+t^3b(t^2)=0\qquad\text{for all }t\in k.
\]
The first term contains only even powers of \(t\); the second contains only odd powers
(starting at degree three).  Since the resulting polynomial in \(t\) vanishes for infinitely
many \(t\), it is zero, and the even and odd coefficient sets vanish separately.  Thus
\(a=b=0\).  Hence every polynomial vanishing on \(Y\) lies in \((y^2-x^3)\), so
\[
I(Y)=(y^2-x^3).
\]
The closure formula gives the claim.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Study \((t^m,t^n)\) for coprime positive integers \(m,n\).

\paragraph{Provenance.}
New challenge connecting topology with the cusp already introduced in VI/01.
\fi
