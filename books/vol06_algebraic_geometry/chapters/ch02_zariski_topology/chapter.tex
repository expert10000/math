\chapter{The Zariski Topology}
\label{ch:zariski-topology}

\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

Chapter VI/01 showed that polynomial zero loci satisfy exactly the formal closure properties
needed for closed sets of a topology.  We now make that observation structural: algebraic
sets are declared to be closed.

The resulting topology is deliberately coarse.  It is very different from the Euclidean
topology familiar from analysis, but it is adapted to polynomial equations.  Its closed
sets remember algebraic constraints, its open sets express the failure of equations, and
its closure operation records all polynomial equations satisfied by a subset.

Throughout, \(k\) is a field and
\[
\mathbb A^n_k=k^n.
\]

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item prove that algebraic subsets define a topology on affine space;
  \item compute Zariski closures using vanishing ideals;
  \item describe the induced topology on an algebraic subset;
  \item use principal open subsets as a basis;
  \item compare the Zariski and Euclidean topologies;
  \item explain why Zariski topology is \(T_1\) on \(k\)-rational affine points but
        usually not Hausdorff;
  \item work explicitly with \(\mathbb A^1_k\), especially when \(k\) is infinite.
\end{itemize}

\section{From algebraic sets to a topology}

Recall from Chapter VI/01 that for ideals \(I,J\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
V((0))=\mathbb A^n_k,\qquad V((1))=\varnothing,
\]
\[
\bigcap_{\lambda}V(I_\lambda)
=
V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right),
\]
and
\[
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]

These identities are precisely the closed-set axioms.

\begin{theorem}[Zariski topology on affine space]
\label{thm:zariski-topology-affine-space}
The collection
\[
\mathcal Z
=
\set{V(I)\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k \mid I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\text{ an ideal}}
\]
is the family of closed sets of a topology on \(\mathbb A^n_k\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The whole space and empty set belong to \(\mathcal Z\) because
\[
\mathbb A^n_k=V((0)),
\qquad
\varnothing=V((1)).
\]
Arbitrary intersections of members of \(\mathcal Z\) are algebraic:
\[
\bigcap_\lambda V(I_\lambda)
=
V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right).
\]
Finite unions are algebraic:
\[
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]
Thus \(\mathcal Z\) satisfies the axioms for closed subsets of a topology.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Zariski topology]
The topology of \cref{thm:zariski-topology-affine-space} is called the
\emph{Zariski topology} on \(\mathbb A^n_k\).
\end{definition}

A subset \(U\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) is therefore Zariski open exactly when
\[
U=\mathbb A^n_k\setminus V(I)
\]
for some ideal \(I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\).

\section{Closed sets as systems of equations}

The topology is built so that polynomial equations define closed conditions.

\begin{example}[A hypersurface is closed]
For one polynomial \(f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
V(f)
=
\set{a\in\mathbb A^n_k\mid f(a)=0}
\]
is Zariski closed.  Such a set is called a \emph{hypersurface} when the geometry has the
expected codimension.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Finite systems are closed]
For \(f_1,\dots,f_r\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
V(f_1,\dots,f_r)
=
V((f_1,\dots,f_r))
\]
is closed.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Hilbert's basis theorem implies that every Zariski closed subset of finite-dimensional affine
space can be defined by finitely many equations.  The topology itself does not require this
fact: the definition allows arbitrary ideals.
\end{remark}

\section{The induced topology on an algebraic set}

Let
\[
X\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k
\]
be algebraic.  As a subset of a topological space, \(X\) inherits the subspace topology.

\begin{definition}[Zariski topology on an affine algebraic set]
If \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) is algebraic, its \emph{Zariski topology} is the subspace
topology induced from \(\mathbb A^n_k\).
\end{definition}

Thus \(Y\subseteq X\) is closed in \(X\) exactly when there is an algebraic set
\(Z\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) with
\[
Y=X\cap Z.
\]

It is useful to write closed subsets intrinsically relative to \(X\).

\begin{definition}[Relative zero set]
For \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), define
\[
V_X(S)
:=
\set{x\in X\mid f(x)=0\text{ for every }f\in S}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:relative-zero-set}
For every \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
V_X(S)=X\cap V(S).
\]
Consequently, the closed subsets of \(X\) are exactly the sets \(V_X(S)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The equality follows directly from the definitions.  Every \(V_X(S)\) is the intersection
of \(X\) with an ambient Zariski closed set, hence is closed in \(X\).  Conversely, every
closed subset of \(X\) has the form \(X\cap Z\) with \(Z=V(S)\) algebraic.
\end{proof}

\section{Zariski closure and equations}

One of the most useful features of the topology is that closure has an exact algebraic
description.

For any subset \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\), recall its vanishing ideal
\[
I(Y)
=
\set{
f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]
\mid
f(y)=0\text{ for all }y\in Y
}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Closure formula]
\label{thm:zariski-closure-formula}
For every subset \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\),
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,\mathrm{Zar}}
=
V(I(Y)).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Chapter VI/01 proved that \(V(I(Y))\) is the smallest algebraic set containing \(Y\).
Since the algebraic sets are exactly the Zariski closed sets, this is precisely the
topological closure of \(Y\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Density criterion]
\label{cor:zariski-density}
A subset \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) is Zariski dense if and only if
\[
I(Y)=I(\mathbb A^n_k).
\]
If \(k\) is infinite, then
\[
I(\mathbb A^n_k)=(0),
\]
so \(Y\) is dense exactly when no nonzero polynomial vanishes on all of \(Y\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Density means
\[
V(I(Y))=\mathbb A^n_k.
\]
The first statement follows by applying \(I\) to the equality of closed sets.  Over an
infinite field, a polynomial vanishing on all of \(k^n\) is zero; this follows by induction
on \(n\), using the one-variable root bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[An infinite line is dense in itself]
Let
\[
L=V(y)\subseteq\mathbb A^2_k
\]
with \(k\) infinite, and let
\[
Y=\set{(t,0)\mid t\in k}.
\]
Then \(Y=L\), so it is of course dense in \(L\).  More generally, an infinite subset of an
affine line is dense in that line.
\end{example}

\section{Principal open subsets}

The complements of single hypersurfaces are especially important.

\begin{definition}[Principal open subset of affine space]
For \(f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), define
\[
D_{\mathbb A^n}(f)
:=
\mathbb A^n_k\setminus V(f)
=
\set{a\in\mathbb A^n_k\mid f(a)\neq0}.
\]
If \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) is algebraic, define
\[
D_X(f)
:=
X\setminus V_X(f).
\]
\end{definition}

The notation \(D_X(f)\) is used here only for classical affine algebraic sets.  The
scheme-theoretic distinguished opens \(D(f)\subseteq\Spec A\) are developed later in
VI/08.

\begin{proposition}[Elementary identities for principal opens]
\label{prop:principal-open-identities}
For \(f,g\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
D_X(1)=X,\qquad D_X(0)=\varnothing,
\]
and
\[
D_X(f)\cap D_X(g)=D_X(fg).
\]
Also,
\[
D_X(f)=D_X(f^m)
\]
for every integer \(m\ge1\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two statements are immediate.  A point \(x\in X\) belongs to both
\(D_X(f)\) and \(D_X(g)\) exactly when
\[
f(x)\neq0\quad\text{and}\quad g(x)\neq0.
\]
Because \(k\) is a field, this is equivalent to
\[
f(x)g(x)\neq0,
\]
hence to \(x\in D_X(fg)\).  Finally, \(f(x)\neq0\) if and only if \(f(x)^m\neq0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Principal opens form a basis]
\label{thm:principal-open-basis}
The family
\[
\set{D_X(f)\mid f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]}
\]
is a basis for the Zariski topology on an affine algebraic set \(X\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(U\subseteq X\) be open, and let \(x\in U\).  Its complement \(X\setminus U\) is
closed in \(X\), so
\[
X\setminus U=V_X(S)
\]
for some family \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\).

Since \(x\notin V_X(S)\), not every polynomial in \(S\) vanishes at \(x\).  Choose
\(f\in S\) with \(f(x)\neq0\).  Then
\[
x\in D_X(f).
\]
Moreover, if \(y\in D_X(f)\), then \(f(y)\neq0\), so \(y\notin V_X(S)=X\setminus U\).
Hence
\[
D_X(f)\subseteq U.
\]
Thus every point of every open set lies in a principal open contained in that open set.
\end{proof}

\section{Points are closed}

The Zariski topology on \(k\)-rational affine space is \(T_1\).

\begin{proposition}[Closed points in affine space]
\label{prop:affine-points-closed}
For every
\[
a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb A^n_k,
\]
the singleton \(\{a\}\) is Zariski closed:
\[
\{a\}
=
V(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The displayed equations force each coordinate \(x_i\) to equal \(a_i\), and the point
\(a\) clearly satisfies all of them.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
Every finite subset of \(\mathbb A^n_k\) is Zariski closed.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
This statement concerns the classical space of \(k\)-rational points.  In
\(\Spec A\), not every prime ideal is a closed point.  Generic points are a central feature
of scheme topology and are postponed to VI/09.
\end{remark}

\section{The affine line}

The Zariski topology becomes particularly transparent in dimension one.

\begin{theorem}[Zariski topology on the affine line over an infinite field]
\label{thm:affine-line-cofinite}
Let \(k\) be infinite.  The proper Zariski closed subsets of
\[
\mathbb A^1_k
\]
are exactly the finite subsets.  Equivalently, the nonempty Zariski open subsets are exactly
the complements of finite subsets.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A proper closed subset has the form \(V(I)\) with \(I\neq(0)\).  Choose a nonzero
\(f\in I\).  Then
\[
V(I)\subseteq V(f).
\]
A nonzero polynomial in one variable has only finitely many roots, so \(V(I)\) is finite.

Conversely, if
\[
F=\{a_1,\dots,a_r\}\subseteq k,
\]
then
\[
F
=
V\!\left(\prod_{j=1}^r(x-a_j)\right),
\]
so every finite subset is Zariski closed.
\end{proof}

Thus \(\mathbb A^1_k\) has the \emph{cofinite topology} whenever \(k\) is infinite.

\begin{corollary}[Not Hausdorff]
\label{cor:a1-not-hausdorff}
If \(k\) is infinite, \(\mathbb A^1_k\) with the Zariski topology is not Hausdorff.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Every nonempty open set has finite complement.  If \(U\) and \(V\) are nonempty open sets,
then
\[
\mathbb A^1_k\setminus(U\cap V)
=
(\mathbb A^1_k\setminus U)\cup(\mathbb A^1_k\setminus V)
\]
is finite.  Since \(k\) is infinite, \(U\cap V\neq\varnothing\).  Therefore two distinct
points cannot have disjoint open neighborhoods.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Finite fields]
If \(k\) is finite, then \(\mathbb A^1_k\) is a finite set and every singleton is Zariski
closed.  Hence every subset is closed and the topology is discrete.  This is a useful
reminder that the topology of \(k\)-rational points can depend strongly on the ground field.
\end{remark}

\section{Comparison with Euclidean topology}

When \(k=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), affine space also carries its usual Euclidean
topology.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:zariski-coarser-euclidean}
On \(\mathbb R^n\) and \(\mathbb C^n\), every Zariski closed set is Euclidean closed.
Consequently, the Zariski topology is coarser than the Euclidean topology.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every polynomial function is Euclidean continuous.  Therefore its zero set is Euclidean
closed.  An arbitrary algebraic set is an intersection of polynomial zero sets, hence is
Euclidean closed.
\end{proof}

The containment is usually strict.

\begin{example}
The Euclidean open interval
\[
(0,1)\subseteq\mathbb R=\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb R}
\]
is not Zariski open.  If it were, its complement would be Zariski closed.  But the complement
is infinite and proper, contradicting \cref{thm:affine-line-cofinite}.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The punctured affine line
\[
\mathbb A^1_k\setminus\{0\}
=
D_{\mathbb A^1}(x)
\]
is Zariski open.  Over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), it is also Euclidean open.
\end{example}

The coarse nature of the Zariski topology is a feature rather than a defect.  Euclidean
topology is designed for distance and limits; Zariski topology is designed so that
polynomial equations become geometric closed conditions.

\section{Dense subsets}

Zariski density can be surprising from a Euclidean viewpoint.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:infinite-subset-a1-dense}
Let \(k\) be infinite.  Every infinite subset of \(\mathbb A^1_k\) is Zariski dense.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A^1_k\) is infinite and its closure were proper, then by
\cref{thm:affine-line-cofinite} its closure would be finite, impossible.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}
The integers
\[
\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb C}
\]
are Zariski dense, even though they form a discrete and very small subset in the usual
Euclidean topology.
\end{example}

\begin{example}
The set
\[
\set{(t,t^2)\mid t\in k}
\subseteq
V(y-x^2)
\]
is the whole parabola, so it is dense in that curve.  If we retain only infinitely many
parameters \(t\) over an infinite field, the resulting subset is still dense in the parabola:
a polynomial condition restricting to the curve becomes a one-variable polynomial
condition in \(t\).
\end{example}

\section{Relative closure inside an algebraic set}

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k\) be algebraic and \(Y\subseteq X\).

\begin{proposition}[Closure inside \(X\)]
\label{prop:relative-closure}
The closure of \(Y\) in \(X\) is
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,X}
=
X\cap V(I(Y)).
\]
Because \(Y\subseteq X\) and \(X\) is algebraic, one in fact has
\[
V(I(Y))\subseteq X,
\]
so
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,X}=V(I(Y)).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The subspace-closure formula gives
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,X}
=
X\cap\overline{Y}^{\,\mathbb A^n_k}.
\]
Apply \cref{thm:zariski-closure-formula}.  Since \(Y\subseteq X\),
\[
I(X)\subseteq I(Y),
\]
so order reversal gives
\[
V(I(Y))\subseteq V(I(X))=X.
\]
\end{proof}

\section{A practical topology dictionary}

For an affine algebraic set \(X\), the following translations are worth memorizing:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Algebraic statement} & \text{Topological statement}\\
\hline
Y=V_X(I) & Y\text{ is closed in }X\\
U=X\setminus V_X(I) & U\text{ is open in }X\\
D_X(f)=\{x\in X:f(x)\neq0\} & \text{basic/principal open}\\
I\subseteq J & V_X(J)\subseteq V_X(I)\\
I(Y) & \text{all equations true on }Y\\
V(I(Y)) & \overline{Y}^{\,\mathrm{Zar}}\\
I(Y)=(0)\text{ over infinite }k & Y\text{ is dense in }\mathbb A^n_k
\end{array}
\]

The guiding principle is simple:

\[
\boxed{
\text{polynomial equations}
\longleftrightarrow
\text{closed geometric conditions}
}
\]

\section{Common pitfalls}

\begin{warning}[Open does not mean Euclidean open]
A Zariski open set may be extremely large.  On \(\mathbb A^1_k\) with \(k\) infinite, every
nonempty open set has finite complement.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Closed points do not imply Hausdorff]
The Zariski topology on \(k\)-rational affine space is \(T_1\), because points are closed.
It can nevertheless fail dramatically to be Hausdorff.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Do not import scheme topology too early]
The principal open \(D_X(f)\) in a classical algebraic set and the distinguished open
\(D(f)\subseteq\Spec A\) have analogous notation and behavior, but the underlying points
are different.  Scheme spectra are developed later.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Closure is algebraic closure, not metric closure]
Zariski closure records polynomial equations.  It is generally much larger than Euclidean
closure; for example, every infinite subset of \(\mathbb A^1_k\) is Zariski dense when \(k\)
is infinite.
\end{warning}

\section{Chapter summary}

The Zariski topology on affine space is defined by declaring algebraic sets to be closed.
Its construction rests on the identities
\[
\bigcap_\lambda V(I_\lambda)
=
V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right),
\qquad
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]
For every subset \(Y\),
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,\mathrm{Zar}}=V(I(Y)),
\]
so topological closure is exactly the operation of imposing all polynomial equations true on
\(Y\).

On an affine algebraic set \(X\), the sets
\[
D_X(f)=\set{x\in X\mid f(x)\neq0}
\]
form a basis of open sets.  Points of classical affine space are closed, but the topology is
usually much coarser than the Euclidean topology.  In particular, if \(k\) is infinite,
\(\mathbb A^1_k\) has the cofinite topology and is not Hausdorff.

The next chapter attaches a ring to an algebraic set.  The coordinate ring will turn
geometric questions about \(X\) into algebraic questions about polynomial functions on \(X\).
