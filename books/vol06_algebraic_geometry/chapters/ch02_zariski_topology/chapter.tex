\chapter{The Zariski Topology}
\label{ch:zariski-topology}

\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

Chapter VI/01 built algebraic subsets of affine space from polynomial equations.  The next
step is conceptual: instead of treating those subsets merely as solution sets, we declare
them to be the \emph{closed sets} of a topology.  This converts algebra into a language of
closure, density, neighborhoods, continuity, and separation.

The topology is much coarser than Euclidean topology.  That is not a defect.  Its open sets
are designed to record where polynomial conditions fail, and its closure operator records
exactly which polynomial equations a subset forces.

Throughout, \(k\) is a field and
\[
  \mathbb A_k^n=k^n.
\]
When an infinite-field hypothesis is needed, it is stated explicitly.

After this chapter the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item derive the topology axioms from identities for zero loci;
\item compute Zariski closures from vanishing ideals;
\item work with the induced topology on affine algebraic sets;
\item use principal opens \(D_X(f)\) as a basis;
\item distinguish finite-field, infinite-field, Euclidean, and product-topology behavior;
\item prove density by showing that no nonzero polynomial vanishes identically;
\item recognize determinant and minor nonvanishing as open rank conditions;
\item explain why classical affine space is \(T_1\) but usually far from Hausdorff.
\end{itemize}

\section{From equations to topology}
\label{sec:vi02-equations-topology}

For ideals \(I,J\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), Chapter VI/01 established
\[
V((0))=\mathbb A_k^n,\qquad V((1))=\varnothing,
\]
\[
\bigcap_{\lambda}V(I_\lambda)
=V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right),
\qquad
V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]
These are exactly the axioms required of closed subsets.

\begin{theorem}[Zariski topology on affine space]
\label{thm:vi02-zariski-topology}
The algebraic subsets
\[
\set{V(I)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\mid I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\text{ an ideal}}
\]
are the closed sets of a topology on \(\mathbb A_k^n\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The whole space and the empty set occur as \(V((0))\) and \(V((1))\).  Arbitrary
intersections are algebraic because they correspond to sums of ideals, and finite unions
are algebraic because they correspond to products of ideals.  These are precisely the
closed-set axioms.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Zariski topology]
The topology of \cref{thm:vi02-zariski-topology} is the \emph{Zariski topology} on
\(\mathbb A_k^n\).
\end{definition}

The main conceptual pipeline is worth keeping visible.

\section{The induced topology on an algebraic set}
\label{sec:vi02-subspace}

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) be algebraic.  Its Zariski topology is the subspace
topology.  Thus \(Y\subseteq X\) is closed precisely when
\[
Y=X\cap Z
\]
for some algebraic \(Z\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\).

\begin{definition}[Relative zero locus]
For \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), define
\[
V_X(S)=\set{x\in X\mid f(x)=0\text{ for all }f\in S}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vi02-relative-zero}
For every \(S\),
\[
V_X(S)=X\cap V(S).
\]
Consequently the closed subsets of \(X\) are exactly the relative zero loci \(V_X(S)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The equality is immediate from the definitions.  It identifies relative zero loci with
closed subsets in the subspace topology in both directions.
\end{proof}

\section{Closure is the algebraic envelope}
\label{sec:vi02-closure}

For an arbitrary subset \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), let
\[
I(Y)=\set{f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\mid f(y)=0\text{ for every }y\in Y}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Closure formula]
\label{thm:vi02-closure-formula}
For every \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\),
\[
\overline{Y}^{\,\mathrm{Zar}}=V(I(Y)).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Chapter VI/01 showed that \(V(I(Y))\) is the smallest algebraic subset containing \(Y\).
The algebraic subsets are now exactly the closed subsets, so this smallest algebraic set is
precisely the topological closure.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Density criterion]
\label{cor:vi02-density}
A subset \(Y\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) is Zariski dense if and only if
\[
I(Y)=I(\mathbb A_k^n).
\]
If \(k\) is infinite, then \(I(\mathbb A_k^n)=(0)\), so density is equivalent to:
\[
\text{no nonzero polynomial vanishes at every point of }Y.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Density means \(V(I(Y))=\mathbb A_k^n\).  Over an infinite field, a polynomial that
vanishes on all of \(k^n\) is zero; prove this by induction on \(n\), using the
one-variable root bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A nonzero principal open is dense]
\label{prop:vi02-principal-dense}
Assume \(k\) is infinite.  If \(0\neq f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), then
\[
D(f)=\set{a\in\mathbb A_k^n\mid f(a)\neq0}
\]
is Zariski dense in \(\mathbb A_k^n\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(g\) vanishes on \(D(f)\).  Then \(fg\) vanishes at every point of
\(\mathbb A_k^n\): on \(D(f)\) because \(g=0\), and on \(V(f)\) because \(f=0\).
Since \(k\) is infinite, \(fg\) is the zero polynomial.  The polynomial ring over a field
is a domain and \(f\neq0\), so \(g=0\).  Hence \(I(D(f))=(0)\), and
\cref{cor:vi02-density} gives density.
\end{proof}

\section{Principal opens form a basis}
\label{sec:vi02-principal-opens}

\begin{definition}[Principal open]
For \(f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) and algebraic \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), set
\[
D_X(f)=X\setminus V_X(f)=\set{x\in X\mid f(x)\neq0}.
\]
\end{definition}

The notation here concerns classical affine algebraic sets.  The scheme-theoretic
\(D(f)\subseteq\Spec A\) is treated later in VI/08.

\begin{proposition}[Principal-open calculus]
\label{prop:vi02-principal-calculus}
For \(f,g\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
D_X(1)=X,\qquad D_X(0)=\varnothing,
\]
\[
D_X(f)\cap D_X(g)=D_X(fg),
\qquad
D_X(f)=D_X(f^m)\quad(m\ge1).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
All statements follow by evaluating at a point and using that a field has no zero divisors
and no nonzero nilpotents.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Principal opens are a basis]
\label{thm:vi02-principal-basis}
The family \(\set{D_X(f)}\) is a basis for the Zariski topology on an affine algebraic set
\(X\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(U\subseteq X\) be open and \(x\in U\).  Write
\[
X\setminus U=V_X(S).
\]
Since \(x\notin V_X(S)\), some \(f\in S\) satisfies \(f(x)\neq0\).  Then
\[
x\in D_X(f)\subseteq U.
\]
Thus every point of every open set lies in a principal open contained in that open set.
\end{proof}

\section{Closed points and separation}
\label{sec:vi02-separation}

\begin{proposition}[Classical affine points are closed]
For \(a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\mathbb A_k^n\),
\[
\set{a}=V(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n).
\]
Hence every finite subset is closed and classical affine space is \(T_1\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This is a statement about \(k\)-rational points.  Later, \(\Spec A\) will contain nonclosed
prime ideals and generic points.  Do not import that scheme-theoretic behavior into this
classical chapter.
\end{remark}

\section{The affine line: infinite versus finite fields}
\label{sec:vi02-affine-line}

\begin{theorem}[Infinite field: cofinite topology]
\label{thm:vi02-a1-cofinite}
If \(k\) is infinite, the proper Zariski closed subsets of \(\mathbb A_k^1\) are exactly the
finite subsets.  Equivalently, every nonempty open set has finite complement.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If \(V(I)\neq\mathbb A_k^1\), choose \(0\neq f\in I\).  Then \(V(I)\subseteq V(f)\),
and a nonzero one-variable polynomial has finitely many roots.  Conversely, every finite
set \(\set{a_1,\dots,a_r}\) is
\[
V\!\left(\prod_{j=1}^r(x-a_j)\right).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
If \(k\) is infinite, \(\mathbb A_k^1\) is \(T_1\) but not Hausdorff, and every infinite
subset is dense.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Two nonempty cofinite open subsets must intersect, so distinct points cannot have disjoint
open neighborhoods.  An infinite subset cannot lie in a proper closed subset because all
proper closed subsets are finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Finite field: discrete topology]
If \(k\) is finite, the Zariski topology on the finite set \(\mathbb A_k^n=k^n\) is discrete.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every singleton is closed, hence every subset, being a finite union of singletons, is
closed.  Therefore every subset is also open.
\end{proof}

\section{Euclidean topology versus Zariski topology}
\label{sec:vi02-euclidean}

For \(k=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), polynomial functions are Euclidean continuous, so
zero loci are Euclidean closed.

\begin{proposition}
Every Zariski closed subset of \(k^n\), for \(k=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), is Euclidean
closed.  Thus the Zariski topology is coarser than the Euclidean topology.
\end{proposition}

The inclusion is strict.  For instance \(\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb C\) is Euclidean closed
but Zariski dense in \(\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb C}\).

The lesson is structural: Euclidean closure measures metric limiting behavior, whereas
Zariski closure measures polynomial identities.

\section{The product topology is not the affine-plane Zariski topology}
\label{sec:vi02-product-topology}

This distinction is subtle enough to deserve its own picture.  Assume here that \(k\) is
infinite.  Give each copy of \(\mathbb A_k^1\) its cofinite Zariski topology and form the
ordinary product topology on the set \(\mathbb A_k^1\times\mathbb A_k^1\).

A proper closed subset in the product topology is contained in a finite union of vertical
and horizontal lines; after removing any whole such lines, only finitely many points remain.
By contrast, the Zariski topology on \(\mathbb A_k^2\) contains arbitrary plane algebraic
curves as closed sets.

\begin{proposition}[Strict comparison]
Assume \(k\) is infinite.  The product topology on
\(\mathbb A_k^1\times\mathbb A_k^1\) is strictly coarser than the Zariski topology on
\(\mathbb A_k^2\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A basic product open has the form \(U\times V\), where \(U\) and \(V\) are cofinite.
Write
\[
U=D(f(x)),\qquad V=D(g(y))
\]
for suitable one-variable polynomials.  Then
\[
U\times V=D(f(x)g(y)),
\]
which is Zariski open in \(\mathbb A_k^2\).  Thus the product topology is no finer.
It is strictly coarser because the diagonal
\[
\Delta=V(y-x)
\]
is Zariski closed, while its complement is not product open: any product neighborhood of a
point off the diagonal contains a diagonal point, since two cofinite subsets of an infinite
set intersect.
\end{proof}

\section{Rank conditions are topological conditions}
\label{sec:vi02-rank}

Identify the affine space \(\mathbb A_k^{mn}\) with the set of \(m\times n\) matrices.
Determinants and minors are polynomial functions in the matrix entries.

\begin{proposition}[Rank loci]
Fix \(r\).  The set
\[
\set{M\mid \rank M\le r}
\]
is Zariski closed, because it is the common zero locus of all \((r+1)\times(r+1)\)
minors.  Therefore
\[
\set{M\mid \rank M\ge r+1}
\]
is Zariski open.
\end{proposition}

For square matrices this gives
\[
GL_n(k)=D(\det).
\]
Over an infinite field, \(\det\) is a nonzero polynomial, so \cref{prop:vi02-principal-dense}
shows that invertible matrices are dense in the space of all matrices.

\section{Common pitfalls}
\label{sec:vi02-pitfalls}

\begin{warning}[Do not visualize Zariski opens as small balls]
A basic Zariski open is generally huge.  In \(\mathbb A_k^1\) over an infinite field it is
all but finitely many points.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Finite fields behave differently]
Statements such as ``every infinite subset of \(\mathbb A^1\) is dense'' presuppose an
infinite field.  Over a finite field the classical rational-point topology is discrete.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Product topology is a different construction]
Although the underlying set \(\mathbb A^2\) equals \(\mathbb A^1\times\mathbb A^1\), the
Zariski topology on the affine plane is not, over an infinite field, the product of the
Zariski topologies on the two affine lines.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Classical \(D_X(f)\) versus scheme \(D(f)\)]
The notation looks identical because the ideas are related, but scheme distinguished opens
carry ring-theoretic information and belong to VI/08.
\end{warning}

\section{Chapter summary}
\label{sec:vi02-summary}

The Zariski topology turns polynomial equations into topology:
\[
\text{closed}=V(I),
\qquad
\text{basic open}=D_X(f),
\qquad
\overline Y=V(I(Y)).
\]
Over an infinite field, density can be tested by the absence of nonzero polynomial
relations.  This makes infinite subsets of the affine line dense, makes nonzero principal
opens dense in affine space, and makes invertibility or full-rank conditions naturally open
and often dense.

The topology depends strongly on context.  The affine line over an infinite field is
cofinite and non-Hausdorff; over a finite field the rational-point topology is discrete;
and the Zariski topology on \(\mathbb A^2\) is strictly finer than the product of the two
cofinite affine-line topologies.  These contrasts are not anomalies: they are the first
sign that algebraic geometry organizes spaces by polynomial relations rather than metric
proximity.
