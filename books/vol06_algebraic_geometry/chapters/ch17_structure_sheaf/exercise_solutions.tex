\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.1]
Since $1$ belongs to no prime ideal, $D(1)=\Spec A=X$.  The principal-open formula therefore gives
\[
\OO_X(X)=\OO_X(D(1))\cong A_1.
\]
Localizing at the multiplicative set $\{1,1^2,\dots\}$ changes nothing: the map $A\to A_1$, $a\mapsto a/1$, is an isomorphism with inverse $a/1^m\mapsto a$.  Hence the canonical global-section map is
\[
A\xrightarrow{\sim}\Gamma(X,\OO_X).
\]
Under this isomorphism, $a\in A$ is the section whose germ at every $\mfp$ is $a/1\in A_{\mfp}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.2]
For any element $f$, the basic open depends only on the radical of $(f)$.  In particular
\[
D(x^3)=\{\mfp:x^3\notin\mfp\}=\{\mfp:x\notin\mfp\}=D(x),
\]
because a prime contains $x^3$ exactly when it contains $x$.
The principal-open formula gives
\[
\OO_X(D(x^3))=k[x]_{x^3}.
\]
If $x^3$ is invertible then $x$ is invertible, since $x^{-1}=x^2(x^3)^{-1}$; conversely, if $x$ is invertible then so is $x^3$.  Thus
\[
k[x]_{x^3}=k[x]_x=k[x,x^{-1}],
\]
so the two open sets and their section rings agree canonically.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.3]
The inclusion $D(xy)\subseteq D(x)$ corresponds to making one additional element, namely $y$, invertible.  Hence the restriction map is
\[
\rho_{D(x),D(xy)}:A_x\longrightarrow (A_x)_y\cong A_{xy}.
\]
Explicitly,
\[
\frac{p(x,y)}{x^m}\longmapsto \frac{p(x,y)}{x^m},
\]
where the same fraction is now viewed in the larger localization in which both $x$ and $y$ are units.  Equivalently one may rewrite it as
\[
\frac{p}{x^m}=\frac{p y^n}{x^m y^n}
\]
for any $n\ge0$.  Functoriality follows from the universal property of localization.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.4]
For every $f\notin\mfp$ there is a canonical localization map $A_f\to A_{\mfp}$, because every power of $f$ lies outside $\mfp$ and is therefore invertible in $A_{\mfp}$.  These maps are compatible as $f$ varies, so they induce
\[
\Phi:\varinjlim_{f\notin\mfp}A_f\longrightarrow A_{\mfp}.
\]
It is surjective: every element of $A_{\mfp}$ has the form $a/s$ with $s\notin\mfp$, and this element already comes from $A_s$.
For injectivity, suppose elements represented by $a/f^r\in A_f$ and $b/g^s\in A_g$ have the same image in $A_{\mfp}$.  The localization equality criterion yields some $u\notin\mfp$ with
\[
u(g^s a-f^r b)=0.
\]
After passing to the common principal neighborhood indexed by $fgu$, the two fractions are equal.  Therefore they represent the same element of the colimit.  Hence $\Phi$ is an isomorphism.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.5]
An element of $A_{\mfp}$ has the form $a/s$ with $s\notin\mfp$.  If $a\notin\mfp$, then $a$ is also inverted in $A_{\mfp}$ and
\[
\frac{s}{a}\cdot\frac{a}{s}=1,
\]
so $a/s$ is a unit.
If $a\in\mfp$, then $a/s$ lies in the extended ideal $\mfp A_{\mfp}$.  It cannot be a unit: otherwise $1$ would belong to the proper ideal $\mfp A_{\mfp}$.  Thus the nonunits are exactly the elements of $\mfp A_{\mfp}$.  In any ring the set of nonunits is a maximal ideal when it is an ideal, so $A_{\mfp}$ is local with unique maximal ideal $\mfp A_{\mfp}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.6]
By the stalk computation,
\[
\kappa(\mfp)=A_{\mfp}/\mfp A_{\mfp}.
\]
Localization commutes with quotient, hence
\[
A_{\mfp}/\mfp A_{\mfp}\cong (A/\mfp)_{S},
\]
where $S$ is the image of $A\setminus\mfp$ in $A/\mfp$.  Because $\mfp$ is prime, $A/\mfp$ is a domain, and $S$ is precisely its set of nonzero elements.  Localizing a domain at all nonzero elements gives its fraction field.  Therefore
\[
\kappa(\mfp)\cong\Frac(A/\mfp).
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.7]
If $A$ is a domain, $(0)$ is a prime ideal and
\[
A\setminus(0)=A\setminus\{0\}.
\]
Thus $A_{(0)}$ is obtained by inverting every nonzero element of $A$, which is exactly the construction of $\Frac(A)$.  The stalk formula therefore gives
\[
\OO_{X,(0)}\cong A_{(0)}=\Frac(A).
\]
This explains geometrically why the function field occurs at the generic point.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.8]
Let $\mfm=(x-a)$.  The stalk is
\[
\OO_{X,\mfm}=k[x]_{(x-a)},
\]
the ring of rational functions $p(x)/q(x)$ whose denominator does not vanish at $a$.  Its unique maximal ideal is generated by $x-a$.
Taking the quotient gives
\[
k[x]_{(x-a)}/(x-a)k[x]_{(x-a)}\cong k[x]/(x-a)\cong k,
\]
where the last map is evaluation at $a$.  Hence $\kappa(\mfm)=k$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.9]
At the prime $(p)$ one inverts every integer not divisible by $p$, so
\[
\OO_{\Spec\mathbb Z,(p)}=\mathbb Z_{(p)}
=\left\{\frac ab\in\mathbb Q:p\nmid b\right\}.
\]
Its maximal ideal is $p\mathbb Z_{(p)}$.  Reduction modulo this ideal sends $a/b$ to
\[
\overline a\,\overline b^{-1}\in\mathbb F_p,
\]
which is well defined because $p\nmid b$.  This gives
\[
\kappa(p)=\mathbb Z_{(p)}/p\mathbb Z_{(p)}\cong\mathbb F_p.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.10]
The principal-open formula gives
\[
\OO_{\Spec\mathbb Z}(D(30))=\mathbb Z_{30}=\mathbb Z[1/30].
\]
Since $30=2\cdot3\cdot5$, inverting $30$ makes each of $2,3,5$ invertible; conversely, if these three primes are invertible then so is $30$.  Hence
\[
\mathbb Z[1/30]=\mathbb Z[1/2,1/3,1/5].
\]
As a subring of $\mathbb Q$, it consists exactly of fractions whose reduced denominator has no prime factors other than $2,3,5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.11]
If $f$ is nilpotent, say $f^N=0$, then every prime ideal contains $f$: since $f^N\in\mfp$ and $\mfp$ is prime, $f\in\mfp$.  Thus no prime lies in $D(f)$ and
\[
D(f)=\varnothing.
\]
In $A_f$, the element $f$ is a unit.  But $f^N=0$, so multiplying by $f^{-N}$ gives $1=0$.  Therefore $A_f$ is the zero ring, in agreement with the fact that the empty open has the zero ring of sections in this convention.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.12]
At a point $\mfp$, the germ of the global section $a$ is $a/1\in A_{\mfp}$.  By the unit criterion for a localization at a prime, $a/1$ is invertible exactly when $a\notin\mfp$.  Therefore the set on which $a$ is a unit is
\[
\{\mfp\in\Spec A:a\notin\mfp\}=D(a).
\]
Thus principal opens can be characterized intrinsically as unit loci of global functions.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.13]
The multiplicative set defining $A_{\mfp}$ is $S=A\setminus\mfp$.  The standard zero criterion for localization says
\[
\frac a1=0\text{ in }S^{-1}A
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists s\in S\text{ such that }sa=0\text{ in }A.
\]
Substituting $S=A\setminus\mfp$ gives exactly
\[
\frac a1=0\text{ in }A_{\mfp}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists s\notin\mfp\text{ with }sa=0.
\]
This criterion is important when $A$ has zero divisors: membership $a\in\mfp$ alone does not imply that the germ vanishes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.14]
Both spectra have one point.  Indeed, $\Spec k$ has the zero prime, while every prime of $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ contains the nilpotent $\varepsilon$, so the unique prime is $(\varepsilon)$.  Hence the underlying topological spaces are homeomorphic.
Their structure sheaves are different.  On the unique nonempty open, the first has section ring $k$, whereas the second has section ring
\[
k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2).
\]
The latter contains the nonzero nilpotent class of $\varepsilon$, while $k$ is reduced.  Therefore the two rings are not isomorphic, so the corresponding ringed spaces are not isomorphic.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.15]
Let $A=k\times k$, with idempotents $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$.  The spectrum consists of two points, one from each factor, and both points are open and closed.  Thus the open subsets are
\[
\varnothing,\quad U_1,\quad U_2,\quad X=U_1\sqcup U_2.
\]
Localization gives
\[
\OO_X(U_1)=A_{e_1}\cong k,
\qquad
\OO_X(U_2)=A_{e_2}\cong k.
\]
By gluing on the disjoint union,
\[
\OO_X(X)\cong k\times k,
\qquad
\OO_X(\varnothing)=0.
\]
The restriction maps $k\times k\to k$ are the two coordinate projections.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.16]
Restriction to the two members of the cover defines
\[
\Phi:\OO_X(D(f)\cup D(g))\longrightarrow A_f\times A_g.
\]
The two restrictions of a global section agree on the overlap
\[
D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg),
\]
so the image of $\Phi$ lies in the fiber product $A_f\times_{A_{fg}}A_g$.
Conversely, a pair $(u,v)$ in this fiber product is a pair of sections on $D(f)$ and $D(g)$ whose restrictions to $D(fg)$ agree.  The sheaf gluing axiom gives a unique section on the union restricting to $(u,v)$.  These constructions are inverse, so
\[
\OO_X(D(f)\cup D(g))\cong A_f\times_{A_{fg}}A_g.
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.17]
In $A=k[x,y]/(xy)$ the product $xy$ is zero, so
\[
D(x)\cap D(y)=D(xy)=D(0)=\varnothing.
\]
Hence the two principal opens are disjoint.  Sections on their union are therefore independent pairs:
\[
\OO_X(D(x)\cup D(y))\cong A_x\times A_y.
\]
In $A_x$, the element $x$ is invertible and $xy=0$, so $y=0$; hence
\[
A_x\cong k[x,x^{-1}].
\]
Similarly
\[
A_y\cong k[y,y^{-1}].
\]
Thus
\[
\OO_X(D(x)\cup D(y))\cong k[x,x^{-1}]\times k[y,y^{-1}].
\]
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.18]
Write the given element as
\[
\alpha=\frac as\in A_{\mfp}
\]
with $s\notin\mfp$.  Then $\mfp\in D(s)$.  The fraction $a/s$ is an element of $A_s$, hence by the principal-open theorem determines a section of $\OO_X(D(s))$.  Its germ at $\mfp$ is precisely the original element $\alpha$.  Thus every stalk element is represented by a section on some principal neighborhood.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.19]
Assume $s,t\notin\mfp$.  The two fractions have the same germ exactly when they are equal in $A_{\mfp}$:
\[
\frac as=\frac bt\quad\text{in }A_{\mfp}.
\]
By the equality criterion for localization, this is equivalent to the existence of some $u\notin\mfp$ such that
\[
u(at-bs)=0\quad\text{in }A.
\]
Equivalently, after shrinking to the principal neighborhood $D(ust)$, the two fractions represent the same regular section.  This algebraic condition is the precise meaning of equality of germs.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.20]
At the generic point $(0)$ the residue field is $\mathbb Q$, so the global integer $n$ has value
\[
n(0)=n\in\mathbb Q.
\]
At the closed point $(p)$ the residue field is $\mathbb F_p$, and the residue map sends the germ $n/1\in\mathbb Z_{(p)}$ to
\[
n(p)=\overline n\in\mathbb F_p.
\]
Thus one global section has values in different fields at different points: rationally at the generic point and modulo $p$ at the closed point.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.21]
Let $A=k[x,y]/(xy)$.  At the minimal prime $(x)$, every element outside $(x)$ becomes invertible; in particular $y$ is invertible.  Since $xy=0$, invertibility of $y$ forces $x=0$ after localization.  Therefore
\[
A_{(x)}\cong k[y]_{(0)}=k(y),
\]
because localizing the domain $k[y]$ at its zero prime gives its fraction field.
Similarly, at the minimal prime $(y)$ one obtains
\[
A_{(y)}\cong k[x]_{(0)}=k(x).
\]
These are the function fields of the two irreducible components.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.22]
Let $N=\Nil(A)$ and write $A_{\mathrm{red}}=A/N$.  Every prime $\mfp$ contains $N$, so it corresponds to the prime $\overline\mfp=\mfp/N$ of $A/N$.  On stalks, the quotient map induces
\[
A_{\mfp}\longrightarrow (A/N)_{\overline\mfp}.
\]
Localization commutes with quotient, giving a canonical isomorphism
\[
(A/N)_{\overline\mfp}\cong A_{\mfp}/N_{\mfp}.
\]
Thus the stalk map is the quotient by the localized nilradical.  Reduction leaves the topological point unchanged but removes all nilpotent germs.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.23]
Let $u\in A_f$ and $v\in A_g$ have the same image in $A_{fg}$.  By the principal-open theorem they are sections on $D(f)$ and $D(g)$, and equality in $A_{fg}$ says precisely that their restrictions agree on the overlap $D(fg)$.  Since $D(f)\cup D(g)=X$, the sheaf gluing axiom yields a unique
\[
s\in\OO_X(X)
\]
restricting to $u$ and $v$.  The global-section isomorphism $A\cong\OO_X(X)$ provides a unique $a\in A$ corresponding to $s$.  Its images in $A_f$ and $A_g$ are exactly $u$ and $v$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.24]
Let $s=(s_{\mfp})_{\mfp\in U}$ be locally represented by fractions and let $W\subseteq U$ be open.  Fix $\mfp\in W$.  By hypothesis there is a neighborhood $V\subseteq U$ of $\mfp$ and a fraction $a/b$ representing $s$ on $V$, with $V\subseteq D(b)$.  Then
\[
V\cap W
\]
is a neighborhood of $\mfp$ inside $W$, it is still contained in $D(b)$, and the restriction of $s$ is represented there by the same fraction $a/b$.  Since this works at every point of $W$, the restricted family is again locally representable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.25]
If two sections are equal, then their germs are equal at every point by functoriality of taking germs.
Conversely, suppose $s,t\in\OO_X(U)$ have the same germ at every $\mfp\in U$.  Equality of germs means that for each $\mfp$ there exists an open neighborhood $V_{\mfp}\subseteq U$ on which
\[
s|_{V_{\mfp}}=t|_{V_{\mfp}}.
\]
The neighborhoods $V_{\mfp}$ cover $U$.  By the locality part of the sheaf axiom, sections agreeing on an open cover are equal.  Hence $s=t$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}[Exercise VI.17.26]
The equality of section rings
\[
\OO_X(D(f))\cong A_f
\]
is not by itself an isomorphism of affine schemes.  One must additionally prove that the locally ringed space obtained by restricting $(X,\OO_X)$ to $D(f)$ is canonically isomorphic to
\[
(\Spec A_f,\OO_{\Spec A_f}).
\]
Concretely, this requires: first, a homeomorphism
\[
\Spec A_f\xrightarrow{\sim}D(f),
\]
identifying primes of $A_f$ with primes of $A$ avoiding $f$; second, compatible identifications of the two structure sheaves on a basis of principal opens; and third, verification that the induced stalk maps are local-ring isomorphisms.  Those are exactly the additional assertions developed in VI/18.
\end{solution}
