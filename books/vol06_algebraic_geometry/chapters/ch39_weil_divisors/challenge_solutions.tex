\subsection*{Challenge VI.39.C1 solution}
% VI39-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-01
\begin{solution}
Let \(C\) be a normal projective integral curve and let \(f\in K(C)^\times\) be nonconstant.  Normality of a one-dimensional Noetherian scheme implies regularity, so the rational map defined by \(f\) extends across every closed point to a morphism
\[
 \varphi_f:C\longrightarrow\mathbb P^1_k.
\]
Because \(f\) is nonconstant, \(\varphi_f\) is dominant.  A dominant morphism between proper integral curves is finite.

The scheme-theoretic fiber over \(0\) is
\[
 \varphi_f^*(0)=\sum_{P\mapsto0} e_P[P],
\]
where \(e_P=\operatorname{ord}_P(f)>0\).  Similarly
\[
 \varphi_f^*(\infty)=\sum_{P\mapsto\infty} e_P[P],
\]
where now \(e_P=-\operatorname{ord}_P(f)>0\).  Thus
\[
 \operatorname{div}(f)=\varphi_f^*(0)-\varphi_f^*(\infty).
\]
For a finite morphism of proper integral curves, every scheme-theoretic fiber has the same weighted degree, namely the degree of the finite map:
\[
 \deg\varphi_f^*(Q)=[K(C):k(f)]\,[\kappa(Q):k]
\]
for rational \(Q\), and in particular the fibers over \(0\) and \(\infty\) have equal degree.  Hence
\[
 \deg\operatorname{div}(f)=0.
\]
The constant case gives the zero divisor immediately.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge VI.39.C2 solution}
% VI39-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-02
\begin{solution}
Let \(A\) be a Noetherian normal domain with fraction field \(K\), and let \(\mathfrak p\subset A\) have height one.  Then \(A_{\mathfrak p}\) is a one-dimensional Noetherian local integrally closed domain, hence a DVR.  Choose a uniformizer \(\pi_{\mathfrak p}\).  Every \(f\in K^\times\) has a unique expression
\[
 f=u\pi_{\mathfrak p}^{n},\qquad
 u\in A_{\mathfrak p}^{\times},\quad n\in\mathbb Z,
\]
and we define
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak p}(f)=n.
\]
The value is independent of the chosen uniformizer because any two uniformizers differ by a unit.

To reconstruct the divisor, set
\[
 \operatorname{div}(f)
 =\sum_{\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=1}
   \operatorname{ord}_{\mathfrak p}(f)[V(\mathfrak p)].
\]
Only finitely many terms are nonzero.  If \(f=a/b\) with \(a,b\in A\setminus\{0\}\), then a nonzero order can occur only at height-one primes containing \(a\) or \(b\).  In a Noetherian ring, the height-one primes minimal over the principal ideals \((a)\) and \((b)\) form a finite set.  The valuation laws give
\[
 \operatorname{div}(fg)=\operatorname{div}(f)+\operatorname{div}(g).
\]
Thus the geometric construction on \(\operatorname{Spec}A\) is exactly the height-one valuation theory of the normal domain \(A\).
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge VI.39.C3 solution}
% VI39-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-03
\begin{solution}
Let
\[
 A=k[x,y,z]/(xy-z^2),\qquad P=(x,z).
\]
The ideal \(P\) is height one, hence defines a prime Weil divisor.  It is not principal at the vertex \(\mathfrak m=(x,y,z)\).  Localize at \(\mathfrak m\).  If \(P_{\mathfrak m}\) were principal, then the \(k\)-vector space
\[
 P_{\mathfrak m}/\mathfrak mP_{\mathfrak m}
\]
would have dimension one.  But the classes of \(x\) and \(z\) are linearly independent there: \(\mathfrak mP\) is generated by products of positive-degree elements and therefore contains no nonzero degree-one linear combination of \(x\) and \(z\).  Hence the displayed quotient has dimension two.  By Nakayama's lemma, \(P_{\mathfrak m}\) requires two generators and is not principal.

On the other hand, the codimension-one valuation calculation gives
\[
 \operatorname{div}(x)=2[P].
\]
Thus twice the Weil divisor \([P]\) is principal while \([P]\) itself is not locally principal at the singular point.  This is exactly the pattern later encoded by the order-two class of \([P]\) in the divisor class group of the quadric cone.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge VI.39.C4 solution}
% VI39-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-04
\begin{solution}
Write the boundary as a union of its distinct prime-divisor components
\[
 B=D_1\cup\cdots\cup D_r
\]
plus any components of codimension at least two.  Restriction
\[
 \rho:\operatorname{Div}(X)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Div}(U)
\]
sends a prime divisor \(Z\) meeting \(U\) to \(Z\cap U\), and sends each \(D_i\subset X\setminus U\) to zero.  Distinct prime divisors of \(X\) meeting \(U\) remain distinct on \(U\), and every prime divisor of \(U\) closes up to a unique prime divisor of \(X\) not contained in the boundary.  Therefore
\[
 \ker\rho=\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\mathbb Z[D_i].
\]
Codimension-at-least-two boundary pieces contribute nothing because they are not Weil-divisor basis elements.

For \(f\in K(X)^\times=K(U)^\times\), compatibility with restriction says
\[
 \operatorname{div}_U(f)=\rho(\operatorname{div}_X(f)).
\]
Hence the difference between the two visible principal divisors is exactly the sum of the zero/pole components supported on the boundary.  On \(\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb A^1\), for example, the pole term \(-[\infty]\) is deleted.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge VI.39.C5 solution}
% VI39-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-05
\begin{solution}
Let Cartier divisors \(D\) and \(E\) be represented on an open cover by rational functions \(f_i\) and \(g_i\).  Their sum is represented by \(f_ig_i\).  At a prime divisor \(Z\), choose an index \(i\) whose open set contains \(\eta_Z\).  The associated Weil coefficient is additive because
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z(f_ig_i)
 =\operatorname{ord}_Z(f_i)+\operatorname{ord}_Z(g_i).
\]
Changing either local equation by a regular unit does not change its order.  Therefore the Cartier-to-Weil construction is a group homomorphism.

A prime Weil divisor \(Z\) comes from an effective Cartier divisor precisely when its ideal sheaf is locally principal and locally generated by a nonzerodivisor.  On an integral scheme the nonzerodivisor condition is automatic for a nonzero local equation, so the decisive extra property is local principality of the codimension-one subscheme.  On a regular scheme every codimension-one prime is locally principal, whereas on a normal singular scheme such as the quadric cone a prime Weil divisor can fail this test.
\end{solution}
