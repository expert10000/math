\chapter{Weil Divisors}
\label{ch:vi39-weil-divisors}
\providecommand{\Div}{\operatorname{Div}}

The projective chapters supplied a global language for schemes built from graded rings.  The next layer of geometry asks a different question: how can codimension-one subvarieties record zeros and poles of rational functions?  Weil divisors answer this by turning codimension-one geometry into an abelian group.

The cleanest valuation-theoretic theory is obtained on a normal Noetherian integral scheme.  The definition of the free abelian group of codimension-one cycles makes sense more generally, but normality is the hypothesis that turns every codimension-one local ring into a discrete valuation ring.  That distinction will be kept explicit throughout the chapter.

The migration ledger assigns eight rules to VI/39.  The primary source is the AG9 block selected by ``Weil divisor|prime divisor'' together with its theorem and exercise descendants.  A later AG10 file contains a broad divisor/Picard/line-bundle overlap and is marked compare-and-migrate rather than canonical.  Accordingly, this chapter uses the ledger as provenance and scope evidence, retains Weil-divisor material, and leaves Cartier divisors, divisor class groups, and Picard theory to VI/40--VI/42.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item identify prime divisors as codimension-one integral closed subschemes;
\item form the Weil divisor group \(\Div(X)\) and manipulate effective, positive, and negative parts;
\item explain why normality makes codimension-one local rings discrete valuation rings;
\item compute \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)\) for rational functions along prime divisors;
\item prove that principal divisors have finite support and that \(f\mapsto \operatorname{div}(f)\) is a homomorphism;
\item compute principal divisors on affine space, projective space, and normal curves;
\item restrict Weil divisors to open subsets and understand the loss of boundary components;
\item recognize the failure of naive valuation language on nonnormal schemes;
\item distinguish what is functorial for principal divisors from the non-functoriality of arbitrary Weil divisors;
\item state the first bridge from Cartier divisors to Weil divisors without using the later class-group theory.
\end{itemize}

\section{Prime divisors and codimension-one points}
Let \(X\) be a Noetherian integral scheme.  Write \(X^{(1)}\) for the set of points of codimension one.

\begin{definition}[Prime divisor]\label{def:vi39-prime-divisor}
A \emph{prime divisor} on \(X\) is an integral closed subscheme \(Z\subseteq X\) of codimension one, endowed with its reduced induced structure.  Equivalently, a prime divisor is the closure
\[
 Z=\overline{\{\eta_Z\}}
\]
of a point \(\eta_Z\in X^{(1)}\).
\end{definition}

The word \emph{prime} refers to irreducibility.  A reducible hypersurface is not one prime divisor; it is a union of prime divisors and later contributes a sum of them.

\begin{example}[Coordinate axes in the affine plane]\label{ex:vi39-axes}
On \(X=\mathbb{A}^2_k=\Spec k[x,y]\), the closed subsets \(V(x)\) and \(V(y)\) are prime divisors.  Their generic points correspond to the height-one prime ideals \((x)\) and \((y)\).  The union \(V(xy)\) is codimension one but reducible, so it is not a prime divisor.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Height-one primes in a UFD]\label{ex:vi39-ufd-primes}
If \(A\) is a UFD, every height-one prime is generated by an irreducible element.  Thus the prime divisors on \(\Spec A\) are exactly the irreducible hypersurfaces \(V(p)\), with \(p\) irreducible up to associates.
\end{example}

\section{The Weil divisor group}
\begin{definition}[Weil divisor]\label{def:vi39-weil}
A \emph{Weil divisor} on \(X\) is a finite formal integer combination
\[
 D=\sum_{Z\in X^{(1)}}n_Z[Z],\qquad n_Z\in\ZZ,
\]
where all but finitely many \(n_Z\) vanish.  The group of Weil divisors is
\[
 \Div(X):=\bigoplus_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\ZZ[Z].
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Support and effectivity]\label{def:vi39-support-effective}
For \(D=\sum n_Z[Z]\), define
\[
 \Supp(D)=\bigcup_{n_Z\ne0}Z.
\]
The divisor is \emph{effective}, written \(D\ge0\), if every \(n_Z\ge0\).  Its positive and negative parts are
\[
 D^+=\sum_Z\max(n_Z,0)[Z],\qquad
 D^-=\sum_Z\max(-n_Z,0)[Z],
\]
so that \(D=D^+-D^-\) and the supports of \(D^+\) and \(D^-\) have no common prime component.
\end{definition}

\input{chapters/ch39_weil_divisors/figures/figure_05}

\begin{example}[Divisors on the affine line]\label{ex:vi39-affine-line-group}
For \(X=\mathbb{A}^1_k\), prime divisors are closed points.  Over an algebraically closed field they are the points \([a]=V(t-a)\), so a Weil divisor is a finite sum \(\sum_a n_a[a]\).  Over a non-algebraically closed field, closed points correspond to irreducible polynomials, and their residue fields may have degree greater than one over \(k\).
\end{example}

\section{Why normality matters in codimension one}
For a normal Noetherian integral scheme, every codimension-one local ring is a DVR.  This is the valuation engine behind Weil divisors.

\begin{theorem}[Codimension one on a normal scheme]\label{thm:vi39-normal-dvr}
Let \(X\) be a normal Noetherian integral scheme and let \(Z\) be a prime divisor with generic point \(\eta_Z\).  Then
\[
 \OO_{X,\eta_Z}
\]
is a discrete valuation ring with fraction field \(K(X)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The local ring is a one-dimensional Noetherian local domain because \(\eta_Z\) has codimension one.  Normality means it is integrally closed in its fraction field.  A one-dimensional Noetherian local integrally closed domain is a DVR.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch39_weil_divisors/figures/figure_02}

\begin{definition}[Order along a prime divisor]\label{def:vi39-order}
Assume \(X\) is normal Noetherian integral.  For a prime divisor \(Z\), let
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z:K(X)^\times\longrightarrow\ZZ
\]
be the normalized discrete valuation of the DVR \(\OO_{X,\eta_Z}\).  If \(\pi_Z\) is a uniformizer and
\[
 f=u\pi_Z^m
\]
with \(u\) a unit, then \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)=m\).
\end{definition}

Thus positive order means a zero along \(Z\), negative order means a pole, and order zero means that \(f\) is a unit at the generic point of \(Z\).

\input{chapters/ch39_weil_divisors/figures/figure_03}

\begin{proposition}[Valuation rules]\label{prop:vi39-valuation-rules}
For \(f,g\in K(X)^\times\),
\[
\operatorname{ord}_Z(fg)=\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)+\operatorname{ord}_Z(g),
\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_Z(f^{-1})=-\operatorname{ord}_Z(f).
\]
If \(f+g\ne0\), then
\[
\operatorname{ord}_Z(f+g)\ge
\min\{\operatorname{ord}_Z(f),\operatorname{ord}_Z(g)\}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A basic order computation]\label{ex:vi39-basic-order}
On \(\mathbb{A}^1_k\) with coordinate \(t\), let \(Z=V(t)\).  The local ring at \(Z\) is \(k[t]_{(t)}\), with uniformizer \(t\).  Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z\!\left(\frac{t^3}{(1+t)^5}\right)=3,
\]
because \(1+t\) is a unit at \((t)\).
\end{example}

\section{Principal divisors}
\begin{definition}[Principal divisor]\label{def:vi39-principal}
Let \(X\) be normal Noetherian integral and \(0\ne f\in K(X)\).  Its \emph{principal Weil divisor} is
\[
 \operatorname{div}_X(f)
 :=\sum_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)[Z].
\]
\end{definition}

The notation makes sense because the sum is finite.

\begin{theorem}[Finite support]\label{thm:vi39-finite-support}
For every \(f\in K(X)^\times\), only finitely many prime divisors \(Z\) satisfy \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)\ne0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose a nonempty affine open \(U=\Spec A\subseteq X\) on which \(f=a/b\) with \(a,b\in A\setminus\{0\}\).  Any codimension-one point of \(U\) at which \(f\) has nonzero order lies in \(V(a)\cup V(b)\).  A Noetherian closed subset has only finitely many irreducible components, hence only finitely many codimension-one generic points arising this way.  The complement \(X\setminus U\) has finitely many irreducible components, and only its codimension-one components can contribute.  Thus the support is finite.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Principal divisors form a homomorphic image]\label{prop:vi39-principal-hom}
The map
\[
 K(X)^\times\longrightarrow\Div(X),
 \qquad f\longmapsto\operatorname{div}(f),
\]
is a group homomorphism from multiplication to addition:
\[
 \operatorname{div}(fg)=\operatorname{div}(f)+\operatorname{div}(g).
\]
In particular, \(\operatorname{div}(f^{-1})=-\operatorname{div}(f)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A rational function on the affine line]\label{ex:vi39-affine-rational}
On \(\mathbb{A}^1_k\), assuming \(0,1\in k\),
\[
 f(t)=\frac{t^3}{(t-1)^2}
\]
has
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{A}^1}(f)=3[0]-2[1].
\]
There is no contribution from infinity because infinity is not a point of \(\mathbb{A}^1\).
\end{example}

\section{The projective line: zeros must balance poles}
Let \(t=x_1/x_0\) be the usual rational coordinate on \(\PP^1_k\).  The point at infinity is \(\infty=[0:1]\).

\begin{example}[The divisor of the coordinate on \(\PP^1\)]\label{ex:vi39-p1-coordinate}
The rational function \(t\) has a simple zero at \(0=[1:0]\) and a simple pole at \(\infty\).  Hence
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\PP^1}(t)=[0]-[\infty].
\]
\end{example}

\input{chapters/ch39_weil_divisors/figures/figure_04}

\begin{example}[Moving one point to another]\label{ex:vi39-p1-ratio}
For distinct \(a,b\in k\),
\[
 \operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{t-a}{t-b}\right)=[a]-[b].
\]
The orders at infinity cancel because numerator and denominator have the same degree.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Polynomial viewed projectively]\label{ex:vi39-polynomial-projective}
For a nonzero polynomial
\[
 p(t)=c\prod_i q_i(t)^{e_i}
\]
with distinct monic irreducible \(q_i\in k[t]\), let \(P_i\) be the corresponding closed points of \(\mathbb{A}^1_k\subset\PP^1_k\).  If \(d=\deg p\), then
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\PP^1}(p)=\sum_i e_i[P_i]-d[\infty].
\]
Over a non-algebraically closed field, the degree of \([P_i]\) is \([\kappa(P_i):k]=\deg q_i\), so the weighted degree of the divisor is zero.
\end{example}

\section{Factorization and divisors on affine space}
On a factorial normal affine scheme, principal divisors can be read directly from irreducible factorization.

\begin{proposition}[Principal divisors in a UFD]\label{prop:vi39-ufd-divisor}
Let \(A\) be a Noetherian UFD and \(X=\Spec A\).  If
\[
 f=u\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}
\]
with \(u\in A^\times\) and pairwise nonassociate irreducibles \(p_i\), then
\[
 \operatorname{div}_X(f)=\sum_{i=1}^r e_i[V(p_i)].
\]
For \(f=a/b\in\Frac(A)^\times\), subtract the factorization divisor of \(b\) from that of \(a\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[A divisor in \(\mathbb{A}^2\)]\label{ex:vi39-a2-factorization}
In \(k[x,y]\),
\[
 f=x^2y(x-y)^3
\]
has
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb{A}^2}(f)
 =2[V(x)]+[V(y)]+3[V(x-y)].
\]
The union of the three lines is the support; the multiplicities are encoded in the coefficients.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A rational function on \(\PP^2\)]\label{ex:vi39-p2-ratio}
Let \(F,G\in k[x_0,x_1,x_2]\) be nonzero homogeneous forms of the same degree.  Their ratio \(F/G\) is a rational function on \(\PP^2\).  Factoring \(F\) and \(G\) into irreducible homogeneous factors yields
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\PP^2}(F/G)=\operatorname{div}(F)-\operatorname{div}(G),
\]
where each irreducible factor contributes its projective hypersurface with the corresponding exponent.
\end{example}

\section{Units and the dependence on the ambient open set}
A principal divisor records codimension-one zeros and poles \emph{inside the chosen scheme}.  Boundary components disappear when one passes to an open subset.

\begin{example}[A nonconstant function with zero divisor]\label{ex:vi39-gm-unit}
On \(\GG_m=\Spec k[t,t^{-1}]\), the function \(t\) is a global unit.  Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\GG_m}(t)=0,
\]
even though \(t\) is nonconstant.  Thus the kernel of \(K(X)^\times\to\Div(X)\) need not consist only of constants on a nonproper scheme.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}[Restriction to an open subset]\label{prop:vi39-restriction}
Let \(j:U\hookrightarrow X\) be a nonempty open subset of a normal Noetherian integral scheme.  For
\[
 D=\sum_Z n_Z[Z],
\]
define
\[
 D|_U:=\sum_{Z\cap U\ne\varnothing}n_Z[\overline{Z\cap U}^{\,U}],
\]
omitting the prime divisors contained entirely in \(X\setminus U\).  Then
\[
 \operatorname{div}_U(f)=\operatorname{div}_X(f)|_U
\]
for every \(f\in K(X)^\times=K(U)^\times\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{example}[Removing infinity]\label{ex:vi39-remove-infinity}
Take \(X=\PP^1_k\) and \(U=\mathbb{A}^1_k=X\setminus\{\infty\}\).  Since
\[
 \operatorname{div}_X(t)=[0]-[\infty],
\]
restriction gives
\[
 \operatorname{div}_U(t)=[0].
\]
The pole has not vanished analytically; its divisor component has been removed from the ambient scheme.
\end{example}

\section{A normal singular surface: multiplicity without smoothness}
Normality does not mean smoothness.  A codimension-one DVR calculation can remain valid even at a singular surface.

\begin{example}[The quadric cone \(xy=z^2\)]\label{ex:vi39-a1-singularity}
Let
\[
 A=k[x,y,z]/(xy-z^2),\qquad X=\Spec A.
\]
This is a normal two-dimensional domain.  Consider the height-one primes
\[
 P=(x,z),\qquad Q=(y,z).
\]
At \(P\), the element \(y\) is a unit and the relation gives \(x=z^2/y\).  Thus \(z\) is a uniformizer in \(A_P\), so
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_P(z)=1,\qquad \operatorname{ord}_P(x)=2.
\]
Similarly,
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Q(z)=1,\qquad \operatorname{ord}_Q(y)=2.
\]
Hence
\[
 \operatorname{div}(z)=[P]+[Q],\qquad
 \operatorname{div}(x)=2[P],\qquad
 \operatorname{div}(y)=2[Q].
\]
The fact that \([P]\) occurs with coefficient two in \(\operatorname{div}(x)\) is a genuine codimension-one multiplicity phenomenon.
\end{example}

\section{What fails without normality}
\begin{example}[The cusp]\label{ex:vi39-cusp-nonnormal}
Let
\[
 A=k[t^2,t^3]\subset k[t]
\]
and \(X=\Spec A\).  The singular closed point \(P=(t^2,t^3)\) has codimension one because \(X\) is a curve, but \(A_P\) is not a DVR.  In particular, there is no element of valuation one lying in \(A_P\): the orders coming from the normalization \(k[t]_{(t)}\) of elements of the maximal ideal begin at two and three.  Thus codimension one alone does not produce the intrinsic DVR valuation used in the normal theory.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}[Normalization restores codimension-one valuations]\label{rem:vi39-normalization}
If \(\nu:\widetilde X\to X\) is the normalization of an integral Noetherian scheme, the codimension-one points of \(\widetilde X\) carry DVR valuations.  Several points of \(\widetilde X\) may lie over one codimension-one point of \(X\), so passing to the normalization can split branches.  This is one reason Weil divisors are especially natural on normal schemes.
\end{remark}

\section{Divisors on normal projective curves}
Let \(C\) be a normal projective integral curve over a field \(k\).  Since every closed point has codimension one,
\[
 \Div(C)=\bigoplus_{P\in C_{\mathrm{cl}}}\ZZ[P].
\]

\begin{definition}[Degree on a proper curve]\label{def:vi39-curve-degree}
For
\[
 D=\sum_P n_P[P],
\]
define
\[
 \deg D:=\sum_P n_P[\kappa(P):k].
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Principal divisors have degree zero]\label{thm:vi39-degree-zero}
For every \(f\in K(C)^\times\),
\[
 \deg\operatorname{div}(f)=0.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If \(f\in k^\times\), the divisor is zero.  Otherwise \(f\) defines a finite morphism \(C\to\PP^1_k\).  The zero divisor and pole divisor are the scheme-theoretic fibers over \(0\) and \(\infty\), with multiplicities given by the corresponding DVR ramification indices.  Every fiber of a finite morphism of proper integral curves has the same weighted degree, namely \([K(C):k(f)]\).  Hence the total weighted order of zeros equals the total weighted order of poles.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Degree on \(\PP^1\)]\label{ex:vi39-p1-degree}
The divisor \([0]-[\infty]\) has degree zero.  More generally, for an irreducible polynomial \(q(t)\) of degree \(d\), the corresponding closed point \(P_q\) satisfies
\[
 \deg[P_q]=d,
\]
and
\[
 \operatorname{div}(q(t))=[P_q]-d[\infty].
\]
\end{example}

\section{Principal pullback and the warning about general pullback}
Let \(g:Y\to X\) be a dominant morphism of normal integral schemes.  It induces an inclusion of function fields
\[
 K(X)\hookrightarrow K(Y).
\]
Therefore every rational function on \(X\) can be pulled back as a rational function on \(Y\).

\begin{proposition}[Pullback of principal divisors]\label{prop:vi39-principal-pullback}
For \(f\in K(X)^\times\), the expression
\[
 \operatorname{div}_Y(g^*f)
\]
is always defined.  It is the only pullback operation on Weil divisors needed in this chapter.
\end{proposition}

\begin{warning}[Arbitrary Weil divisors do not pull back automatically]\label{warn:vi39-no-naive-pullback}
There is no canonical pullback \(g^*:\Div(X)\to\Div(Y)\) for an arbitrary morphism of normal schemes.  A prime divisor may map into codimension greater than one, a morphism may factor through its support, and multiplicities require extra hypotheses.  Cartier divisors have a substantially better pullback theory; this is one motivation for VI/40.
\end{warning}

\section{First bridge to Cartier divisors}
A Cartier divisor is locally represented by nonzero rational functions modulo regular units.  On a normal integral scheme, those local equations have well-defined orders along every prime divisor.

\begin{proposition}[Cartier-to-Weil map: preview]\label{prop:vi39-cartier-weil-preview}
Let \(X\) be normal Noetherian integral.  If a Cartier divisor is represented near the generic point of a prime divisor \(Z\) by \(f_Z\in K(X)^\times\), define its coefficient along \(Z\) by \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(f_Z)\).  These coefficients are independent of the chosen local equation and yield a Weil divisor.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Two local equations differ by a regular unit.  A unit in the DVR \(\OO_{X,\eta_Z}\) has order zero, so the order is unchanged.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[A hyperplane is both Cartier and Weil]\label{ex:vi39-hyperplane-preview}
On \(\PP^n_k\), the hyperplane \(H=V_+(x_0)\) is a prime Weil divisor.  It is also locally cut out by one nonzerodivisor, so VI/40 will identify it with an effective Cartier divisor.  The associated Weil divisor has coefficient one along \(H\).
\end{example}

\input{chapters/ch39_weil_divisors/figures/figure_08}

\begin{example}[A Weil divisor that signals the Cartier distinction]\label{ex:vi39-noncartier-preview}
On the normal surface \(xy=z^2\), the prime divisor \(P=(x,z)\) is a perfectly good Weil divisor.  The relation \(\operatorname{div}(x)=2[P]\) hints that \([P]\) need not itself be principal or locally cut out by one equation at the singular point.  VI/40 will make the Cartier distinction precise, and VI/41 will measure the remaining obstruction in the divisor class group.
\end{example}

\section{Worked examples}
The following examples consolidate the chapter's main computational patterns.

\begin{example}[Zero and pole orders from a local parameter]\label{ex:vi39-worked-local}
Let \(C\) be a smooth curve, \(P\in C\), and \(u\) a uniformizer at \(P\).  If
\[
 f=u^{-4}(1+u+u^3),
\]
then the parenthesized factor is a unit, so \(\operatorname{ord}_P(f)=-4\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Cancellation must be tested before taking orders]\label{ex:vi39-worked-cancel}
If \(\operatorname{ord}_P(f)=\operatorname{ord}_P(g)=2\), the order of \(f+g\) can exceed two.  For instance, in \(k[[u]]\), take \(f=u^2\) and \(g=-u^2+u^5\).  Then \(f+g=u^5\), so the order jumps to five.
\end{example}

\section*{Chapter summary}
For a normal Noetherian integral scheme \(X\), codimension-one points are controlled by discrete valuation rings.  They produce the free abelian group
\[
\Div(X)=\bigoplus_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\ZZ[Z],
\]
and every nonzero rational function determines the finite principal divisor
\[
\operatorname{div}(f)=\sum_Z\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)[Z].
\]
This turns zeros and poles into codimension-one geometry with multiplicity.  Restriction to open subsets discards boundary components, normality is what makes the valuation theory intrinsic, and arbitrary Weil divisors do not admit unrestricted pullback.  VI/40 now replaces formal codimension-one cycles by local rational equations and develops Cartier divisors, whose pullback behavior is substantially better.
