% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-SOLUTIONS
% Detailed solutions for the canonical 24 exercises of VI/39.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E1.}\label{sol:vi39-e01}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-01
Write \(D=\sum_Z n_Z[Z]\).  By definition only finitely many coefficients \(n_Z\) are nonzero, say for \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_r\).  Hence
\[
 \operatorname{Supp}(D)=Z_1\cup\cdots\cup Z_r.
\]
Each \(Z_i\) is a closed integral subscheme of \(X\), so its underlying set is closed.  A finite union of closed subsets is closed.  The finiteness built into the definition of a Weil divisor is essential here.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E2.}\label{sol:vi39-e02}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-02
For each prime divisor \(Z\), put
\[
 n_Z^+=\max(n_Z,0),\qquad n_Z^- = \max(-n_Z,0).
\]
Then \(n_Z=n_Z^+-n_Z^-\), both coefficients are nonnegative, and at most one of \(n_Z^+,n_Z^-\) is nonzero.  Thus
\[
 D^+=\sum_Z n_Z^+[Z],\qquad D^-=\sum_Z n_Z^-[Z]
\]
are effective, have no common prime component, and satisfy \(D=D^+-D^-\).  If \(D=A-B\) is another such decomposition with \(A,B\ge0\) and disjoint prime support, comparison of the coefficient of each \(Z\) forces the coefficient of \(A\) to be \(n_Z^+\) and that of \(B\) to be \(n_Z^-\).  Hence the decomposition is unique.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E3.}\label{sol:vi39-e03}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-03
On \(\mathbb A^1_k=\operatorname{Spec}k[t]\), the only height-one prime at which \(t\) is not a unit is \((t)\), corresponding to \(0\).  In the DVR \(k[t]_{(t)}\), \(t\) is a uniformizer, so
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_0(t^m)=m
\]
for every \(m\in\mathbb Z\), including negative \(m\).  At every other closed point, \(t\) is a unit and the order is zero.  Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb A^1}(t^m)=m[0].
\]
For \(m<0\), the negative coefficient records a pole at \(0\).

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E4.}\label{sol:vi39-e04}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-04
On the standard affine chart of \(\mathbb P^1\), \(t\) has a simple zero at \(0\), hence order \(m\) for \(t^m\).  Near \(\infty\), use the local parameter \(s=1/t\).  Then
\[
 t^m=s^{-m},
\]
so \(\operatorname{ord}_{\infty}(t^m)=-m\).  There are no other zeros or poles.  Thus
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb P^1}(t^m)=m[0]-m[\infty].
\]
The divisor has degree zero, as every principal divisor on a proper normal curve must.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E5.}\label{sol:vi39-e05}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-05
Let
\[
 f=\frac{(t-a)^r}{(t-b)^s},\qquad a\ne b.
\]
At \(a\), the denominator is a unit and \(t-a\) is a uniformizer, so \(\operatorname{ord}_a(f)=r\).  At \(b\), the numerator is a unit and \(\operatorname{ord}_b(f)=-s\).  Every other irreducible factor is absent.  Since \(\mathbb A^1\) has no point at infinity,
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb A^1}(f)=r[a]-s[b].
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E6.}\label{sol:vi39-e06}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-06
The finite-point contributions are again \(r[a]-s[b]\).  At infinity write \(s_\infty=1/t\).  Then
\[
 \frac{(t-a)^r}{(t-b)^s}
 =s_\infty^{\,s-r}
   \frac{(1-a s_\infty)^r}{(1-b s_\infty)^s}.
\]
The final fraction is a unit at \(s_\infty=0\), so the order at infinity is \(s-r\).  Consequently
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb P^1}(f)
 =r[a]-s[b]+(s-r)[\infty].
\]
The coefficients sum to zero when the points are \(k\)-rational.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E7.}\label{sol:vi39-e07}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-07
The ring \(k[x,y]\) is a UFD.  The elements \(x\), \(y\), and \(x-y\) are pairwise nonassociate irreducibles, hence define the relevant height-one prime divisors.  Factorization gives
\[
 \frac{x^4y^2}{(x-y)^3}=x^4y^2(x-y)^{-3},
\]
and therefore
\[
 \operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{x^4y^2}{(x-y)^3}\right)
 =4[V(x)]+2[V(y)]-3[V(x-y)].
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E8.}\label{sol:vi39-e08}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-08
In a DVR every nonzero fraction has a unique form \(u\pi^m\), with \(u\) a unit and \(m\in\mathbb Z\).  If the element itself is a unit, no power of the maximal-ideal generator \(\pi\) occurs, so \(m=0\).  Hence a unit of \(\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}\) has \(\operatorname{ord}_Z=0\).

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E9.}\label{sol:vi39-e09}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-09
For every prime divisor \(Z\), the DVR valuation is a homomorphism from multiplication to addition.  Thus
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z(f/g)
 =\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)+\operatorname{ord}_Z(g^{-1})
 =\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)-\operatorname{ord}_Z(g).
\]
Taking these identities as the coefficients of the corresponding prime divisors and summing over \(Z\) yields
\[
 \operatorname{div}(f/g)=\operatorname{div}(f)-\operatorname{div}(g).
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E10.}\label{sol:vi39-e10}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-10
Let \(Z\) be a prime divisor of \(X\) with \(Z\cap U\ne\varnothing\).  Since \(Z\) is irreducible, every nonempty open subset of \(Z\) contains its generic point \(\eta_Z\); hence \(\eta_Z\in U\).  The closure of \(\eta_Z\) inside \(U\) is exactly \(Z\cap U\) with reduced structure, so it is integral.  Moreover
\[
 \mathcal O_{U,\eta_Z}=\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z},
\]
so the codimension of \(\eta_Z\) remains one.  Thus \(\overline{Z\cap U}^{\,U}\) is a prime divisor on \(U\).

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E11.}\label{sol:vi39-e11}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-11
By definition
\[
 \operatorname{Div}(X)=\bigoplus_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\mathbb Z[Z],
\]
so its basis elements are exactly the integral closed subschemes of codimension one.  A codimension-two subset may lie inside the support of one or more prime divisors, but it cannot itself appear with an independent coefficient in a Weil divisor.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E12.}\label{sol:vi39-e12}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-12
In the DVR \(R=\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}\), a nonzero fraction \(f\) has order zero exactly when \(f=u\pi^0=u\) for some \(u\in R^\times\).  Membership in \(R^\times\) means precisely that \(f\) is regular at \(\eta_Z\) and its value there is nonzero.  Therefore
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z(f)=0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 f\in\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}^{\times}.
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E13.}\label{sol:vi39-e13}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-13
Again write \(f=u\pi^m\) in the fraction field of the DVR \(R=\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}\).  If \(m\ge0\), then \(u\pi^m\in R\).  If \(m<0\), then a negative power of the uniformizer occurs, so \(f\notin R\).  Hence
\[
 f\in\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \operatorname{ord}_Z(f)\ge0.
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E14.}\label{sol:vi39-e14}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-14
For
\[
 D=2[Z_1]-3[Z_2]+[Z_3]
\]
the positive coefficients are those of \(Z_1\) and \(Z_3\), while the negative coefficient occurs at \(Z_2\).  Thus
\[
 D^+=2[Z_1]+[Z_3],\qquad D^-=3[Z_2],
\]
and
\[
 \operatorname{Supp}(D)=Z_1\cup Z_2\cup Z_3.
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E15.}\label{sol:vi39-e15}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-15
On the quadric cone, the chapter computes
\[
 \operatorname{div}(z)=[P]+[Q],\quad
 \operatorname{div}(x)=2[P],\quad
 \operatorname{div}(y)=2[Q].
\]
Therefore multiplicativity gives
\[
 \operatorname{div}(z^2)=2[P]+2[Q].
\]
On the other hand,
\[
 \operatorname{div}(xy)=\operatorname{div}(x)+\operatorname{div}(y)
 =2[P]+2[Q].
\]
This agrees with the ring relation \(xy=z^2\), as it must.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E16.}\label{sol:vi39-e16}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-16
If \(u\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X)^\times\), then its image is a unit in every local ring of \(X\), in particular in every codimension-one DVR \(\mathcal O_{X,\eta_Z}\).  Hence \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(u)=0\) for every \(Z\), so
\[
 \operatorname{div}(u)=0.
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E17.}\label{sol:vi39-e17}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-17
Take \(X=\mathbb G_m=\operatorname{Spec}k[t,t^{-1}]\).  The function \(t\) is nonconstant but belongs to the unit group of the coordinate ring, with inverse \(t^{-1}\).  By the preceding exercise it has zero order at every prime divisor, so
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb G_m}(t)=0.
\]
This is the standard warning that ``zero divisor'' does not mean ``constant'' on a nonproper scheme.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E18.}\label{sol:vi39-e18}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-18
Let \(P_q\) be the closed point of \(\mathbb A^1_k\subset\mathbb P^1_k\) defined by the irreducible polynomial \(q\), with \(\deg q=d\).  The zero along \(P_q\) is simple, while at infinity a polynomial of degree \(d\) has a pole of order \(d\).  Hence
\[
 \operatorname{div}_{\mathbb P^1}(q)=[P_q]-d[\infty].
\]
Because \([\kappa(P_q):k]=d\) and \([\kappa(\infty):k]=1\),
\[
 \deg\operatorname{div}(q)=d-d=0.
\]

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E19.}\label{sol:vi39-e19}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-19
For divisors \(D=\sum_P n_P[P]\) and \(E=\sum_P m_P[P]\),
\[
 \deg(D+E)
 =\sum_P(n_P+m_P)[\kappa(P):k]
 =\deg D+\deg E.
\]
All sums are finite, so rearrangement is harmless.  Also \(\deg 0=0\).  Hence \(\deg:\operatorname{Div}(C)\to\mathbb Z\) is a group homomorphism.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E20.}\label{sol:vi39-e20}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-20
The equality \(\operatorname{div}(f)=0\) says exactly that \(\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)=0\) for every codimension-one prime \(Z\): there are no codimension-one zeros or poles.  On a normal Noetherian integral scheme this actually implies that \(f\) is a global unit: on every normal affine open \(\operatorname{Spec}A\), the Krull-domain identity
\[
 A=\bigcap_{\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak p=1}A_{\mathfrak p}
\]
shows that both \(f\) and \(f^{-1}\) lie in \(A\).  But a global unit need not be constant on a nonproper scheme; \(t\in k[t,t^{-1}]^\times\) is the basic example.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E21.}\label{sol:vi39-e21}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-21
If \(F\) and \(G\) are homogeneous of the same degree \(d\), then under projective rescaling
\[
 (x_0,\ldots,x_n)\longmapsto(\lambda x_0,\ldots,\lambda x_n)
\]
one has \(F\mapsto\lambda^dF\) and \(G\mapsto\lambda^dG\).  Therefore
\[
 \frac{F(\lambda x)}{G(\lambda x)}
 =\frac{\lambda^dF(x)}{\lambda^dG(x)}
 =\frac{F(x)}{G(x)}.
\]
Thus \(F/G\) is a well-defined element of the function field of \(\mathbb P^n\).

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E22.}\label{sol:vi39-e22}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-22
If a Cartier local equation \(f\) is replaced by \(uf\), where \(u\) is a regular unit, then at every prime divisor \(Z\)
\[
 \operatorname{ord}_Z(uf)
 =\operatorname{ord}_Z(u)+\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)
 =0+\operatorname{ord}_Z(f).
\]
Hence every coefficient in the associated Weil divisor is unchanged.  This is exactly why the Cartier-to-Weil construction descends from local equations modulo units.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E23.}\label{sol:vi39-e23}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-23
A dominant morphism \(g:Y\to X\) induces a homomorphism of function fields.  Hence
\[
 g^*(fh)=g^*f\,g^*h.
\]
Applying the principal-divisor homomorphism on \(Y\) gives
\[
 \operatorname{div}_Y(g^*(fh))
 =\operatorname{div}_Y(g^*f)+\operatorname{div}_Y(g^*h).
\]
This statement uses pullback of rational functions only; it does not assert that arbitrary Weil divisors possess a pullback for arbitrary morphisms.

\paragraph{Solution VI.39.E24.}\label{sol:vi39-e24}
% VI39-DETAILED-EXERCISE-24
On a two-dimensional integral scheme, a closed point has codimension two.  Prime Weil divisors, by definition, have codimension one.  Therefore an isolated singular point of a normal surface is not itself a prime divisor, even though prime divisors may pass through it and their local behavior there can distinguish Cartier from non-Cartier phenomena.
