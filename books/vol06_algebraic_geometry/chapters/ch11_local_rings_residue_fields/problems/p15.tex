\begin{problem}[Specialization chains and maps of local rings]
Let $\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak q$ be prime ideals of $A$.  Construct the canonical map
\[
A_{\mathfrak q}\longrightarrow A_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
Apply this to $(0)\subset(x)\subset(x,y)$ in $k[x,y]$ and describe the three rings.
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
The direction of local-ring maps is a frequent source of confusion and reflects contravariance.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
Compare the multiplicative sets $A\setminus\mathfrak q$ and $A\setminus\mathfrak p$.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
A smaller prime has a larger complement, so its localization inverts more elements.

\begin{solution}
If $\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak q$, then $A\setminus\mathfrak q\subseteq A\setminus\mathfrak p$.  Thus every denominator inverted in $A_{\mathfrak q}$ is already invertible in $A_{\mathfrak p}$, giving the canonical map.  For $A=k[x,y]$,
\[
A_{(x,y)}\to A_{(x)}\to A_{(0)}=k(x,y).
\]
The middle ring inverts every element not divisible by $x$, and the final ring inverts every nonzero polynomial.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
Generalization of points corresponds to further localization of local rings.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Study an arbitrary saturated chain of primes and the induced tower of localizations.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
New canonical synthesis problem based on the local-ring and residue-field corpus.
