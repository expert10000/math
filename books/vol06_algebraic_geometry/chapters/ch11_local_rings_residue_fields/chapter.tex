\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

The spectrum of a ring tells us where the prime ideals are and how they specialize, but it
still treats each point largely as a topological position.  Local algebra adds the next layer.
Given a point $\mathfrak p\in\Spec A$, we invert every element that does not vanish at that
point and obtain the local ring
\[
A_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
This ring records algebraic information visible arbitrarily near $\mathfrak p$.  Its unique
maximal ideal is $\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}$, and quotienting by that maximal ideal gives
the residue field
\[
\kappa(\mathfrak p)=A_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
The residue field is the correct field of values at a scheme-theoretic point.

This chapter is intentionally example-rich.  In addition to the main worked-example section,
there is a separate \emph{Advanced examples} section containing arithmetic, reducible,
nonreduced, and singular local rings.  The examples are drawn from and expand the legacy
commutative-algebra corpus, especially its detailed calculations in $k[t]$, $\mathbb R[t]$,
$\mathbb Z$, finite fields, crossing components, and mixed rings such as $\mathbb Z[x]$.

We remain primarily in affine algebra.  The formal construction of stalks from sheaves comes
in VI/13, the structure sheaf in VI/17, affine schemes as locally ringed spaces in VI/18,
fibers in VI/25, integral schemes and function fields in VI/27, and tangent spaces in VI/32.
Where these ideas naturally appear here, we mark them as previews rather than importing the
later chapters wholesale.

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item recognize and characterize local rings abstractly;
\item compute units and the unique maximal ideal in a local ring;
\item construct $A_{\mathfrak p}$ and determine its units and maximal ideal;
\item identify $\Spec A_{\mathfrak p}$ with the generalizations of $\mathfrak p$;
\item compute residue fields by both localization and quotient methods;
\item prove $\kappa(\mathfrak p)\cong\Frac(A/\mathfrak p)$;
\item distinguish rational, non-rational closed, and generic points via residue fields;
\item compute local rings at smooth points, crossings, generic points, and nonreduced points;
\item compare local rings before and after restricting to a principal open;
\item combine quotients and localization correctly;
\item recognize local homomorphisms and the induced maps on residue fields;
\item use local rings to see when components disappear after localization;
\item work comfortably with arithmetic local rings such as $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$;
\item interpret the short exact sequence
      $0\to\mathfrak m\to A\to A/\mathfrak m\to0$ in local geometry;
\item avoid common confusions between $A_f$, $A_{\mathfrak p}$, $A/\mathfrak p$, and
      $\kappa(\mathfrak p)$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Local rings as algebraic objects}

\begin{definition}[Local ring]
A commutative ring $R$ is \emph{local} if it has a unique maximal ideal.  We usually write
\[
(R,\mathfrak m)
\]
for a local ring together with its maximal ideal.
\end{definition}

The most useful test for locality is a statement about units.

\begin{theorem}[Units and nonunits in a local ring]
For a commutative ring $R$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R$ is local;
\item the set of nonunits of $R$ is an ideal;
\item there exists a proper ideal $\mathfrak m$ such that every element of
      $R\setminus\mathfrak m$ is a unit;
\item for every $a\in R$, at least one of $a$ and $1-a$ is a unit.
\end{enumerate}
When these conditions hold, the nonunits are exactly the unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $R$ has unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, every nonunit lies in some maximal ideal and
therefore lies in $\mathfrak m$; conversely no element of $\mathfrak m$ is a unit.  Hence the
nonunits are exactly $\mathfrak m$ and form an ideal.  This proves $(1)\Rightarrow(2)$ and
$(2)\Rightarrow(3)$ is immediate.

If $(3)$ holds and $\mathfrak n$ is any maximal ideal, then $\mathfrak n$ contains no unit,
so $\mathfrak n\subseteq\mathfrak m$.  Maximality forces $\mathfrak n=\mathfrak m$, proving
$(3)\Rightarrow(1)$.

Finally, if $R$ is local and both $a$ and $1-a$ were nonunits, then both would lie in
$\mathfrak m$, forcing $1\in\mathfrak m$.  Conversely, assume condition $(4)$.  If two
maximal ideals $\mathfrak m\neq\mathfrak n$ existed, choose $a\in\mathfrak m$ and
$b\in\mathfrak n$ with $a+b=1$ after using $\mathfrak m+\mathfrak n=R$.  Then $a$ and
$1-a=b$ would both be nonunits, contradiction.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_units_nonunits.tex}

\begin{corollary}
If $(R,\mathfrak m)$ is local, then
\[
R^\times=R\setminus\mathfrak m.
\]
In particular, every element is either a unit or lies in the maximal ideal.
\end{corollary}

\section{Localization at a prime}

Let $A$ be a ring and $\mathfrak p\in\Spec A$.  We localize at the multiplicative set
\[
S=A\setminus\mathfrak p.
\]
The resulting ring is
\[
A_{\mathfrak p}=S^{-1}A.
\]
An element is represented by a fraction $a/s$ with $s\notin\mathfrak p$.

\begin{theorem}[Prime localization is local]
The ring $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is local.  Its unique maximal ideal is
\[
\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}
=
\left\{\frac{a}{s}:a\in\mathfrak p,\ s\notin\mathfrak p\right\}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\frac{a}{s}\in A_{\mathfrak p}
\quad\text{is a unit}\quad
\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a\notin\mathfrak p.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $a\notin\mathfrak p$, then $a$ belongs to the multiplicative set, so
$(a/s)^{-1}=s/a$.  If $a\in\mathfrak p$ and $a/s$ were a unit, there would be $b/t$ with
$ab/(st)=1$.  After clearing denominators, some element outside $\mathfrak p$ would lie in
$(a)\subseteq\mathfrak p$, impossible.  Thus the nonunits are exactly
$\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}$, and the preceding theorem shows that this is the unique
maximal ideal.
\end{proof}

\section{The local spectrum and generalizations}

Localization at a prime has a clean geometric meaning.

\begin{theorem}[Prime ideals of $A_{\mathfrak p}$]
Contraction and extension give an inclusion-preserving bijection
\[
\Spec A_{\mathfrak p}
\longleftrightarrow
\{\mathfrak q\in\Spec A:\mathfrak q\subseteq\mathfrak p\}.
\]
Under the canonical map $\Spec A_{\mathfrak p}\to\Spec A$, the image consists exactly of
the generalizations of $\mathfrak p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Prime ideals of a localization $S^{-1}A$ correspond to prime ideals of $A$ disjoint from
$S$.  Here $S=A\setminus\mathfrak p$, so disjointness means every element of
$\mathfrak q$ lies in $\mathfrak p$, i.e. $\mathfrak q\subseteq\mathfrak p$.
\end{proof}

Thus the unique closed point of $\Spec A_{\mathfrak p}$ is
$\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}$, while smaller primes appear as more generic points.

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_local_spectrum.tex}

\begin{remark}[A local spectrum is not usually an open neighborhood]
The image $\{\mathfrak q:\mathfrak q\subseteq\mathfrak p\}$ need not be open in
$\Spec A$.  Localization at a single element $f$ produces the open subset $D(f)$;
localization at a prime is a different operation that inverts \emph{all} elements outside
$\mathfrak p$.
\end{remark}

\section{Residue fields}

\begin{definition}[Residue field]
For $\mathfrak p\in\Spec A$, the \emph{residue field at $\mathfrak p$} is
\[
\kappa(\mathfrak p)
:=A_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
\end{definition}

The canonical map
\[
A\longrightarrow A_{\mathfrak p}\longrightarrow\kappa(\mathfrak p)
\]
has kernel exactly $\mathfrak p$.  Thus elements of $A$ can be ``evaluated'' at
$\mathfrak p$, but the values need not lie in the original ground field.

\begin{theorem}[Computational formula for residue fields]
For every prime ideal $\mathfrak p\subseteq A$,
\[
\boxed{
\kappa(\mathfrak p)
\cong
\Frac(A/\mathfrak p).
}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Localize the quotient map $A\to A/\mathfrak p$ at $S=A\setminus\mathfrak p$.  This gives
\[
A_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}
\cong
S^{-1}(A/\mathfrak p).
\]
Since $A/\mathfrak p$ is a domain and the image of $S$ is its set of nonzero elements,
this localization is exactly its field of fractions.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_residue_formula.tex}

\begin{corollary}[Maximal ideals]
If $\mathfrak m$ is maximal, then
\[
\kappa(\mathfrak m)\cong A/\mathfrak m,
\]
because $A/\mathfrak m$ is already a field.
\end{corollary}

\section{Rational, non-rational, and generic points}

Suppose $A$ is a $k$-algebra.  A closed point $\mathfrak m$ is $k$-rational when the
structural map $k\to\kappa(\mathfrak m)$ is an isomorphism.  If $k$ is not algebraically
closed, closed points can have larger residue fields.

At the opposite extreme, if $A$ is a domain and $\eta=(0)$ is the generic point, then
\[
\kappa(\eta)=\Frac(A).
\]
More generally, the generic point $\mathfrak p$ of the irreducible closed subset
$V(\mathfrak p)$ carries the field
\[
\kappa(\mathfrak p)=\Frac(A/\mathfrak p),
\]
which is the function field of that irreducible piece.

\section{Local rings inside principal opens}

Suppose $f\notin\mathfrak p$.  Then $\mathfrak p\in D(f)$ and the corresponding prime of
$A_f$ is $\mathfrak pA_f$.

\begin{proposition}[Local ring does not change after shrinking around the point]
If $f\notin\mathfrak p$, then there are canonical isomorphisms
\[
(A_f)_{\mathfrak pA_f}\cong A_{\mathfrak p},
\qquad
\kappa_{A_f}(\mathfrak pA_f)\cong\kappa_A(\mathfrak p).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In $A_{\mathfrak p}$ the element $f$ is already invertible, so the universal property of
localization gives $A_f\to A_{\mathfrak p}$.  Localizing $A_f$ at
$\mathfrak pA_f$ then inverts exactly the remaining elements outside $\mathfrak p$, giving
$A_{\mathfrak p}$.  The residue-field isomorphism follows by quotienting by the maximal
ideals.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_shrink_invariance.tex}

This proposition is the algebraic reason a local ring depends only on an arbitrarily small
affine neighborhood of the point.

\section{Quotients and local rings}

Let $I\subseteq\mathfrak p$.  The point $\mathfrak p$ of $\Spec A$ corresponds to the
point $\mathfrak p/I$ of $\Spec(A/I)$.

\begin{proposition}[Localization after quotient]
There is a canonical isomorphism
\[
(A/I)_{\mathfrak p/I}
\cong
A_{\mathfrak p}/IA_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
The two points have the same residue field:
\[
\kappa_{A/I}(\mathfrak p/I)
\cong
\kappa_A(\mathfrak p).
\]
\end{proposition}

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_quotient_localization.tex}

The quotient changes the local ring by imposing the equations $I$, but once we reach the
residue field at a point containing $I$, those equations already vanish.

\section{When a fraction or germ is zero}

A subtle but important localization test is the following.

\begin{proposition}[Zero in a localization]
For $a\in A$,
\[
\frac{a}{1}=0\text{ in }A_{\mathfrak p}
\]
if and only if there exists $s\notin\mathfrak p$ such that
\[
sa=0\text{ in }A.
\]
\end{proposition}

Thus an element can vanish in the local ring without being globally zero: it is enough that
some element nonvanishing at $\mathfrak p$ kills it.  Geometrically, the element vanishes on
some principal neighborhood of the point.

\section{Maps of local rings}

Let $\varphi:A\to B$ be a ring homomorphism and let $\mathfrak q\in\Spec B$.  Set
\[
\mathfrak p=\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q).
\]
Every element of $A\setminus\mathfrak p$ maps outside $\mathfrak q$, so there is a canonical
localized homomorphism
\[
\varphi_{\mathfrak q}:A_{\mathfrak p}\longrightarrow B_{\mathfrak q}.
\]

\begin{definition}[Local homomorphism]
A homomorphism of local rings
\[
(R,\mathfrak m)\longrightarrow(S,\mathfrak n)
\]
is \emph{local} if the inverse image of $\mathfrak n$ is $\mathfrak m$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
The map $A_{\mathfrak p}\to B_{\mathfrak q}$ above is local and induces an injective field
homomorphism
\[
\kappa(\mathfrak p)\longrightarrow\kappa(\mathfrak q).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An element $a/s$ maps into $\mathfrak qB_{\mathfrak q}$ exactly when
$\varphi(a)\in\mathfrak q$, equivalently $a\in\mathfrak p$.  Hence the inverse image of
the maximal ideal is $\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}$.  Quotienting produces a unital field
homomorphism between residue fields, and every unital field homomorphism is injective.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch11_local_rings_residue_fields/figures/vi11_local_map.tex}

\section{The local exact sequence}

Every local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$ sits in the canonical short exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow\mathfrak m
\longrightarrow R
\longrightarrow R/\mathfrak m
\longrightarrow0.
\]
For $R=A_{\mathfrak p}$ this becomes
\[
0\longrightarrow\mathfrak pA_{\mathfrak p}
\longrightarrow A_{\mathfrak p}
\longrightarrow\kappa(\mathfrak p)
\longrightarrow0.
\]
The maximal ideal consists of local functions vanishing at the point, while the quotient is
their value field.

\section{Local rings can reveal geometry invisible globally}

Two nearby points on the same reducible scheme can have very different local rings.  On
\[
X=\Spec k[x,y]/(xy),
\]
the origin sees both components, so its local ring has zero divisors.  At a point on the
$x$-axis with $x\neq0$, the element $x$ becomes invertible; the relation $xy=0$ then forces
$y=0$, and locally the other component disappears.  This is the simplest model of how local
algebra isolates the geometry actually passing through a point.

\section{Common pitfalls}

Keep the following distinctions explicit:
\begin{itemize}
\item $A_f$ is the ring on a principal open $D(f)$; $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is the local ring
      obtained by inverting every element outside $\mathfrak p$.
\item $A/\mathfrak p$ is a domain, but it need not be a field unless $\mathfrak p$ is
      maximal.  The residue field is $\Frac(A/\mathfrak p)$.
\item A closed point need not be rational over the chosen base field.
\item A local ring need not be a domain, reduced, Noetherian, or zero-dimensional.
\item A one-point spectrum need not be a field: $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ is the
      standard counterexample.
\item Localization can make a component disappear because elements outside the chosen prime
      become units.
\item If $\mathfrak p\subsetneq\mathfrak q$, the natural map of local rings goes
      $A_{\mathfrak q}\to A_{\mathfrak p}$, not the other way around.
\item There is generally no canonical residue-field map associated merely to a specialization
      $\mathfrak p\subsetneq\mathfrak q$; residue-field maps arise naturally from morphisms of
      schemes/ring maps together with chosen points above one another.
\end{itemize}
