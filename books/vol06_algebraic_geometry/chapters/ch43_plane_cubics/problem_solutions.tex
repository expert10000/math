% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-01
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.1}
\begin{solution}
The rational function \(\ell/m\) on \(E\) is regular and nonzero away from the line sections.  At each point \(P\), its order is \(I_P(E,L)-I_P(E,M)\).  Therefore
\[
\operatorname{div}_E(\ell/m)=(L\cdot E)-(M\cdot E).
\]
In particular every line section represents the same hyperplane class \(H\), with tangency automatically included through intersection multiplicity.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-02
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.2}
\begin{solution}
Let the tangent at \(P\) meet \(E\) in \(2P+R\).  For a flex origin \(O\), every line section is linearly equivalent to \(3O\), so
\[
2P+R\sim3O.
\]
Thus \(2P=-R\) in the group law.  To construct \(-R\), draw the line through \(R,O\); if its residual point is \(S\), then \(R+O+S\sim3O\), hence \(S=-R=2P\).
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-03
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.3}
\begin{solution}
Let the tangent at \(O\) have intersection divisor \(2O+A\).  Draw the line through \(P\) and \(A\), with residual point \(B\).  Since both are line sections,
\[
2O+A\sim H\sim P+A+B.
\]
Cancel \(A\) to get \(P+B\sim2O\).  Applying \(P\mapsto[P-O]\), this is \([B-O]=-[P-O]\), so \(B\) is the inverse of \(P\) for the group law based at the arbitrary origin \(O\).
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-04
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.4}
\begin{solution}
For distinct points the joining secant is unique; in the repeated-point case smoothness gives a unique tangent.  Bézout then determines a residual intersection scheme of degree one after the prescribed multiplicities are removed.  The second line through that residual point and \(O\) is again unique in the same sense.  Algebraically the output \(S\) is characterized by \(P+Q\sim O+S\), and degree-one rigidity shows that no different point \(S'\) can satisfy the same relation.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-05
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.5}
\begin{solution}
Collinearity gives \(P+Q+R\sim3O\).  Hence
\[
(P-O)+(Q-O)+(R-O)\sim0,
\]
so in \(\mathrm{Pic}^0(E)\),
\[
\alpha_O(R)=-\alpha_O(P)-\alpha_O(Q).
\]
Thus the third collinear point is the negative of the group sum of the first two, which is the conceptual content of the familiar reflect-the-third-point rule.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-06
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.6}
\begin{solution}
In characteristic different from \(2\), a finite point is two-torsion iff it equals its inverse, hence iff \(y=0\).  On \(y^2=x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)\), the nonzero two-torsion points are
\[
(0,0),\quad(1,0),\quad(-1,0).
\]
Together with \(O\), these four points form \(E[2]\); over an algebraically closed field of characteristic not \(2\), \(E[2]\simeq(\mathbb Z/2)^2\).
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-07
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.7}
\begin{solution}
For \(P=(x,y)\), \(y\neq0\), the tangent slope is
\[
m=\frac{3x^2+A}{2y}.
\]
The third tangent intersection has \(x\)-coordinate \(m^2-2x\), and reflection across the \(x\)-axis preserves this coordinate.  Therefore
\[
x(2P)=m^2-2x,\qquad
y(2P)=m\bigl(x-x(2P)\bigr)-y.
\]
These are obtained from the secant formulas by the tangent limit.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-08
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.8}
\begin{solution}
Choose a flex as origin.  A point \(P\) is a flex iff its tangent section is \(3P\).  Since all line sections are equivalent to \(3O\), every flex satisfies \(3(P-O)\sim0\), so it is \(3\)-torsion.  Conversely, the standard flex/three-torsion correspondence identifies flexes with \(E[3]\).  When \(\operatorname{char}k\neq3\), multiplication by \(3\) is separable of degree \(9\), hence \(E[3](\bar k)\) has nine points.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-09
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.9}
\begin{solution}
First repeatedly use the two-point reduction to obtain
\[
P_1+P_2+P_3\sim P+2O,\qquad
Q_1+Q_2+Q_3\sim Q+2O.
\]
Then \(D\sim P-Q\).  Construct \(-Q=B\) geometrically, so \(B-O\sim-(Q-O)\), and reduce \(P+B\sim O+S\).  It follows that
\[
D\sim P-Q\sim S-O.
\]
This gives an explicit secant/tangent algorithm for reducing an arbitrary displayed degree-zero divisor to one point minus \(O\).
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-10
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.10}
\begin{solution}
The normalization of a nodal cubic is \(\mathbb P^1\), and the node has two preimages corresponding to its two branches.  The smooth locus therefore identifies with \(\mathbb P^1\) minus two points, hence with \(\mathbb G_m\) after a coordinate change.  Under a suitable normalization parameter, the generalized chord law becomes multiplication; this is the multiplicative degeneration of the elliptic group.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-11
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.11}
\begin{solution}
A cusp is unibranch, so its normalization \(\mathbb P^1\) has only one point above the singularity.  Removing it leaves \(\mathbb A^1\).  With a suitable normalization parameter the generalized chord law becomes addition, so the smooth locus carries the group \(\mathbb G_a\).  This is the additive degeneration of elliptic addition.
\end{solution}

% VI43-DETAILED-PROBLEM-12
\subsection*{Problem VI.43.12}
\begin{solution}
Every line and tangent used in the two-line construction is defined over \(k\) when the input points are \(k\)-rational.  Restricting the cubic to such a line gives a cubic polynomial over \(k\) with two known roots counted with multiplicity, so the residual root is also defined over \(k\).  Repeating this for the second line proves the final output lies in \(E(k)\).
\end{solution}
