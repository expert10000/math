VI/44 ended with a symbolic calculation.  For the standard quadratic Cremona transformation
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  \sigma:\PP^2&\dashrightarrow\PP^2,\\
  [x:y:z]&\longmapsto[yz:xz:xy].
  \end{aligned}
\]
the pullback of a general target line behaves numerically like
\[
  2H-E_1-E_2-E_3.
\]
The symbols \(E_i\) were deliberately left unexplained: the plane itself contains no such divisors.  The present chapter constructs the surface on which they become genuine curves.

A \emph{blow-up} replaces a subvariety by the projectivized family of normal directions along it.  For a point on a smooth surface this is especially concrete.  The point disappears and is replaced by a copy of \(\PP^1\), one point for each tangent direction through the original center.  This exceptional curve remembers precisely the directional data that a rational map loses at a base point.

For the origin of \(\mathbb A^2\), the blow-up is the incidence surface
\[
  \widetilde{\mathbb A^2}
  =\bigl\{((x,y),[s:t])\in\mathbb A^2\times\PP^1:xt=ys\bigr\}.
\]
Away from \((0,0)\), the direction \([s:t]=[x:y]\) is forced, so the projection to \(\mathbb A^2\) is an isomorphism.  Over the origin, the equation becomes \(0=0\), leaving the whole \(\PP^1\).  The blow-up therefore changes nothing away from the center but separates all directions through it.

The chapter develops this local model first, then gives the intrinsic Rees-algebra construction
\[
  \Bl_ZX=\Proj_X\!\left(\bigoplus_{n\ge0}\IIcal_Z^n\right).
\]
After that, strict transforms and multiplicities turn local equations into divisor formulas.  For the blow-up of \(\PP^2\) at a point,
\[
  \Pic(\Bl_p\PP^2)\cong\ZZ H\oplus\ZZ E,
\]
with
\[
  H^2=1,\qquad H\cdot E=0,\qquad E^2=-1.
\]
Finally we blow up the three base points of \(\sigma\).  The rational map becomes regular, the symbolic classes \(E_i\) become actual exceptional divisors, and the degree formula from VI/44 becomes an intersection calculation on the resolved surface.

\section*{Standing conventions}
Unless stated otherwise, \(k\) is an algebraically closed field.  Surfaces are integral, separated, and of finite type over \(k\).  When local coordinates at a smooth point are used, the completed or local ring is regular of dimension two.  The blow-up of an ideal sheaf \(\IIcal\) means the relative Proj of its Rees algebra.  For a curve \(C\) through a blown-up point, \(\widetilde C\) denotes its strict transform, while \(\pi^*C\) denotes its total transform as a Cartier divisor whenever this notation is defined.

\section{Blowing up the affine plane at the origin}

We begin with the local model that controls every blow-up of a smooth surface at a point.

\begin{definition}[Incidence model of the point blow-up]\label{def:vi45-incidence-blowup}
The blow-up of \(\mathbb A^2=\Spec k[x,y]\) at the origin is
\[
  \Bl_0\mathbb A^2
  :=\bigl\{((x,y),[s:t])\in\mathbb A^2\times\PP^1:xt=ys\bigr\}.
\]
The projection onto the first factor is denoted
\[
  \pi:\Bl_0\mathbb A^2\longrightarrow\mathbb A^2.
\]
\end{definition}

The equation says that \([s:t]\) records the direction of the vector \((x,y)\).  If \((x,y)\neq(0,0)\), then \([s:t]=[x:y]\) is uniquely determined.  Over the origin there is no constraint on \([s:t]\).

\begin{proposition}[The two affine charts]
The blow-up \(\Bl_0\mathbb A^2\) is covered by two affine planes.

On \(s\neq0\), write \(u=t/s\).  Then
\[
  y=xu,
\]
so this chart has coordinates \((x,u)\) and projection
\[
  (x,u)\longmapsto(x,xu).
\]
On \(t\neq0\), write \(v=s/t\).  Then
\[
  x=yv,
\]
so this chart has coordinates \((v,y)\) and projection
\[
  (v,y)\longmapsto(yv,y).
\]
On the overlap, \(u=v^{-1}\) and \(y=xu\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On \(s\neq0\), divide the incidence equation \(xt=ys\) by \(s\) to obtain \(xu=y\).  Thus \(y\) is eliminated and the chart is \(\Spec k[x,u]\).  The second chart is symmetric.  Where both \(s,t\neq0\), one has \(u=t/s\) and \(v=s/t\), hence \(uv=1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Exceptional divisor]\label{def:vi45-exceptional-divisor}
The inverse image of the center,
\[
  E:=\pi^{-1}(0),
\]
is the \emph{exceptional divisor}.  In the incidence model it is
\[
  E=\{(0,0)\}\times\PP^1\cong\PP^1.
\]
\end{definition}

In the \((x,u)\)-chart, \(E\) is the divisor \(x=0\); in the \((v,y)\)-chart, it is \(y=0\).  The coordinate \(u\) records the slope \(y/x\), while \(v\) records the reciprocal slope.  The two charts on \(E\) are exactly the two standard affine charts of \(\PP^1\).

\section{Why the exceptional divisor is a space of directions}

Fix a line through the origin,
\[
  L_a:\ y=ax.
\]
In the \((x,u)\)-chart its inverse image is defined by
\[
  x(u-a)=0.
\]
This equation has two components: the exceptional divisor \(x=0\) and the closure \(u=a\) of the inverse image of \(L_a\setminus\{0\}\).  The latter is the strict transform of the line.  It meets \(E\) at the single point \((x,u)=(0,a)\), so the point of \(E\) records the direction of \(L_a\).

\begin{proposition}[Blow-up is unchanged off the center]\label{prop:vi45-isomorphism-off-center}
The restriction
\[
  \pi:\Bl_0\mathbb A^2\setminus E\longrightarrow\mathbb A^2\setminus\{0\}
\]
is an isomorphism.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The inverse sends \((x,y)\neq(0,0)\) to \(((x,y),[x:y])\).  This map is regular on the standard opens \(x\neq0\) and \(y\neq0\), and the two formulas agree on their overlap.
\end{proof}

Thus a blow-up is not a local surgery that destroys the surrounding surface.  It is a birational modification supported exactly at the center.

\section{The intrinsic Rees-algebra construction}

The incidence equation is not the definition in general.  Let \(X\) be a scheme and \(Z\subset X\) a closed subscheme with quasi-coherent ideal sheaf \(\IIcal\).  The graded \(\OO_X\)-algebra
\[
  \mathcal R(\IIcal)
  :=\OO_X\oplus\IIcal\oplus\IIcal^2\oplus\cdots
\]
is the \emph{Rees algebra} of \(\IIcal\).  Here the summand \(\IIcal^n\) is placed in degree \(n\).

\begin{definition}[Blow-up along a closed subscheme]\label{def:vi45-rees-blowup}
The blow-up of \(X\) along \(Z\) is
\[
  \Bl_ZX:=\Proj_X\mathcal R(\IIcal),
\]
with its canonical projective morphism
\[
  \pi:\Bl_ZX\longrightarrow X.
\]
\end{definition}

For an affine scheme \(X=\Spec A\) and ideal \(I\subset A\), this is the ordinary Proj of the graded Rees algebra
\[
  A[It]=A\oplus It\oplus I^2t^2\oplus\cdots\subset A[t].
\]
If \(I=(f_0,\ldots,f_r)\), the blow-up comes with a natural closed immersion into \(X\times\PP^r\), and the projective coordinates record the ratios of the generators of \(I\).  For \(I=(x,y)\), this recovers \(xt=ys\).

\begin{theorem}[Universal property of the blow-up]\label{thm:vi45-universal-property}
Let \(\pi:\Bl_ZX\to X\) be the blow-up of the ideal sheaf \(\IIcal\).  If \(f:Y\to X\) is a morphism such that
\[
  \IIcal\OO_Y
\]
is an invertible ideal sheaf on \(Y\), then there is a unique morphism
\[
  \widetilde f:Y\longrightarrow\Bl_ZX
\]
with \(f=\pi\circ\widetilde f\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An invertible quotient of the pullback of the degree-one piece of the Rees algebra determines, by the universal property of relative Proj, a morphism to \(\Proj_X\mathcal R(\IIcal)\).  The construction is forced by the invertible ideal \(\IIcal\OO_Y\), giving uniqueness.
\end{proof}

This property explains why blow-ups resolve base ideals: after blowing up the ideal, the ideal becomes locally principal and therefore defines a line bundle rather than an indeterminate ratio.

\section{Total transforms and strict transforms}

Let \(\pi:\widetilde X\to X\) be the blow-up of a smooth surface at a point \(p\), with exceptional divisor \(E\).  If \(C\subset X\) is a curve, two different transforms appear.

\begin{definition}[Total and strict transforms]\label{def:vi45-transforms}
The \emph{total transform} of a Cartier divisor \(C\) is the pullback divisor \(\pi^*C\).  The \emph{strict transform} \(\widetilde C\) is the closure in \(\widetilde X\) of
\[
  \pi^{-1}(C\setminus\{p\}).
\]
\end{definition}

The total transform usually contains the exceptional divisor; the strict transform removes that forced exceptional component.

Suppose local parameters at \(p\) are \(x,y\), and \(C\) has local equation
\[
  f(x,y)=f_m(x,y)+f_{m+1}(x,y)+\cdots,
\]
where \(f_j\) is homogeneous of degree \(j\) and \(f_m\neq0\).  Then \(m=\mult_p(C)\).

\begin{proposition}[Multiplicity becomes exceptional multiplicity]\label{prop:vi45-total-transform-formula}
With the preceding notation,
\[
  \boxed{\pi^*C=\widetilde C+mE.}
\]
In the chart \(y=xu\), one has
\[
  f(x,xu)=x^m\bigl(f_m(1,u)+x f_{m+1}(1,u)+\cdots\bigr).
\]
The factor \(x^m\) is the exceptional component of the total transform.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every monomial of total degree at least \(m\) acquires at least a factor \(x^m\) after substituting \(y=xu\), and the degree-\(m\) part contributes exactly \(x^m f_m(1,u)\).  Since \(x=0\) defines \(E\) in this chart, the order along \(E\) is \(m\).  Removing that factor gives the local equation of the strict transform.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Tangent cone on the exceptional divisor]\label{prop:vi45-tangent-cone}
In the chart \(y=xu\), the intersection \(\widetilde C\cap E\) is cut out by
\[
  f_m(1,u)=0.
\]
Thus the points where \(\widetilde C\) meets \(E\), counted with multiplicity and completed by the second chart, are exactly the projective tangent directions of \(C\) at \(p\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The strict-transform equation is the factor in parentheses above.  Restricting to \(E\), where \(x=0\), leaves \(f_m(1,u)=0\).  Homogenizing across the two charts gives the zero scheme of the tangent cone \(f_m(s,t)\) in \(\PP^1\).
\end{proof}

\section{Blowing up singular plane curves}

The tangent-cone proposition gives an immediate geometric use of blow-ups.  If a curve has several tangent directions at a singular point, those directions become different points of the exceptional divisor.

For example, consider an ordinary node whose quadratic tangent cone factors into two distinct lines.  The two branches of the strict transform meet \(E\) at two distinct points, so the blow-up separates them.  For the cusp
\[
  C:y^2=x^3,
\]
the chart \(y=xu\) gives
\[
  x^2(u^2-x)=0.
\]
The strict transform \(u^2=x\) is smooth, but it meets \(E:x=0\) with multiplicity two.  Thus one blow-up removes the singularity of the curve while still remembering its high-order tangency to the exceptional divisor.

\begin{proposition}[Ordinary multiple points separate by direction]
Suppose the tangent cone of a plane curve singularity of multiplicity \(m\) is a product of \(m\) distinct linear forms.  Then after blowing up the singular point, the strict transform meets the exceptional divisor in \(m\) distinct points.  In particular, the local branches are separated at the first blow-up.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:vi45-tangent-cone}, the intersection with \(E\) is the projectivized zero set of the tangent cone.  Distinct linear factors give distinct points of \(\PP^1\).
\end{proof}

This is the first glimpse of embedded resolution: repeated blow-ups can replace a complicated singular curve by a configuration of smooth strict transforms and exceptional curves with controlled intersections.

\section{The Picard group of the blown-up plane}

Now let
\[
  \pi:S=\Bl_p\PP^2\longrightarrow\PP^2
\]
be the blow-up of a point.  Write
\[
  H:=\pi^*[\text{line}],
\]
and let \(E\) be the exceptional divisor.

\begin{theorem}[Picard group after one point blow-up]\label{thm:vi45-picard-one-point}
There is an isomorphism
\[
  \Pic(S)\cong\ZZ H\oplus\ZZ E.
\]
Every divisor class on \(S\) can be written uniquely as
\[
  dH-mE,
\qquad d,m\in\ZZ.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A divisor on \(S\) not supported on \(E\) pushes forward to a divisor on \(\PP^2\), whose class is a multiple of a line.  Subtracting the pullback of that class leaves a divisor supported on \(E\), hence an integral multiple of \(E\).  Uniqueness follows from the intersection numbers computed below: if \(dH+mE\sim0\), intersecting first with \(H\) gives \(d=0\), and then with \(E\) gives \(m=0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Intersection form]\label{thm:vi45-intersection-form}
On \(S=\Bl_p\PP^2\),
\[
  \boxed{H^2=1,\qquad H\cdot E=0,\qquad E^2=-1.}
\]
Consequently
\[
  (dH-mE)\cdot(d'H-m'E)=dd'-mm'.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose a line in \(\PP^2\) avoiding \(p\).  Its inverse image is isomorphic to the line and does not meet \(E\), so \(H\cdot E=0\).  Two general such lines meet once, giving \(H^2=1\).

Now choose two distinct lines through \(p\).  Their strict transforms are disjoint because their only intersection downstairs was \(p\), and on the blow-up they meet \(E\) at two different direction points.  Each strict transform has class \(H-E\).  Hence
\[
  0=(H-E)^2=H^2-2H\cdot E+E^2=1+E^2,
\]
so \(E^2=-1\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Class of a strict transform]\label{prop:vi45-strict-class}
Let \(C\subset\PP^2\) be a degree-\(d\) curve with multiplicity \(m\) at \(p\), and assume \(C\) does not contain an exceptional artifact because it lives downstairs.  Then
\[
  [\widetilde C]=dH-mE.
\]
Moreover,
\[
  \widetilde C\cdot E=m.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Downstairs, \([C]=d[\text{line}]\), so \(\pi^*C\sim dH\).  Proposition~\ref{prop:vi45-total-transform-formula} gives \(\pi^*C=\widetilde C+mE\), hence \([\widetilde C]=dH-mE\).  Intersecting with \(E\) yields
\[
  (dH-mE)\cdot E=m.
\]
\end{proof}

The final equality is a global version of the tangent-cone calculation: the total number of tangent directions, counted with multiplicity, is the multiplicity of the original curve at the blown-up point.

\section{Canonical divisors and the exceptional correction}

Blow-ups also change canonical divisors in a controlled way.  Let \(\pi:\widetilde X\to X\) blow up a smooth point of a smooth surface.

\begin{theorem}[Canonical divisor under a point blow-up]\label{thm:vi45-canonical-class}
If \(E\) is the exceptional divisor, then
\[
  \boxed{K_{\widetilde X}=\pi^*K_X+E.}
\]
For \(S=\Bl_p\PP^2\), this becomes
\[
  K_S=-3H+E.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Take local coordinates \((x,y)\) at the center.  On the blow-up chart \(y=xu\),
\[
  dx\wedge dy
  =dx\wedge(u\,dx+x\,du)
  =x\,dx\wedge du.
\]
Thus the pullback of a local generator of the canonical bundle vanishes to order one along \(E:x=0\).  This gives the discrepancy \(+E\).  Since \(K_{\PP^2}=-3[\text{line}]\), the displayed formula follows for the blown-up plane.
\end{proof}

The coefficient \(+1\) is one of the simplest examples of a discrepancy in birational geometry.  Here it is visible directly from the Jacobian of the chart substitution.

\section{Blowing up three noncollinear points}

Return to the three coordinate points
\[
  p_1=[1:0:0],\qquad p_2=[0:1:0],\qquad p_3=[0:0:1].
\]
Let
\[
  \pi:S=\Bl_{p_1,p_2,p_3}\PP^2\longrightarrow\PP^2
\]
be the blow-up of all three points.  Since the centers are distinct, the order of these three blow-ups does not matter up to canonical isomorphism.  Let \(E_i\) be the exceptional divisor over \(p_i\).

\begin{theorem}[Picard lattice after three point blow-ups]\label{thm:vi45-three-point-lattice}
The Picard group is
\[
  \Pic(S)\cong\ZZ H\oplus\ZZ E_1\oplus\ZZ E_2\oplus\ZZ E_3,
\]
with intersection form
\[
  H^2=1,\qquad H\cdot E_i=0,\qquad E_i\cdot E_j=0\ (i\neq j),
  \qquad E_i^2=-1.
\]
If \(C\) has degree \(d\) and multiplicities \(m_i\) at the three points, then
\[
  [\widetilde C]=dH-m_1E_1-m_2E_2-m_3E_3.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the one-point statement successively.  Because later blow-up centers do not lie on the earlier exceptional divisors, the exceptional curves remain pairwise disjoint and retain self-intersection \(-1\).  The strict-transform formula is likewise obtained by subtracting the multiplicity at each center.
\end{proof}

Let \(L_x=V_+(x)\), \(L_y=V_+(y)\), \(L_z=V_+(z)\).  Their strict transforms have classes
\[
  \widetilde L_x=H-E_2-E_3,
  \qquad
  \widetilde L_y=H-E_1-E_3,
  \qquad
  \widetilde L_z=H-E_1-E_2.
\]
Each has self-intersection \(-1\), and the three are pairwise disjoint on \(S\).

\section{Resolving the standard quadratic transformation}

The standard Cremona map is defined by the net
\[
  \langle yz,xz,xy\rangle.
\]
Every member is a conic through \(p_1,p_2,p_3\), so after blowing up those base points the corresponding divisor class is
\[
  D=2H-E_1-E_2-E_3.
\]
The key point is that the proper transform of this linear system has no base points.

\begin{theorem}[Resolution of the standard Cremona map]\label{thm:vi45-resolve-cremona}
Let \(S=\Bl_{p_1,p_2,p_3}\PP^2\).  The rational map
\[
  \sigma[x:y:z]=[yz:xz:xy]
\]
lifts to a morphism
\[
  \widetilde\sigma:S\longrightarrow\PP^2
\]
defined by the base-point-free linear system \(|2H-E_1-E_2-E_3|\).

For each \(i\), the exceptional divisor \(E_i\) maps isomorphically onto the coordinate line opposite \(p_i\), while each strict transform \(\widetilde L_x,\widetilde L_y,\widetilde L_z\) is contracted to the corresponding coordinate point.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Near \(p_1\), use affine coordinates \(u=y/x\), \(v=z/x\).  Then
\[
  \sigma[1:u:v]=[uv:v:u].
\]
On the blow-up chart \(v=ut\), this becomes
\[
  [u^2t:ut:u]=[ut:t:1],
\]
which is regular even at \(u=0\).  On \(E_1\), where \(u=0\), the image is \([0:t:1]\), varying along the target line \(X=0\).  The complementary blow-up chart covers the missing point of that line.  Thus the indeterminacy at \(p_1\) is removed and \(E_1\) maps isomorphically to \(X=0\).  The same calculation applies cyclically at \(p_2,p_3\).

Away from the base points the blow-up is an isomorphism and the map agrees with \(\sigma\), so these local extensions glue to a global morphism.  Finally, on \(L_x\setminus\{p_2,p_3\}\), one has \(\sigma=[1:0:0]=p_1\), so \(\widetilde L_x\) is contracted to \(p_1\), and similarly for the other two coordinate lines.
\end{proof}

There is a numerical check.  Since
\[
  D\cdot E_i=1,
\]
the map has degree one on each \(E_i\).  Meanwhile
\[
  D\cdot(H-E_2-E_3)=2-1-1=0,
\]
so \(\widetilde L_x\) is contracted, exactly as the coordinate calculation shows.

\section{The lifted Cremona involution on the blown-up surface}

The morphism \(\widetilde\sigma:S\to\PP^2\) still contracts three curves.  Blow up the three target coordinate points as well.  Because the original Cremona map is an involution, the source and target blow-ups are naturally isomorphic, and the rational involution becomes a genuine automorphism
\[
  \widehat\sigma:S\xrightarrow{\sim}S.
\]
It exchanges exceptional divisors with strict transforms of opposite coordinate lines.

\begin{theorem}[Cremona action on the Picard lattice]\label{thm:vi45-cremona-picard-action}
For the lifted involution \(\widehat\sigma\), the pullback action on \(\Pic(S)\) is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  H&\longmapsto 2H-E_1-E_2-E_3,\\
  E_1&\longmapsto H-E_2-E_3,\\
  E_2&\longmapsto H-E_1-E_3,\\
  E_3&\longmapsto H-E_1-E_2.
\end{aligned}
\]
This linear transformation is an involution and preserves the intersection form.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A general target line pulls back to a conic through all three source base points, giving the first formula.  The target exceptional divisor over \(p_1\) pulls back to the strict transform of the source line \(x=0\), because that line was contracted to \(p_1\); this gives the second formula, and the remaining two are cyclic.

Applying the transformation twice to the basis returns each basis element.  Preservation of the intersection form can be checked on the basis using
\[
  H^2=1,\qquad E_i^2=-1,
\]
and all mixed products zero.  Geometrically, it must also preserve intersections because \(\widehat\sigma\) is an automorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Degree and multiplicity transformation]\label{prop:vi45-degree-multiplicity-action}
Let a curve have strict-transform class
\[
  C\sim dH-m_1E_1-m_2E_2-m_3E_3,
\]
and suppose it contains none of the three coordinate lines as a component.  Then its noncontracted Cremona transform has coefficients
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
  d'&=2d-m_1-m_2-m_3,\\
  m_1'&=d-m_2-m_3,\\
  m_2'&=d-m_1-m_3,\\
  m_3'&=d-m_1-m_2.
\end{aligned}}
\]
In particular, the degree formula of VI/44 is the \(H\)-coefficient of the Picard-lattice action.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:vi45-cremona-picard-action} to
\[
  dH-m_1E_1-m_2E_2-m_3E_3
\]
and collect coefficients of \(H,E_1,E_2,E_3\).  Since the transformation is involutive, the same formulas recover \((d,m_1,m_2,m_3)\) from the primed data.
\end{proof}

\section{Blowing up a base ideal resolves a projective rational map}

The Cremona calculation is a special case of a general mechanism.  Suppose \(X\) is integral and
\[
  \phi:X\dashrightarrow\PP^r
\]
is represented by sections \(f_0,\ldots,f_r\) of a line bundle after choosing a common twist.  Their common vanishing defines a base ideal \(\IIcal\).

\begin{theorem}[Resolution by the base ideal]\label{thm:vi45-base-ideal-resolution}
Let
\[
  \pi:\widetilde X=\Bl_{\IIcal}X\longrightarrow X
\]
be the blow-up of the base ideal of \(\phi=[f_0:\cdots:f_r]\).  Then the pullback ideal \(\IIcal\OO_{\widetilde X}\) is invertible, and the ratios of the pulled-back sections define a morphism
\[
  \widetilde\phi:\widetilde X\longrightarrow\PP^r
\]
that agrees with \(\phi\circ\pi\) over the original domain of definition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By construction of the blow-up, the pullback of the base ideal becomes the tautological invertible ideal.  Locally one generator of the ideal is chosen as a denominator on each standard Proj chart, and all ratios \(f_i/f_j\) become regular there.  These local projective-coordinate maps agree on overlaps and therefore glue to \(\widetilde\phi\).
\end{proof}

One should distinguish this statement from a complete resolution theorem.  Blowing up the base ideal resolves the given map to projective space, but when one wants a particularly smooth source or a factorization into point blow-ups, further normalization or repeated blow-ups may be required.

\section{Infinitely near points and repeated blow-ups}

After one blow-up, a transformed linear system can still have a base point on the exceptional divisor.  Such a point represents a direction at the original center and is not a point of the original surface.

\begin{definition}[Infinitely near point]\label{def:vi45-infinitely-near}
A point on an exceptional divisor created by a blow-up is called a point \emph{infinitely near} to the original center.  More generally, a point appearing on any surface obtained by a sequence of blow-ups is infinitely near to its image on the original surface.
\end{definition}

If a strict transform still has a singularity or a linear system still has a base point there, one blows up again.  The new exceptional divisor records directions through the infinitely near point.  Iterating produces a tree of exceptional curves whose intersection pattern records the history of the resolution.

For plane curve singularities this process leads toward embedded resolution.  For plane Cremona maps it leads to the complete cluster of proper and infinitely near base points.  The elementary three-proper-point quadratic transformation is the first nontrivial case where the entire process terminates after one blow-up at each base point.

\section{Exceptional curves and blow-downs}

A point blow-up introduces a smooth rational curve \(E\cong\PP^1\) with self-intersection \(-1\).  Conversely, under suitable hypotheses a smooth rational \((-1)\)-curve on a smooth surface can be contracted to a smooth point.  This is the geometric inverse of blowing up.

For the resolved Cremona transformation, this inverse viewpoint is essential.  Starting from
\[
  S=\Bl_{p_1,p_2,p_3}\PP^2,
\]
one can obtain the target plane by contracting the three disjoint \((-1)\)-curves
\[
  \widetilde L_x,
  \qquad
  \widetilde L_y,
  \qquad
  \widetilde L_z.
\]
Thus the standard quadratic transformation factors schematically as
\[
  \PP^2
  \xleftarrow{\ \pi\ }
  S
  \xrightarrow{\ \widetilde\sigma\ }
  \PP^2,
\]
where the left arrow blows up three points and the right arrow blows down three different curves.

This factorization is the basic pattern of birational surface geometry: replace an indeterminate rational map by a diagram of honest morphisms, then study those morphisms through exceptional curves and the intersection lattice.

\section*{Bridge to VI/46}

The blow-up chapter completes the first birational-geometry arc of Volume VI.  Weil divisors, Cartier divisors, class groups, line bundles, Picard groups, plane cubics, Cremona transformations, and blow-ups now fit into one chain:
\[
  \begin{gathered}
  \text{local equations}\Longrightarrow\text{divisor classes}\\
  \Longrightarrow\text{birational modifications}\Longrightarrow\text{intersection bookkeeping}.
  \end{gathered}
\]
The symbolic exceptional classes of VI/44 have become actual curves, and the indeterminacy of the standard Cremona map has been replaced by a diagram of morphisms.

The next chapter changes direction.  VI/46 begins the cohomological arc with \emph{flabby sheaves}.  The geometric constructions developed here will later return through line bundles, divisor sequences, and cohomology, but the immediate task is categorical: identify sheaves whose restriction maps are surjective and understand why they are acyclic tools for computing derived invariants.
