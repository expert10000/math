\subsection*{VI.22.P14: Scheme-theoretic union}
\begin{problem}\label{prob:vi22-p14}
Show that the smallest affine closed subscheme containing both $V(I)$ and $V(J)$ is defined by $I\cap J$.
\end{problem}

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
% VI22-FULL-P14
\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
This distinguishes the order-reversing correspondence between ideals and affine closed subschemes.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
A closed subscheme defined by $K$ contains the one defined by $I$ exactly when $K\subseteq I$.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Translate containment of closed subschemes into factorization of quotient maps.
\item Require $K\subseteq I$ and $K\subseteq J$.
\item Find the largest ideal satisfying both conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{solution}
Write
\[
 Z_I=\Spec(A/I),\qquad Z_K=\Spec(A/K).
\]
The inclusion $Z_I\subseteq Z_K$ holds scheme-theoretically exactly when the quotient map
$A\twoheadrightarrow A/I$ factors through $A/K$.  By the quotient universal property, this
happens exactly when
\[
 K\subseteq I.
\]
Therefore a closed subscheme $V(K)$ contains both $V(I)$ and $V(J)$ precisely when
\[
 K\subseteq I\cap J.
\]
Because larger ideals define smaller closed subschemes, the \emph{smallest} closed subscheme
containing both is obtained by taking the largest possible such $K$, namely
\[
 K=I\cap J.
\]
Hence the scheme-theoretic union is
\[
 \Spec(A/(I\cap J)).
\]
Its support is $V(I\cap J)=V(I)\cup V(J)$, agreeing with the ordinary topological union.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
The inclusions reverse.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Compute the scheme-theoretic union of the coordinate axes and compare it with the intersection calculation.
