\begin{example}[Advanced: every sheaf on a discrete space is a product of stalks]
Let $X$ be discrete.  Cover $U\subseteq X$ by singleton opens.  There are no nontrivial
overlap conditions, so the sheaf axiom gives
\[
\mathcal F(U)\xrightarrow{\sim}\prod_{x\in U}\mathcal F(\{x\})
\cong\prod_{x\in U}\mathcal F_x.
\]
Thus the category of sheaves of abelian groups on a discrete $X$ is equivalent to the
product category $\prod_{x\in X}\mathbf{Ab}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: the naive constant presheaf on an irreducible space]
Suppose every two nonempty opens of $X$ meet.  Then the naive constant presheaf $A$ (with
value $0$ on $\varnothing$ in the abelian-group case) is actually a sheaf: in any open
cover, compatibility forces all local constants to be equal because every pair of nonempty
cover members meets.  Thus failure of the naive constant presheaf is controlled by the
connectedness pattern of covers, not by constancy alone.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: a Sierpinski space]
Let $X=\{\eta,x\}$ with opens $\varnothing,\{\eta\},X$.  A presheaf of abelian groups is a
single restriction map $A=\mathcal F(X)\to B=\mathcal F(\{\eta\})$, together with
$\mathcal F(\varnothing)=0$.  Every cover of $X$ contains $X$ itself, so no additional
gluing condition appears: every such presheaf is a sheaf.  The stalks are
$\mathcal F_x\cong A$ and $\mathcal F_\eta\cong B$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: skyscraper sheaf on a non-$T_1$ space]
For the same Sierpinski space, place a skyscraper at $\eta$.  Every neighborhood of $x$
contains $\eta$, so the stalk at $x$ is also $A$.  This shows why the common slogan
``skyscraper stalks vanish away from the support point'' silently uses a separation
hypothesis.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: local holomorphic logarithms on $\CC^*$]
Let $\mathcal L(U)$ be the set of holomorphic functions $g$ on $U\subseteq\CC^*$ satisfying
$e^g=z$.  Restriction gives a sheaf of sets.  Every point has a neighborhood admitting such
a branch, but $\mathcal L(\CC^*)=\varnothing$.  Thus nonempty stalks do not imply a global
section; gluing can be obstructed by incompatible choices around a loop.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: local square roots]
On $X=\CC^*$, let $\mathcal R(U)$ be holomorphic $g$ with $g^2=z$.  Again the stalk at every
point is nonempty, while there is no global holomorphic square root on all of $\CC^*$.  On a
simply connected slit domain there are two global branches.  This is a concrete preview of
monodromy without requiring its formal theory.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: sheaf of local solutions]
Let $L$ be a linear differential operator on a manifold.  Define
\[
\mathcal S(U)=\{u\in C^\infty(U):Lu=0\}.
\]
Because $L$ is local, compatible local solutions glue and remain solutions.  Thus
$\mathcal S$ is a sheaf.  For $L=d/dx$ on an interval, $\mathcal S$ is the constant sheaf
$\underline\RR$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: germs of rational functions regular at a point]
Let $k$ be a field and consider the affine line informally through its classical points.  At
a point $a$, rational functions $f/g$ with $g(a)\neq0$ form a local ring under germ
equivalence.  This is the geometric pattern formalized in VI/11 by $k[t]_{(t-a)}$ and later
in VI/17 by the structure sheaf.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: stalk of a direct image]
For a continuous map $f:X\to Y$ and a sheaf $\mathcal F$ on $X$,
\[
(f_*\mathcal F)_y=\varinjlim_{y\in V}\mathcal F(f^{-1}V).
\]
This need not equal a single stalk of $\mathcal F$ because $f^{-1}(V)$ can contain many
points.  For the map from a two-point discrete space to a point,
$(f_*\mathcal F)_*=\mathcal F(X)\cong\mathcal F_p\times\mathcal F_q$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: support uses germs, not point values]
For the continuous function $f(x)=x$ on $\RR$, the point value at $0$ vanishes but the germ
$f_0$ is nonzero, because $f$ is not identically zero on any neighborhood of $0$.  Thus
\[
\{x:f_x\neq0\}=\RR=\operatorname{supp}(f),
\]
which is exactly the closure of the pointwise nonvanishing set $\RR\setminus\{0\}$.
This illustrates why support is naturally expressed by germs.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: sheaf condition on principal opens]
Let $A$ be a ring and imagine the basis $D(f)$ of $\Spec A$.  The rule $D(f)\mapsto A_f$
comes with restriction maps induced by further localization.  To construct a sheaf on the
whole spectrum, it is enough to verify locality and gluing on covers by principal opens.
VI/17 will carry out this program for the structure sheaf.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: a stalk sees only eventual behavior]
Let $X=\RR$ and define continuous functions $f_n$ that agree with $0$ on $(-1/n,1/n)$ but
behave arbitrarily outside.  Every $f_n$ represents the zero germ at $0$.  Thus an infinite
amount of global information can collapse to one stalk element.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: sheaf morphisms can be locally surjective but not sectionwise surjective]
The exponential map of sheaves of smooth functions
\[
\exp:C^\infty_\RR\longrightarrow(C^\infty_\RR)_{>0}
\]
is sectionwise surjective on intervals because positive smooth functions admit smooth
logarithms.  On a more general sheaf problem, stalkwise lifts can exist without a global
lift; the logarithm sheaf on $\CC^*$ illustrates the obstruction.  This is why VI/16 uses a
local, stalkwise notion of surjectivity.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: basis data do not determine a presheaf, but can determine a sheaf]
VI/12 showed that arbitrary presheaves may agree on a basis and differ on larger opens.
For sheaves the gluing axiom removes this freedom: if two sheaves have naturally isomorphic
restrictions to a basis and the isomorphisms commute with basis restrictions, the basis
criterion extends them uniquely to an isomorphism on all opens.
\end{example}
