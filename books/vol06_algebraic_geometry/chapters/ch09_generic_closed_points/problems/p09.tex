\begin{problem}[Separation axioms for spectra]
Prove:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every $\Spec A$ is $T_0$;
\item $\Spec A$ is $T_1$ iff every prime ideal is maximal.
\end{enumerate}
Give one example of a spectrum that is $T_1$ and one that is not.
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
It locates Zariski spectra precisely between ordinary separation axioms and explains why
closures of points carry algebraic information.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
For distinct primes, use an element in their set-theoretic difference.  For $T_1$, use the
closed-point criterion.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
A field has a one-point $T_1$ spectrum; $k[t]$ does not.

\begin{solution}
Let $\mathfrak p\neq\mathfrak q$.  Assume
$f\in\mathfrak q\setminus\mathfrak p$.  Then
\[
\mathfrak p\in D(f),\qquad \mathfrak q\notin D(f),
\]
so the two points are topologically distinguishable.  Thus $\Spec A$ is $T_0$.

A space is $T_1$ iff every singleton is closed.  In $\Spec A$ a singleton
$\{\mathfrak p\}$ is closed iff $\mathfrak p$ is maximal.  Therefore
\[
\Spec A\text{ is }T_1
\iff
\text{every prime of }A\text{ is maximal}.
\]
For a field $K$, $\Spec K$ is one point and hence $T_1$.  For $k[t]$, the generic point
$(0)$ is not closed, so the spectrum is not $T_1$.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
The $T_0$ property guarantees uniqueness of generic points, while failure of $T_1$ is what
allows nonclosed generic and intermediate points to exist.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Show $\Spec(A\times B)$ is $T_1$ iff both $\Spec A$ and $\Spec B$ are $T_1$.

\paragraph{Provenance.}
New structural problem extracted from the legacy closure and closed-point discussion.
