\paragraph{Solution to Challenge VI.18.C1 (The punctured affine plane).}
\begin{solution}
Let $A=k[x,y]$ and
\[
U=D(x)\cup D(y)=\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{(x,y)\}.
\]
By the sheaf equalizer for a two-open cover,
\[
\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong A_x\times_{A_{xy}}A_y.
\]
Because $A$ is a domain, view all three rings inside the fraction field $k(x,y)$. A compatible pair is then one rational function lying in both $A_x$ and $A_y$, so
\[
\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)=A_x\cap A_y\subseteq k(x,y).
\]

Take $r\in A_x\cap A_y$. Write
\[
r=\frac{p}{x^m}=\frac{q}{y^n},\qquad p,q\in A.
\]
Then $y^np=x^mq$. Since $k[x,y]$ is a UFD and $x,y$ are nonassociate prime elements, $x^m\mid p$ and $y^n\mid q$. Hence $p=x^mp_0$ and $r=p_0\in A$. The reverse inclusion $A\subseteq A_x\cap A_y$ is obvious. Therefore
\[
\boxed{\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)=k[x,y].}
\]

Now suppose $U$ were affine. Affine reconstruction would give
\[
U\cong\Spec\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)\cong\Spec k[x,y]=\mathbb A_k^2.
\]
Under the computed identification of global functions, the canonical map $U\to\Spec\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)$ is the original open immersion $U\hookrightarrow\mathbb A_k^2$. It omits the origin, so it is not surjective and cannot be an isomorphism. This contradiction proves that the punctured affine plane is not affine.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Solution to Challenge VI.18.C2 (Projective space is not affine).}
\begin{solution}
Cover $\mathbb P_k^n$ by the standard affine opens $U_i=D_+(x_i)$. On $U_0$ use coordinates
\[
t_j=x_j/x_0\qquad(1\le j\le n),
\]
so a global regular function restricts to a polynomial
\[
f(t_1,\dots,t_n)\in k[t_1,\dots,t_n].
\]
Fix $i\ge1$. On $U_i$ use coordinates
\[
s_0=x_0/x_i=1/t_i,
\qquad
s_j=x_j/x_i=t_j/t_i\quad(j\ne i).
\]
Write $f=\sum_{d=0}^N f_d$, where each $f_d$ is homogeneous of total degree $d$ in the $t_j$. After substituting the $U_i$ coordinates, the degree-$d$ part becomes
\[
f_d(t_1,\dots,t_n)=s_0^{-d}\,F_{d,i}(s_0,s_1,\dots,\widehat{s_i},\dots,s_n),
\]
where $F_{d,i}$ is a polynomial and the numerator is not divisible by $s_0$ unless the homogeneous piece is zero. If $N>0$, the highest-degree nonzero piece produces a negative power $s_0^{-N}$ that cannot be canceled by lower-degree pieces. Hence regularity on $U_i$ forces $N=0$. Therefore $f$ is constant. Thus
\[
\Gamma(\mathbb P_k^n,\mathcal O)=k.
\]

If $\mathbb P_k^n$ were affine, reconstruction would yield
\[
\mathbb P_k^n\cong\Spec k,
\]
which is impossible for $n\ge1$: projective space has distinct points such as $[1:0:\cdots:0]$ and $[0:1:0:\cdots:0]$. Hence $\mathbb P_k^n$ is not affine.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Solution to Challenge VI.18.C3 (Finite affine schemes).}
\begin{solution}
Let $A$ be Artinian. First, every prime ideal of $A$ is maximal. Indeed, if $\mathfrak p$ is prime, then $A/\mathfrak p$ is an Artinian domain. In an Artinian domain every nonzero element is a unit: the descending chain $(a)\supseteq(a^2)\supseteq\cdots$ stabilizes, and cancellation in the domain yields an inverse. Thus $A/\mathfrak p$ is a field and $\mathfrak p$ is maximal.

An Artinian ring has only finitely many maximal ideals $\mathfrak m_1,\dots,\mathfrak m_r$. Since all primes are maximal,
\[
\Spec A=\{\mathfrak m_1,\dots,\mathfrak m_r\}.
\]
Every point is closed. A finite $T_1$ space is discrete: for each $i$, the complement of $\{\mathfrak m_i\}$ is a finite union of closed points and hence closed, so the singleton is open as well. Therefore $\Spec A$ is a finite discrete space.

The Artinian structure theorem gives a canonical finite product decomposition
\[
A\cong A_1\times\cdots\times A_r,
\]
where each $A_i$ is Artinian local (equivalently one may take suitable quotients by powers of the distinct maximal ideals). Applying the product-spectrum decomposition repeatedly gives
\[
\Spec A\cong\bigsqcup_{i=1}^r\Spec A_i.
\]
Each local Artinian factor has exactly one prime, its maximal ideal, so each $\Spec A_i$ is one point. The product decomposition of the ring is therefore exactly the decomposition of the finite affine scheme into its open-and-closed local components.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Solution to Challenge VI.18.C4 (Reconstruct the sheaf from the principal basis).}
\begin{solution}
Let $\mathcal B=\{D(f):f\in A\}$ be the distinguished-open basis. It is stable under finite intersections because
\[
D(f)\cap D(g)=D(fg).
\]
Assign to $D(f)$ the ring $A_f$. If $D(g)\subseteq D(f)$, the restriction map is the canonical localization map determined by the fact that every element inverted on $D(f)$ remains invertible on $D(g)$. In particular, for the standard inclusion $D(fg)\subseteq D(f)$ the map is $A_f\to A_{fg}$.

These basis data satisfy the sheaf equalizer condition. For a distinguished open $D(f)$ covered by distinguished opens $D(fg_i)$, compatible elements
\[
s_i\in A_{fg_i}
\]
glue uniquely to an element of $A_f$. Algebraically, the cover condition says that the images of the $g_i$ generate the unit ideal in $A_f$ (after reducing to a finite subcover by quasi-compactness). Clearing sufficiently large common powers of the $g_i$ and using a relation $1=\sum a_ig_i^N$ produces the glued element; equality on overlaps guarantees independence. This is the standard localization gluing lemma.

A sheaf on a space is uniquely determined by its values and restriction maps on a basis once the basis sheaf axioms hold. Explicitly, for an arbitrary open $U$, cover it by distinguished opens $D(f_i)\subseteq U$. Define a section on $U$ to be a compatible family $s_i\in A_{f_i}$, with two such descriptions identified after passing to a common distinguished refinement. The basis equalizer property makes this construction independent of the chosen cover, gives restriction maps, and verifies locality and gluing.

The resulting sheaf has value $A_f$ on each distinguished open and hence is canonically isomorphic to the usual structure sheaf $\mathcal O_{\Spec A}$. Uniqueness follows because any other sheaf with the same basis data yields the same compatible-family construction on every open set.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Solution to Challenge VI.18.C5 (When is a principal open all or nothing?).}
\begin{solution}
First suppose $D(f)=\Spec A$. Then $f$ belongs to no prime ideal, hence in particular to no maximal ideal. If $f$ were not a unit, the principal ideal $(f)$ would be proper and therefore contained in a maximal ideal, contradiction. Thus $f$ is a unit. Conversely, if $f$ is a unit, no proper ideal contains it, so no prime contains it and $D(f)=\Spec A$. Hence
\[
\boxed{D(f)=\Spec A\iff f\in A^\times.}
\]
In this case $A_f\cong A$: the extreme localization changes nothing.

Next, $D(f)=\varnothing$ exactly when every prime ideal contains $f$. The intersection of all prime ideals is the nilradical $\sqrt{(0)}$, so
\[
\boxed{D(f)=\varnothing\iff f\in\sqrt{(0)}.}
\]
Equivalently, $f$ is nilpotent. If $f^N=0$, then in $A_f$ the element $f$ is both nilpotent and invertible, so $1=f^{-N}f^N=0$ and $A_f=0$. Thus the other extreme localization produces the zero ring and therefore the empty affine scheme.
\end{solution}
