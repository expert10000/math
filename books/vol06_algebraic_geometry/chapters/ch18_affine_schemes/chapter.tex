\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

The previous chapter attached a structure sheaf to every spectrum.  We now package the
pair
\[
  (\Spec A,\OO_{\Spec A})
\]
as one geometric object: an \emph{affine scheme}.  This is the basic local model for all
scheme theory.  Its points are prime ideals, its open sets are Zariski opens, its regular
functions are local fractions, and its local ring at $\mfp$ is $A_{\mfp}$.

The chapter begins from the Volume VI legacy-problem ledger rather than from a desired
problem count.  Two substantive corpus items belong here and are preserved in full:
AG3 Problem 11, proving that a distinguished open $D(f)$ is the affine scheme
$\Spec A_f$ as a locally ringed space, and the affineness subproblem AG6 Problem 6.4,
which exhibits $\mathbb P^1_k$ as a non-affine scheme.  The neighboring AG3 Problems
12--14 remain reserved for VI/19, VI/21, and VI/20 respectively.

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item define affine schemes as spectra equipped with their structure sheaves;
\item pass freely among the ring $A$, its spectrum, and the corresponding locally ringed
space $(\Spec A,\OO_{\Spec A})$;
\item compute global sections, stalks, residue fields, and distinguished opens;
\item prove that $D(f)$ is canonically $\Spec A_f$ as a locally ringed space;
\item recognize that distinguished opens form an affine basis;
\item prove that every affine scheme is quasi-compact;
\item recover the coordinate ring of an affine scheme from global sections;
\item work with affine schemes arising from fields, polynomial rings, product rings,
quotients, localizations, arithmetic rings, and nonreduced rings;
\item distinguish ``affine'' from ``locally affine'' and explain why open subsets need not
be affine;
\item prove the legacy example that $\mathbb P^1_k$ is not affine;
\item keep scheme-morphism and gluing results properly reserved for VI/19 and VI/20.
\end{itemize}

\section{Locally ringed spaces and the affine model}

A \emph{locally ringed space} is a topological space $X$ equipped with a sheaf of rings
$\OO_X$ such that every stalk $\OO_{X,x}$ is a local ring.  VI/17 proved that for
$X=\Spec A$,
\[
\OO_{X,\mfp}\cong A_{\mfp},
\]
and $A_{\mfp}$ is local with maximal ideal $\mfp A_{\mfp}$.  Thus every spectrum with
its structure sheaf is naturally a locally ringed space.

\begin{definition}[Affine scheme]
An \emph{affine scheme} is a locally ringed space isomorphic to
\[
  \Spec A := (\Spec A,\OO_{\Spec A})
\]
for some commutative ring $A$ with identity.
\end{definition}

The adjective ``affine'' refers to the fact that one ring controls the whole space.  Later,
a general scheme will be a locally ringed space that is only \emph{locally} affine.

\section{The three layers of an affine scheme}

For $X=\Spec A$, one should keep three layers visible simultaneously:
\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\text{topology: prime ideals and Zariski opens},\\[2pt]
\text{functions: }\OO_X(D(f))=A_f,\\[2pt]
\text{local algebra: }\OO_{X,\mfp}=A_{\mfp}.
\end{array}}
\]
The residue field at a point is
\[
\kappa(\mfp)=A_{\mfp}/\mfp A_{\mfp}\cong\Frac(A/\mfp).
\]
Thus an affine scheme is much richer than its underlying topological space.

\section{Global sections recover the affine coordinate ring}

Since $X=D(1)$, VI/17 gives
\[
\Gamma(X,\OO_X)=\OO_X(X)\cong A.
\]
Consequently, if $X$ is affine then its coordinate ring can be recovered intrinsically:
\[
\boxed{A\cong\Gamma(X,\OO_X).}
\]
In particular, if $X\cong\Spec A$, then
\[
X\cong\Spec\Gamma(X,\OO_X).
\]
This elementary recovery principle is one of the strongest tests for non-affineness used
later in the chapter.

\begin{example}[Distinguished open as an affine scheme]\label{ex:vi18-expand-01}
For $X=\operatorname{Spec}A$, the open $D(f)$ with its restricted structure sheaf is canonically $\operatorname{Spec}A_f$.  For $A=k[x]$ and $f=x$, this gives $D(x)\cong\operatorname{Spec}k[x,x^{-1}]$.
\end{example}

\section{Distinguished opens are affine}

Let $X=\Spec A$ and $f\in A$.  Topologically, primes of $A_f$ correspond exactly to
primes of $A$ not containing $f$.  Hence
\[
\Spec A_f\longleftrightarrow D(f).
\]
The structure sheaves agree under this correspondence because, for $g\in A$,
\[
\OO_{\Spec A}|_{D(f)}(D(fg))=A_{fg}
\]
and
\[
\OO_{\Spec A_f}(D(g/1))=(A_f)_{g/1}\cong A_{fg}.
\]
Therefore one has the fundamental geometric localization theorem
\[
\boxed{
  (D(f),\OO_X|_{D(f)})\cong\Spec A_f.
}
\]
This is the full content of legacy AG3 Problem 11 and is proved in detail in Problem
VI.18.P1.

\input{chapters/ch18_affine_schemes/figures/vi18_basic_open_affine.tex}

\begin{corollary}[Affine basis]
Every affine scheme has a basis of open subsets that are themselves affine schemes.
\end{corollary}

Indeed, the distinguished opens $D(f)$ form a basis of $\Spec A$, and every one of them is
an affine scheme $\Spec A_f$.

\section{Finite principal covers and quasi-compactness}

Suppose
\[
\Spec A=\bigcup_{i\in I}D(f_i).
\]
The complement is
\[
V((f_i)_{i\in I}),
\]
so the cover condition is equivalent to the ideal generated by the $f_i$ being all of $A$.
Thus there exist finitely many indices $i_1,\dots,i_r$ and elements $a_j\in A$ such that
\[
1=a_1f_{i_1}+\cdots+a_rf_{i_r}.
\]
Hence
\[
\Spec A=D(f_{i_1})\cup\cdots\cup D(f_{i_r}).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Affine schemes are quasi-compact]
Every open cover of an affine scheme has a finite subcover.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Refine the cover by distinguished opens.  The refined distinguished-open cover admits a
finite subcover by the preceding ideal argument; the corresponding members of the original
cover form a finite subcover as well.
\end{proof}

\section{Basic affine schemes}

The basic examples include
\[
\A^n_k=\Spec k[x_1,\dots,x_n],
\qquad
\Spec\mathbb Z,
\qquad
\Spec k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),
\]
and reducible spectra such as
\[
\Spec k[x,y]/(xy).
\]
The multiplicative group is the distinguished open
\[
\Gm=\Spec k[t,t^{-1}]\cong D(t)\subseteq\A^1_k.
\]
Product rings give disconnected affine schemes:
\[
\Spec(A\times B)\cong \Spec A\sqcup\Spec B
\]
as locally ringed spaces.

\begin{example}[A nonreduced affine scheme]\label{ex:vi18-expand-02}
The affine scheme $\operatorname{Spec}k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ has one topological point but a nonreduced global ring.  Its structure sheaf remembers the nilpotent section $\varepsilon$.
\end{example}

\section{The empty affine scheme}

The zero ring has no prime ideals, so
\[
\Spec 0=\varnothing.
\]
With its unique sheaf of rings, this is the empty affine scheme.  It is important to allow
this object: it appears naturally as $D(f)$ whenever $f$ is nilpotent, since then $A_f=0$.

\section{Affine schemes can have the same underlying space}

The spectra
\[
\Spec k
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Spec k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)
\]
both have one point, but their global rings are different.  Since an affine scheme recovers
its global ring, they are not isomorphic as affine schemes.  Affine geometry therefore
remembers nilpotent directions that the underlying topology forgets.

\section{Local affine schemes}

If $(A,\mfm)$ is a local ring, then $\Spec A$ has a unique closed point, namely $\mfm$.
Conversely, if an affine scheme $\Spec A$ has a unique closed point, then $A$ has a unique
maximal ideal and is local.  Thus
\[
\boxed{
A\text{ local}\iff\Spec A\text{ has exactly one closed point}.}
\]
This gives a useful geometric interpretation of local rings without confusing a local scheme
with a one-point space: a local ring can have many nonclosed prime ideals.

\begin{example}[Disconnected affine scheme]\label{ex:vi18-expand-03}
For $A=k\times k$, $\operatorname{Spec}A$ is two disjoint points.  The orthogonal idempotents $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ split the ring and the affine scheme into two open-and-closed components.
\end{example}

\section{Affine algebraic sets versus affine schemes}

If $k$ is algebraically closed and
\[
V=V(I)\subseteq k^n,
\qquad
A=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I,
\]
then the classical points of $V$ correspond to maximal ideals of $A$ of the form
$(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n)/I$.  The affine scheme $\Spec A$ also contains nonclosed prime
ideals, generic points, residue-field information, and nilpotent structure when $I$ is not
radical.  Thus affine schemes extend rather than merely rename classical affine algebraic
sets.

\section{Open subsets of affine schemes need not be affine}
Distinguished opens are affine, but arbitrary open subsets of affine schemes need not be.  The standard example is the punctured affine plane
\[
U=\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}=D(x)\cup D(y)\subseteq\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y].
\]
The two pieces are affine and their intersection is $D(xy)$.  The sheaf condition gives
\[
\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong k[x,y]_x\cap k[x,y]_y
\subseteq k(x,y).
\]
Because $x$ and $y$ are relatively prime in the UFD $k[x,y]$, this intersection is exactly
\[
\boxed{\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)=k[x,y].}
\]
If $U$ were affine, affine reconstruction would identify it with
\[
\operatorname{Spec}\Gamma(U,\mathcal O_U)=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]=\mathbb A_k^2.
\]
Under the displayed identification of global sections, the canonical morphism is the original open immersion $U\hookrightarrow\mathbb A_k^2$, which omits the origin and therefore cannot be an isomorphism.  Hence $U$ is not affine.

\section{An affine cover does not imply affineness}
A second, logically different phenomenon is that a scheme may admit an affine open cover without itself being affine.  The projective line is the standard example:
\[
\mathbb P^1_k=U_0\cup U_1,
\qquad U_0\cong U_1\cong\mathbb A^1_k.
\]
In the advanced examples and Problem VI.18.P2 we compute
\[
\Gamma(\mathbb P^1_k,\mathcal O)=k
\]
and deduce that affineness would force
\[
\mathbb P^1_k\cong\operatorname{Spec}k,
\]
which is impossible.  Thus
\[
\boxed{\text{covered by affine opens}\not\Rightarrow\text{affine}.}
\]
The formal gluing construction of general schemes is reserved for VI/20--VI/21.

\section{Common pitfalls}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An affine scheme is not only the topological space $\Spec A$; the structure sheaf is
part of the object.
\item ``Affine'' means globally isomorphic to one spectrum.  Being covered by affine opens
is weaker.
\item Not every open subset of an affine scheme is affine, although every distinguished open
$D(f)$ is affine.
\item The coordinate ring of an affine scheme is recovered as $\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$; this is a
powerful invariant.
\item $\Spec(A/I)$ is certainly affine, but interpreting it as a \emph{closed subscheme} of
$\Spec A$ is reserved for VI/22.
\item A ring homomorphism induces a contravariant map of spectra, but the full definition and
universal property of affine-scheme morphisms are reserved for VI/19 and AG3 Problem 12.
\item The scheme-gluing theorem AG3 Problem 14 remains reserved for VI/20.
\item A local affine scheme need not be a one-point space; it has one closed point but may
have many generic or intermediate points.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Summary}

An affine scheme is one globally controlled by a ring:
\[
\boxed{X=\Spec A=(\Spec A,\OO_{\Spec A}).}
\]
Its fundamental computations are
\[
\Gamma(X,\OO_X)=A,
\qquad
\OO_{X,\mfp}=A_{\mfp},
\qquad
\kappa(\mfp)=\Frac(A/\mfp),
\]
and its basic open charts satisfy
\[
\boxed{D(f)\cong\Spec A_f.}
\]
Distinguished opens form an affine basis and affine schemes are quasi-compact.  The ring is
recoverable from global sections, while topology alone is insufficient to recover the
scheme.  The legacy ledger now records AG3 Problem 11 as VI.18.P1 and the non-affineness
subproblem AG6 Problem 6.4 as VI.18.P2, while AG3 Problems 12--14 remain reserved for their
later canonical destinations.

\subsection*{Proofs}

\begin{exercise}[Distinguished affine]\label{exr:vi18-expand-06}
Prove: Prove $(D(f),\mathcal O_X|_{D(f)})\cong\operatorname{Spec}A_f$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Work directly from affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Use prime correspondence for localization and compare the structure sheaves on the distinguished-open bases.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Global recovery]\label{exr:vi18-expand-07}
Prove: Prove $\Gamma(\operatorname{Spec}A,\mathcal O)=A$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Work directly from affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Use $D(1)=\operatorname{Spec}A$ and the basic-open section formula.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Quasi-compact]\label{exr:vi18-expand-08}
Prove: Prove every affine scheme is quasi-compact.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Work directly from affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Refine an open cover by distinguished opens and use the unit-ideal finite-generation argument.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Product disjoint]\label{exr:vi18-expand-09}
Prove: Prove $\operatorname{Spec}(A\times B)$ is the disjoint union of the two spectra as locally ringed spaces.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Work directly from affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Classify primes using idempotents and compare localizations on each component.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Applications and investigations}

\begin{exercise}[Local models]\label{exr:vi18-expand-13}
Why are affine schemes the local building blocks of schemes?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Translate the question into affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
General schemes are covered by opens isomorphic to affine spectra.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Computational bridge]\label{exr:vi18-expand-14}
Why are affine schemes computationally convenient?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Translate the question into affine schemes, global sections, and distinguished opens.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Global geometry is encoded by one ring and localization describes basic opens.
\end{solution}

\end{solution}
