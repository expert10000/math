\chapter{Coordinate Rings}
\label{ch:coordinate-rings}

\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

The first two chapters moved from polynomial equations to algebraic sets and then to the
Zariski topology.  We now reverse direction.  Given an affine algebraic set \(X\), we ask:
\emph{which polynomial functions actually live on \(X\), and what algebraic relations do
they satisfy?}

The answer is the coordinate ring
\[
  k[X]=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X).
\]
This quotient is not merely a convenient presentation.  It is the ring of polynomial
functions on \(X\), and it is the first object that makes the algebra--geometry dictionary
behave systematically.

Throughout, \(k\) is a field and \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) is an affine algebraic set.
Whenever a computation identifies \(I(V(J))\) with a displayed ideal \(J\), the necessary
field hypothesis is stated explicitly.

After this chapter the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item construct \(k[X]\) from restriction of ambient polynomials;
\item recognize \(I(X)\) as the kernel of restriction and as the complete set of relations;
\item compute coordinate rings of lines, parabolas, hyperbolas, finite sets, and unions;
\item distinguish a ring from a chosen presentation by generators and relations;
\item use evaluation maps to encode points by maximal ideals;
\item turn a closed inclusion \(Y\subseteq X\) into a quotient \(k[X]\twoheadrightarrow k[Y]\);
\item explain why classical coordinate rings are reduced and why nilpotents require a richer geometry;
\item understand how finite fields alter the ring of polynomial functions;
\item solve a nontrivial legacy example: the monomial curve \((t^3,t^4,t^5)\).
\end{itemize}

\section{From ambient polynomials to polynomial functions}
\label{sec:vi03-polynomial-functions}

Every ambient polynomial restricts to a function on \(X\).  The central point is that
many different ambient polynomials can restrict to the same function.

\begin{definition}[Polynomial function]
A function \(\varphi:X\to k\) is a \emph{polynomial function} if there exists
\(f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) such that \(\varphi(a)=f(a)\) for every \(a\in X\).
\end{definition}

Consider the restriction homomorphism
\[
\rho_X:k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\longrightarrow \operatorname{Fun}(X,k),
\qquad f\longmapsto f|_X.
\]
Its image is exactly the ring of polynomial functions on \(X\).

\begin{proposition}[Kernel of restriction]
\label{prop:vi03-kernel}
\[
\ker\rho_X=I(X).
\]
Hence \(f\) and \(g\) induce the same polynomial function on \(X\) if and only if
\(f-g\in I(X)\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A polynomial belongs to the kernel precisely when it vanishes at every point of \(X\),
which is the definition of \(I(X)\).  The second statement follows by applying this to
\(f-g\).
\end{proof}

The entire construction can be summarized by the first isomorphism theorem.

\section{Definition of the coordinate ring}
\label{sec:vi03-definition}

\begin{definition}[Coordinate ring]
The \emph{coordinate ring} of \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) is
\[
  k[X]:=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X).
\]
\end{definition}

By \cref{prop:vi03-kernel}, \(k[X]\) is canonically the ring of polynomial functions on
\(X\).  An element can therefore be read in three equivalent ways:
\[
\boxed{[f]\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad f|_X\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{a polynomial function }X\to k.}
\]

Write \(\bar x_i\) for the class of \(x_i\) in \(k[X]\).  Then
\[
  k[X]=k[\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n].
\]
The ideal \(I(X)\) is exactly the set of polynomial relations among these coordinate
functions.

\begin{proposition}[Relations among coordinates]
For \(F\in k[t_1,\dots,t_n]\),
\[
F(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n)=0\text{ in }k[X]
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in I(X).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The quotient map \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\to k[X]\) has kernel \(I(X)\).
\end{proof}

\section{First computations}
\label{sec:vi03-first-computations}

When \(k\) is infinite,
\[
k[\mathbb A_k^n]=k[x_1,\dots,x_n].
\]
For a rational point \(a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\),
\[
k[\{a\}]
=\frac{k[x_1,\dots,x_n]}{(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n)}\cong k.
\]

A line and a parabola already show why the presentation of a coordinate ring is not
intrinsic.  Assume \(k\) is infinite.  For
\[
L=V(y)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2,
\qquad
P=V(y-x^2)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2,
\]
one has
\[
k[L]\cong k[t],
\qquad
k[P]=k[x,y]/(y-x^2)\cong k[t].
\]
The two plane embeddings look different, but their polynomial-function rings are
isomorphic.

\section{Finitely generated algebras and presentations}
\label{sec:vi03-presentations}

\begin{definition}[Finitely generated \(k\)-algebra]
A \(k\)-algebra \(A\) is finitely generated if
\[
A=k[a_1,\dots,a_m]
\]
for finitely many elements \(a_i\in A\).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
Every classical affine coordinate ring is a finitely generated \(k\)-algebra.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The classes \(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n\) generate the quotient
\(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X)\).
\end{proof}

Conversely, every finitely generated commutative \(k\)-algebra admits a presentation
\[
A\cong k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/J.
\]
This does \emph{not} mean every such quotient is the coordinate ring of the classical point
set \(V(J)\): the classical point set forgets nilpotent and, over non-algebraically-closed
fields, sometimes further information.  Schemes will repair this loss later.

\section{Reducedness and what classical geometry forgets}
\label{sec:vi03-reducedness}

Since \(I(X)\) is radical, classical coordinate rings contain no nonzero nilpotents.

\begin{proposition}[Classical coordinate rings are reduced]
If \(u^m=0\) in \(k[X]\) for some \(m\ge1\), then \(u=0\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write \(u=[f]\).  Then \(f^m\in I(X)\).  Radicality of \(I(X)\) gives \(f\in I(X)\), so
\([f]=0\).
\end{proof}

The basic warning is visible in the one-point example:
\[
\frac{k[x]}{(x)}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad \frac{k[x]}{(x^2)}.
\]
Both ideals have the same classical zero set \(\{0\}\), but the class of \(x\) in the
second ring is nonzero and nilpotent.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Classical point} & \textbf{Thickened point (later)}\\
\midrule
ideal & \((x)\) & \((x^2)\)\\
set of rational zeros & \(\{0\}\) & \(\{0\}\)\\
quotient ring & \(k\) & \(k[x]/(x^2)\)\\
nonzero nilpotent & no & yes: \([x]^2=0\)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Points as evaluation homomorphisms}
\label{sec:vi03-evaluation}

For \(a\in X\), evaluation descends to a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism
\[
\operatorname{ev}_a:k[X]\to k,
\qquad [f]\mapsto f(a).
\]
It is surjective because constants lie in \(k[X]\).  Its kernel
\[
\mathfrak m_a=\set{\varphi\in k[X]\mid\varphi(a)=0}
\]
is maximal, since \(k[X]/\mathfrak m_a\cong k\).

This is the first signal that points should be encoded by ideals.  The full spectrum of a
ring is postponed to VI/07.

\section{Closed subsets become quotient rings}
\label{sec:vi03-closed-quotients}

If \(Y\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) are algebraic, then
\(I(X)\subseteq I(Y)\).  Hence there is a natural surjection
\[
k[X]\twoheadrightarrow k[Y].
\]

\begin{proposition}[Closed subsets give quotient rings]
Define
\[
I_X(Y):=I(Y)/I(X)\subseteq k[X].
\]
Then
\[
k[Y]\cong k[X]/I_X(Y).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the first isomorphism theorem to
\[
\frac{k[x_1,\dots,x_n]}{I(X)}\longrightarrow
\frac{k[x_1,\dots,x_n]}{I(Y)}.
\]
Its kernel is \(I(Y)/I(X)\).
\end{proof}

The direction reversal is fundamental:

induce homomorphisms of coordinate rings in the opposite direction.

\section{Finite sets and the Chinese remainder picture}
\label{sec:vi03-finite-sets}

Let \(X=\{a_1,\dots,a_r\}\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) be distinct rational points.  Their
maximal ideals \(\mathfrak m_{a_i}\) are pairwise comaximal and
\[
I(X)=\bigcap_{i=1}^r\mathfrak m_{a_i}.
\]
Therefore the Chinese remainder theorem gives
\[
k[X]\cong\prod_{i=1}^r k.
\]
Every function on a finite set of rational points is consequently polynomial.

\section{Zero divisors as geometric signals}
\label{sec:vi03-zero-divisors}

Assume \(k\) is algebraically closed, and let
\[
X=V(xy)=V(x)\cup V(y)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2.
\]
Then
\[
k[X]=k[x,y]/(xy).
\]
The nonzero classes \(\bar x\) and \(\bar y\) satisfy
\[
\bar x\bar y=0.
\]
The zero divisors record the fact that functions supported on different components can
annihilate each other.  The precise relation between irreducibility and integral domains is
proved in VI/05.

\section{Finite fields and polynomial function rings}
\label{sec:vi03-finite-fields}

Over an infinite field, no nonzero polynomial vanishes on all of \(k^n\).  Over a finite
field this fails dramatically.  If \(k=\mathbb F_q\), then
\[
x^q-x
\]
vanishes at every element of \(\mathbb F_q\), and
\[
I(\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb F_q})=(x^q-x).
\]
Hence
\[
\mathbb F_q[\mathbb A^1_{\mathbb F_q}]
\cong \mathbb F_q[x]/(x^q-x)
\cong \prod_{a\in\mathbb F_q}\mathbb F_q.
\]
Thus the coordinate ring of the \emph{finite rational-point set} is the full algebra of
functions on that finite set.

For several variables,
\[
I(\mathbb F_q^n)=(x_1^q-x_1,\dots,x_n^q-x_n),
\]
a result developed further in the Challenges.

\section{Common pitfalls}
\label{sec:vi03-pitfalls}

\begin{warning}[Use \(I(X)\), not an arbitrary ideal cutting out \(X\)]
If \(X=V(J)\), the classical coordinate ring is
\[
k[X]=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X),
\]
not automatically \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/J\).  Different ideals can define the same point set.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[A presentation is not the ring itself]
The rings \(k[x,y]/(y-x^2)\) and \(k[t]\) are isomorphic.  Generators and relations are a
presentation, not an intrinsic coordinate system.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Classical coordinate rings are reduced]
A quotient with nonzero nilpotents carries more information than the classical zero set.
That extra information belongs to scheme theory.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Finite fields change polynomial functions]
Over \(\mathbb F_q\), distinct polynomials can define the same function even on all of
affine space.  The relations \(x_i^q-x_i\) must be taken into account.
\end{warning}

\section{Chapter summary}
\label{sec:vi03-summary}

The coordinate ring
\[
k[X]=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X)
\]
is canonically the ring of polynomial functions on \(X\).  The ambient coordinate classes
generate the ring, while \(I(X)\) records every relation among them.  Points produce
evaluation maps to \(k\); closed subsets produce quotient rings in the reverse direction;
finite rational-point sets produce product rings; and reducible geometry can manifest as
zero divisors.

Classical coordinate rings are finitely generated and reduced, so they deliberately forget
nilpotent thickening information.  Over finite fields the ring of polynomial functions is
smaller than the polynomial ring because \(x_i^q-x_i\) vanishes identically on rational
points.  These facts prepare the next chapter, where maps of affine algebraic sets will
become homomorphisms of coordinate rings in the opposite direction.
