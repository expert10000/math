\begin{problem}[Dense image without injectivity]\label{prob:vi19-p14}
Give and analyze an explicit noninjective ring map whose spectrum map is surjective on points, and explain why this does not contradict affine anti-equivalence.
\end{problem}

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
This is a sharp warning that topological information alone does not recover affine morphisms.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
Use the reduction of a nonreduced one-point ring.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Take $A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ and $B=k$.
\item Compare prime spectra and global rings.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{solution}
% VI19 full-solutions refinement v1
Take
\[
A=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),
\qquad
B=k,
\]
and let \(\varphi:A\to B\) be the quotient map \(\varepsilon\mapsto0\).  Its kernel is the nonzero ideal \((\varepsilon)\), so \(\varphi\) is not injective.  The ring \(A\) has only one prime ideal, namely \((\varepsilon)\), and \(B\) has only the zero prime.  Under contraction,
\[
(0)\subset B\longmapsto(\varepsilon)\subset A.
\]
Thus the induced spectrum map is a bijection between one-point spaces, in particular it is surjective and dense.

This does not contradict affine anti-equivalence.  The anti-equivalence identifies affine \emph{scheme morphisms} with ring maps, not continuous maps of their underlying topological spaces.  The map \(\Spec k\to\Spec A\) carries a nontrivial sheaf pullback whose map on global sections is \(A\to k\), killing \(\varepsilon\).  If it were an isomorphism of affine schemes, its global pullback would have to be a ring isomorphism, which it is not.  Thus the topology forgets the infinitesimal direction represented by \(\varepsilon\), while the structure sheaf and its morphism remember it.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
The sheaf map carries infinitesimal information that the underlying point map forgets.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Replace the dual numbers by $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^n)$.
