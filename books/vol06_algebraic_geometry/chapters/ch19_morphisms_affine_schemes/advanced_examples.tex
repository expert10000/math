\begin{example}[Advanced: Affine anti-equivalence as a reconstruction theorem]
The assignments $A\mapsto\Spec A$ and $X\mapsto\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$ are inverse up to canonical isomorphism on affine objects.  Hence all affine-scheme morphism questions may be translated into ring-homomorphism questions and back.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Universal property beyond affine sources]
For a locally ringed space $X$, a ring map $A\to\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$ already determines a unique locally-ringed-space morphism $X\to\Spec A$.  No affine hypothesis on $X$ is required.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Maps to affine line detect global sections]
Taking $A=\mathbb Z[t]$ gives $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{LRS}}(X,\Spec\mathbb Z[t])\cong\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$.  Thus a global function may be viewed geometrically as a map to the universal affine line.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Factorization through a quotient]
For $I\subseteq A$ and $f:X\to\Spec A$ corresponding to $\alpha:A\to\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$, the map factors through $\Spec(A/I)$ exactly when $I\subseteq\ker\alpha$.  Closed-subscheme language is postponed to VI/22.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Factorization through a principal open]
The same $f$ factors through $D(a)=\Spec A_a$ exactly when $\alpha(a)$ is a unit in $\Gamma(X,\OO_X)$.  This is the localization universal property translated geometrically.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Closure of image]
For $\varphi:A\to B$, the closure of the image of $\Spec B\to\Spec A$ is $V(\ker\varphi)$.  Hence a spectrum map may be dense even when the ring map is not injective, provided its kernel is nilpotent.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Same topology, different sheaf maps]
The endomorphisms $\varepsilon\mapsto\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon\mapsto0$ of $k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$ give identical continuous maps on the one-point spectrum but different pullbacks on global sections.  The sheaf component is essential.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Automorphisms of the affine line]
A $k$-algebra automorphism of $k[t]$ has $t\mapsto at+b$ with $a\in k^\times$.  Therefore affine-line automorphisms over $k$ are exactly the corresponding affine coordinate changes.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Morphisms into affine space as tuples]
For a $k$-algebra $B$, morphisms $\Spec B\to\A^n_k$ correspond to $n$-tuples of elements of $B$.  Composition becomes substitution of polynomials.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Contraction and specialization]
If $\mfp\subseteq\mfp'$ in $B$, then $\varphi^{-1}(\mfp)\subseteq\varphi^{-1}(\mfp')$ in $A$.  Thus a spectrum morphism preserves specialization order.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Local maps force the correct point]
Given a locally-ringed-space morphism $f:X\to\Spec A$, the point $f(x)$ can be recovered from the global pullback: it consists exactly of those $a\in A$ whose germ at $x$ lands in the maximal ideal of $\OO_{X,x}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Residue-field extensions at non-rational points]
A morphism may send a point with residue field $K$ to one with residue field $k$ while inducing a nontrivial embedding $k\hookrightarrow K$.  This is why scheme-valued points retain arithmetic information beyond the topological image.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Localization transitivity]
For $A\to A_f\to(A_f)_{g/1}\cong A_{fg}$, the corresponding morphisms compose as $D(fg)\hookrightarrow D(f)\hookrightarrow\Spec A$.  This is functoriality plus VI/18 localization.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Advanced: Boundary to gluing]
VI/19 explains affine morphisms entirely algebraically.  VI/20 will ask when morphisms defined on overlapping affine charts agree and glue.  The universal property here is the local algebraic engine for that later construction.
\end{example}
