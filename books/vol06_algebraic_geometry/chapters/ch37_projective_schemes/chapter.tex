\chapter{Projective Schemes}
\label{ch:vi37-projective-schemes}

VI/34 developed graded rings, VI/35 constructed \(\Proj S\), and VI/36 specialized that construction to projective space.  We now pass from the ambient space \(\mathbb P^n_k\) to the schemes cut out by homogeneous equations.  If
\[
  S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]
\]
and \(I\subseteq S\) is homogeneous, then the quotient \(S/I\) is again graded and
\[
  X=\Proj(S/I)
\]
is the projective scheme defined by \(I\).  On every standard chart this is ordinary affine scheme theory:
\[
  X\cap D_+(f)\cong \Spec\bigl((S/I)_{(f)}\bigr)
  \cong \Spec\bigl(S_{(f)}/I_{(f)}\bigr).
\]
Thus projective equations become affine equations after dehomogenization, while the homogeneous grading guarantees that the chart descriptions glue.

The migration ledger assigns the projective-scheme section of the AG4 source, together with its theorem-like and exercise/problem descendants, to VI/37.  The raw legacy source is not present in the accessible repository archive, so this chapter uses the ledger as provenance and gives a new canonical treatment compatible with VI/34--VI/36.  General projective morphisms and the general theory of closed embeddings remain VI/38.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item construct \(\Proj(S/I)\) from a homogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S\);
\item identify its underlying closed set with \(V_+(I)\subseteq\Proj S\);
\item compute its affine charts by degree-zero localization and dehomogenization;
\item define projective schemes over a field using finitely generated standard graded algebras;
\item work with homogeneous coordinate rings while recognizing their dependence on an embedding;
\item compute projective hypersurfaces and their affine charts;
\item form scheme-theoretic intersections by adding homogeneous ideals;
\item distinguish set-theoretic union from scheme-theoretic union and retain nilpotent structure;
\item compute saturation with respect to the irrelevant ideal and explain why saturation does not change Proj;
\item recognize empty projective schemes from powers of the irrelevant ideal;
\item construct projective closures of affine schemes by homogenization and saturation;
\item relate a projective scheme to its affine cone;
\item detect reducedness, irreducibility, and components from homogeneous coordinate data, with the necessary caveats about irrelevant torsion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n],\ I\ \text{homogeneous}\\[2pt]
\downarrow\\[-2pt]
S/I\ \text{graded}\quad\longrightarrow\quad X=\Proj(S/I)\\[2pt]
\downarrow\quad\quad\quad\downarrow\\[-2pt]
(S/I)_{(f)}\cong S_{(f)}/I_{(f)}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad X\cap D_+(f)
\end{array}}
\]
Projective scheme theory is therefore affine scheme theory performed coherently on homogeneous localizations.

\section{Homogeneous quotients and projective closed loci}
Let \(S=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}S_d\) be a graded ring and \(I\subseteq S\) a homogeneous ideal.  Then \(S/I\) inherits a grading and the quotient map preserves degree.

\begin{theorem}[Proj of a homogeneous quotient]\label{thm:vi37-quotient-proj}
There is a canonical identification of underlying topological spaces
\[
  \Proj(S/I)\cong V_+(I)\subseteq\Proj S.
\]
For every homogeneous \(f\in S_+\), the corresponding chart is
\[
  D_+(\bar f)\subseteq\Proj(S/I)
  \cong
  \Spec\bigl(S_{(f)}/I_{(f)}\bigr),
\]
where \(I_{(f)}\) is the degree-zero part of the localized ideal \(IS_f\).
These affine closed subschemes glue to a canonical closed subscheme of \(\Proj S\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Homogeneous primes of \(S/I\) correspond to homogeneous primes \(\mathfrak p\subseteq S\) containing \(I\).  The condition that the irrelevant ideal of \(S/I\) is not contained in the prime is exactly the condition \(S_+\not\subseteq\mathfrak p\).  Hence the point set is \(V_+(I)\).  On \(D_+(f)\), localization commutes with quotient:
\[
  (S/I)_f\cong S_f/IS_f.
\]
Taking degree-zero parts gives
\[
  (S/I)_{(f)}\cong S_{(f)}/I_{(f)}.
\]
These quotient descriptions are compatible on overlaps by localization in stages, so the affine closed subschemes glue.
\end{proof}

The theorem is the projective analogue of \(V(I)=\Spec(A/I)\) in affine geometry.  It is the special quotient construction needed in this chapter; VI/38 develops closed embeddings in general.

\section{Projective schemes over a field}
Let \(k\) be a field.

\begin{definition}[Projective scheme in this volume]\label{def:vi37-projective-scheme}
A \(k\)-scheme \(X\) is called projective if it is isomorphic to
\[
  \Proj A
\]
for some finitely generated standard graded \(k\)-algebra
\[
  A=\bigoplus_{d\ge0}A_d,
  \qquad A_0=k,
\]
generated by \(A_1\) over \(k\).
\end{definition}

Choosing generators of \(A_1\) gives a surjection
\[
  k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\twoheadrightarrow A
\]
with homogeneous kernel \(I\), so
\[
  X\cong\Proj(k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]/I).
\]
Thus every projective scheme in this sense is presented by homogeneous equations inside some projective space.  VI/38 will repackage this using the general language of closed embeddings and projective morphisms.

\section{Homogeneous coordinate rings}
If
\[
  X=\Proj(S/I)\subseteq\mathbb P^n_k,
  \qquad S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n],
\]
then
\[
  S(X):=S/I
\]
is called the homogeneous coordinate ring of the displayed projective presentation.

\begin{remark}[Coordinate ring depends on the embedding]
Unlike an affine scheme, a projective scheme does not have one intrinsic global coordinate ring recovering the scheme by Spec.  Different projective embeddings can produce different homogeneous coordinate rings.  What is intrinsic is the scheme; the graded ring records the chosen embedding data.
\end{remark}

The standard chart \(U_i=D_+(x_i)\) has coordinate ring
\[
  \Gamma(X\cap U_i,\OO_X)
  \cong (S/I)_{(x_i)}.
\]
Computationally this is obtained by setting \(x_i=1\) in homogeneous equations after dividing to degree zero.

\section{Projective hypersurfaces}
Let \(F\in k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\) be a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree \(d>0\).  The projective hypersurface defined by \(F\) is
\[
  X=\Proj\bigl(k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]/(F)\bigr).
\]
On \(U_i\), divide by \(x_i^d\) and write
\[
  f_i=\frac{F}{x_i^d}
\]
in the ratio coordinates.  Then
\[
  X\cap U_i\cong V(f_i)\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Dimension of a projective hypersurface]\label{thm:vi37-hypersurface-dim}
Assume \(n\ge1\).  If \(F\ne0\) is homogeneous of positive degree, then every irreducible component of
\[
  V_+(F)\subseteq\mathbb P^n_k
\]
has dimension \(n-1\).  Consequently the projective hypersurface has dimension \(n-1\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial ring \(S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\) has dimension \(n+1\).  Every minimal prime over the principal ideal \((F)\) has height one by the principal ideal theorem, since \(S\) is a domain and \(F\ne0\).  Hence every corresponding graded quotient component has Krull dimension \(n\).  Passing from a nontrivial standard graded affine cone to Proj lowers the dimension by one, yielding dimension \(n-1\) on every projective component.
\end{proof}

\section{Scheme-theoretic intersections}
Suppose two projective closed subschemes of \(\mathbb P^n_k\) are presented by homogeneous ideals \(I,J\subseteq S\).  Their scheme-theoretic intersection is obtained chart by chart by taking the sum of the defining ideals.

\begin{proposition}[Intersection by sum of ideals]\label{prop:vi37-intersection}
If
\[
  X=\Proj(S/I),\qquad Y=\Proj(S/J),
\]
then their scheme-theoretic intersection inside \(\mathbb P^n_k\) is
\[
  X\cap Y=\Proj\bigl(S/(I+J)\bigr).
\]
On every standard chart this reduces to the affine formula
\[
  V(I_{(x_i)})\cap V(J_{(x_i)})=V(I_{(x_i)}+J_{(x_i)}).
\]
\end{proposition}

This formula retains multiplicity and nilpotent structure.  Replacing an ideal by its radical would generally change the scheme even when the underlying point set remains unchanged.

\section{Unions and nonreduced structure}
For homogeneous ideals \(I,J\subseteq S\), the quotient by \(I\cap J\) gives the scheme-theoretic union of the two corresponding closed subschemes:
\[
  \boxed{X\cup Y=\Proj\bigl(S/(I\cap J)\bigr).}
\]
Replacing \(I\cap J\) by its saturation gives the same projective scheme.
Set-theoretically,
\[
  V_+(I\cap J)=V_+(IJ)=V_+(I)\cup V_+(J).
\]
Scheme-theoretically, however, \(I\cap J\), \(IJ\), and \(\sqrt{IJ}\) need not define the same structure.

A basic example is the double hyperplane
\[
  \Proj S/(x_0^2).
\]
Its underlying closed set is the hyperplane \(x_0=0\), but its structure sheaf contains a nilpotent class coming from \(x_0\).  Projective schemes therefore remember infinitesimal thickness just as affine schemes do.

\section{Saturation and irrelevant torsion}
Let \(S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\) with irrelevant ideal
\[
  \mathfrak m=S_+=(x_0,\ldots,x_n).
\]
For a homogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S\), define its saturation by
\[
  I^{\mathrm{sat}}
  :=I:\mathfrak m^\infty
  =\bigcup_{r\ge0}(I:\mathfrak m^r).
\]
Equivalently, a homogeneous element \(g\) lies in \(I^{\mathrm{sat}}\) if some power of the irrelevant ideal sends \(g\) into \(I\).

\begin{theorem}[Saturation does not change Proj]\label{thm:vi37-saturation}
For every homogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S\),
\[
  \Proj(S/I)\cong\Proj(S/I^{\mathrm{sat}})
\]
as closed subschemes of \(\mathbb P^n_k\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the chart \(D_+(x_i)\), the element \(x_i\) is invertible.  If \(g\in I^{\mathrm{sat}}\), then for some \(r\) one has \(\mathfrak m^r g\subseteq I\), hence in particular \(x_i^r g\in I\).  After localizing at \(x_i\), this gives \(g\in IS_{x_i}\).  Thus \(I\) and \(I^{\mathrm{sat}}\) have the same localized ideal on every standard chart, hence the same degree-zero chart ideal.  The resulting closed subschemes agree on the standard cover.
\end{proof}

Saturation removes algebra supported only at the irrelevant cone vertex, which is absent from Proj.

\begin{example}[Vertex-supported embedded structure]
Let
\[
  I=(x_0^2,x_0x_1,\ldots,x_0x_n)=x_0\mathfrak m.
\]
Then \(x_0\in I^{\mathrm{sat}}\), and in fact
\[
  I^{\mathrm{sat}}=(x_0).
\]
Thus \(I\) and \((x_0)\) define the same projective hyperplane even though their affine cones differ at the origin.
\end{example}

\section{When Proj is empty}
The saturation viewpoint gives a useful criterion.

\begin{proposition}[Empty projective scheme criterion]\label{prop:vi37-empty}
For a homogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]\), the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \(\Proj(S/I)=\varnothing\);
\item \(I^{\mathrm{sat}}=S\);
\item \(\mathfrak m^r\subseteq I\) for some \(r\ge1\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

For example,
\[
  \Proj k[x_0,x_1]/(x_0,x_1)^2=\varnothing,
\]
even though the graded quotient ring is nonzero.  All of its geometry is concentrated at the irrelevant vertex.

\section{Projective closure of affine schemes}
Let
\[
  Y=V(J)\subseteq\mathbb A^n_k
\]
with coordinates \(t_1,\ldots,t_n\).  Introduce homogeneous coordinates \(x_0,\ldots,x_n\) and identify the affine chart \(U_0\) by \(t_i=x_i/x_0\).

For a polynomial \(f\in k[t_1,\ldots,t_n]\) of degree \(d\), its homogenization is
\[
  f^h(x_0,\ldots,x_n)
  =x_0^d f(x_1/x_0,\ldots,x_n/x_0).
\]
The projective closure of \(Y\) is defined by the homogenized ideal
\[
  J^h=(f^h:f\in J)\subseteq k[x_0,\ldots,x_n].
\]
If one starts from a chosen generating set of \(J\), merely homogenizing those generators can give an ideal smaller than \(J^h\).  The missing equations are recovered by saturation with respect to the homogenizing variable \(x_0\): for a finite generating set \(f_1,\ldots,f_r\), one computes
\[
  J^h=(f_1^h,\ldots,f_r^h):x_0^\infty
\]
after choosing a generating set for which this standard homogenization procedure is applied.  A final saturation with respect to the irrelevant ideal does not change Proj and removes purely projectively invisible vertex torsion.

\begin{remark}[Two different saturations]
Saturation by \(x_0\) repairs a naive homogenization of affine generators; it can remove spurious components contained entirely in the hyperplane at infinity.  Saturation by the irrelevant ideal \(S_+\) is different: it leaves the projective scheme unchanged and removes only algebra invisible on every standard projective chart.  These operations should not be conflated.
\end{remark}

The intersection of the closure with \(x_0=0\) records the projective points at infinity.

\section{Affine cones and projectivization}
Let \(A=S/I\) be a standard graded homogeneous coordinate ring.  Its affine cone is
\[
  C(X):=\Spec A.
\]
The ideal
\[
  A_+=\bigoplus_{d>0}A_d
\]
defines the cone vertex when \(A_0=k\).  The projective scheme
\[
  X=\Proj A
\]
forgets the vertex and records homogeneous directions away from it.

The cone and the projective scheme carry related but distinct local geometry.  A smooth projective scheme can have a singular affine cone at the vertex; conversely, nilpotent or embedded structure supported only at the vertex can disappear after passing to Proj.  Saturation is the algebraic mechanism that removes precisely this projectively invisible torsion.

\section{Reducedness, integrality, and components}
If \(A\) is a reduced graded ring, then every chart ring \(A_{(f)}\) is reduced, so \(\Proj A\) is reduced.  If \(A\) is a domain and \(\Proj A\ne\varnothing\), then \(\Proj A\) is integral.

The converses need not hold because irrelevant torsion can disappear on every projective chart.  For example, let
\[
  A=k[x_0,x_1,z]/(z^2,x_0z,x_1z)
\]
with all variables of degree one.  The nilpotent class \(z\) is annihilated by the irrelevant ideal after passing to the quotient, so it vanishes on both standard charts \(D_+(x_0)\) and \(D_+(x_1)\).  Consequently
\[
  \Proj A\cong\mathbb P^1_k
\]
is reduced although \(A\) is not.

For a Noetherian standard graded ring, irreducible components of \(\Proj A\) correspond to homogeneous minimal primes that do not contain the irrelevant ideal.  Minimal primes supported only at the cone vertex contribute no projective component.

\section{Common pitfalls}
\begin{itemize}
\item A homogeneous ideal and its saturation can be different rings but define the same projective scheme.
\item The homogeneous coordinate ring is attached to a chosen projective presentation; it is not an intrinsic replacement for \(\Gamma(X,\OO_X)\).
\item Homogeneous equations must be dehomogenized separately on each affine chart.
\item Taking radicals preserves the underlying closed set but can destroy nilpotent and multiplicity information.
\item Scheme-theoretic intersection uses \(I+J\), not \(I\cap J\).
\item Scheme-theoretic union is controlled by an intersection of ideals, not by their sum.
\item Homogenizing only a convenient generating set can introduce the wrong projective closure unless saturation is checked.
\item The affine cone contains the irrelevant vertex; \(\Proj\) does not.
\item Reducedness or integrality of a homogeneous coordinate ring implies the corresponding property for Proj, but the converse can fail because projectively invisible torsion may live at the irrelevant vertex.
\item General closed-embedding and projective-morphism theorems are not proved here; they belong to VI/38.
\end{itemize}

\section{Boundaries of VI/37}
VI/37 owns projective schemes defined by homogeneous quotients, their affine-chart equations, homogeneous coordinate rings, projective hypersurfaces, scheme-theoretic intersections and unions, saturation, projective closure, affine cones, and basic component/reducedness diagnostics.  VI/38 develops projective morphisms, general closed embeddings, and the embedding theorems for Veronese and Segre maps.  Divisors, line bundles in general, Picard groups, and sheaf cohomology remain later topics.

\section*{Chapter synthesis}
A projective scheme is affine scheme theory organized by homogeneous localization.  Homogeneous equations define quotient graded rings, standard opens turn those quotients into ordinary affine coordinate rings, and the resulting pieces glue inside projective space.  Saturation separates genuine projective geometry from algebra supported only at the irrelevant cone vertex.  With this distinction in place, homogeneous coordinate rings can be used safely to compute hypersurfaces, intersections, closures, components, and nilpotent thickenings.  The next chapter turns these constructions into a general theory of projective morphisms and closed embeddings.
