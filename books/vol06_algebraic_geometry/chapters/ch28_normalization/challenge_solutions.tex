% VI28-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-01
\subsection*{Challenge 1 solution}
\begin{solution}
Let
\[
A=k[t^m,t^n]\subseteq k[t],
\qquad
\gcd(m,n)=1,
\qquad
m,n\ge2.
\]
First compute the fraction field. Bezout gives integers \(a,b\) with
\[
am+bn=1.
\]
After moving negative powers to the denominator, this expresses \(t\) as a quotient of monomials in
\(t^m,t^n\). Hence
\[
\Frac(A)=k(t).
\]

The element \(t\) is integral over \(A\), since it satisfies
\[
T^m-t^m=0.
\]
Therefore \(k[t]=A[t]\) is integral over \(A\), so
\[
k[t]\subseteq\overline A^{\,k(t)}.
\]
Conversely \(k[t]\) is a PID, hence integrally closed in \(k(t)\). Since it is an integrally closed intermediate
domain containing \(A\), minimality of the integral closure gives
\[
\overline A^{\,k(t)}\subseteq k[t].
\]
Thus
\[
\boxed{\overline A=k[t].}
\]

We now locate the defect. Choose \(a>0\) with
\[
an\equiv1\pmod m,
\]
and write \(an-bm=1\) with \(b\ge0\). On \(D(t^m)\),
\[
t=\frac{(t^n)^a}{(t^m)^b}\in A_{t^m}.
\]
Hence
\[
A_{t^m}=k[t,t^{-1}]=k[t]_{t^m},
\]
so normalization is an isomorphism over \(D(t^m)\).

The complement is
\[
V(t^m)=V(t^m,t^n),
\]
because \((t^n)^m=(t^m)^n\). This is the single point corresponding to evaluation \(t=0\). Therefore the
normalization repairs only the monomial-curve point at the origin; away from it the original curve is already
normal.
\end{solution}

% VI28-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-02
\subsection*{Challenge 2 solution}
\begin{solution}
Let
\[
A=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^2(x+1)),
\qquad
\operatorname{char}k\ne2.
\]
The defining polynomial is irreducible in \(k[x,y]\): viewed as a quadratic in \(y\), it would factor only if
\(x+1\) were a square in \(k[x]\), which it is not. Hence \(A\) is a domain.

Inside \(K=\Frac(A)\), define
\[
t=\frac yx.
\]
Then the defining equation gives
\[
t^2=\frac{y^2}{x^2}=x+1.
\]
Thus
\[
x=t^2-1,
\qquad
y=tx=t(t^2-1).
\]
Consequently every generator of \(A\) lies in \(k[t]\), while \(t=y/x\in K\); hence
\[
K=k(t).
\]

The equation
\[
T^2-(x+1)=0
\]
is monic with coefficients in \(A\), so \(t\) is integral over \(A\). Adjoining \(t\) gives
\[
A[t]=k[t],
\]
because \(x=t^2-1\) and \(y=t(t^2-1)\). Therefore \(k[t]\) is integral over \(A\). Since \(k[t]\) is a PID,
it is integrally closed in \(k(t)\); hence
\[
\boxed{\overline A=k[t].}
\]

The normalization map is
\[
\Spec k[t]\longrightarrow\Spec A,
\qquad
x=t^2-1,\quad y=t(t^2-1).
\]
At the node \((0,0)\), the equation \(t^2-1=0\) gives \(t=\pm1\), so the two branches are separated into two
distinct points upstairs.
\end{solution}

% VI28-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-03
\subsection*{Challenge 3 solution}
\begin{solution}
Let \(f:Z\to X\) be dominant with \(Z\) normal integral. We construct the factorization entirely from affine
rings.

Fix \(U=\Spec A\subseteq X\). Cover \(f^{-1}(U)\) by affine opens
\[
W_\lambda=\Spec B_\lambda.
\]
Dominance gives an injective map
\[
K(X)\hookrightarrow K(Z).
\]
For \(z\in\overline A\subseteq K(X)\), its image in \(K(Z)\) satisfies a monic equation
\[
z^r+a_{r-1}z^{r-1}+\cdots+a_0=0
\]
with \(a_i\in A\). Under \(A\to B_\lambda\), the same equation shows that \(z\) is integral over
\(B_\lambda\). Since \(B_\lambda\) is integrally closed in \(K(Z)\),
\[
z\in B_\lambda.
\]
Thus each \(A\to B_\lambda\) has a uniquely determined extension
\[
\overline A\to B_\lambda.
\]

On an overlap \(W_\lambda\cap W_\mu\), choose a common distinguished affine refinement. Both extended maps send
each \(z\in\overline A\) to the same element of \(K(Z)\), so after localization they agree. Therefore the affine
morphisms
\[
W_\lambda\to\Spec\overline A
\]
glue to a morphism
\[
f^{-1}(U)\to U^\nu.
\]

Now take two affine target charts \(U,V\subseteq X\). On a common distinguished refinement of \(U\cap V\), the
integral closures are identified by localization, and both constructed maps again come from the same embedding
\(K(X)\hookrightarrow K(Z)\). Hence the maps over \(U\) and \(V\) agree and glue globally:
\[
\widetilde f:Z\to X^\nu.
\]
By construction \(\nu\circ\widetilde f=f\).

Uniqueness is ring-theoretic as well. Any factorization must send \(z\in\overline A\subseteq K(X)\) to its fixed
image in \(K(Z)\). Thus every affine ring map \(\overline A\to B_\lambda\) is forced, so the global factorization
is unique.
\end{solution}

% VI28-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-04
\subsection*{Challenge 4 solution}
\begin{solution}
The finite-normalization theorem uses a strong finiteness hypothesis: a finitely generated domain over a field is
a Noetherian \(k\)-algebra of the class for which the integral-closure finiteness theorem applies. An arbitrary
integral extension need not be finite as a module.

Here is an explicit non-Noetherian warning. Let
\[
B=k[x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]
\]
and
\[
A=k[x_1^2,x_1^3,x_2^2,x_2^3,x_3^2,x_3^3,\ldots]\subseteq B.
\]
Both are domains. For every \(i\),
\[
x_i=\frac{x_i^3}{x_i^2}\in\Frac(A),
\]
so
\[
\Frac(A)=\Frac(B).
\]
Each \(x_i\) is integral over \(A\), since it satisfies
\[
T^2-x_i^2=0.
\]
Every element of \(B\) involves only finitely many variables, hence belongs to a finite extension obtained by
adjoining finitely many integral \(x_i\)'s. Thus \(B\) is integral over \(A\).

The ring \(B\) is a UFD: every polynomial uses only finitely many variables and factors uniquely in the
corresponding finite-variable polynomial ring. Hence \(B\) is integrally closed. Since \(B\) is both integral over
\(A\) and an integrally closed intermediate domain in their common fraction field,
\[
\boxed{\overline A=B.}
\]

But \(B\) is not finite as an \(A\)-module. Let
\[
\mathfrak m=(x_1^2,x_1^3,x_2^2,x_2^3,\ldots)\subseteq A.
\]
Then \(A/\mathfrak m\cong k\). If \(B\) were a finite \(A\)-module,
\[
B/\mathfrak mB
\]
would be finite-dimensional over \(k\). Yet the classes
\[
1,x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots
\]
are linearly independent in that quotient, because \(\mathfrak mB\) contains no nonzero linear combination of
the degree-one monomials. Hence the quotient is infinite-dimensional, contradiction.

Therefore normalization is always integral, but without finiteness hypotheses it need not be finite.
\end{solution}

% VI28-DETAILED-CHALLENGE-05
\subsection*{Challenge 5 solution}
\begin{solution}
Fix a prime \(p\) and let
\[
k=\mathbb F_p(u),
\qquad
L=k(t),
\qquad
t^p=u.
\]
Then \(L/k\) is a nontrivial purely inseparable extension of degree \(p\). The scheme
\[
X=\Spec L
\]
is normal because \(L\) is a field. Thus \(X^\nu=X\).

Base-change \(X\) from \(k\) to \(L\):
\[
X_L=\Spec(L\otimes_kL).
\]
Writing
\[
L\cong k[T]/(T^p-u),
\]
we obtain
\[
L\otimes_kL
\cong
L[T]/(T^p-u).
\]
Inside \(L[T]\),
\[
T^p-u=T^p-t^p=(T-t)^p.
\]
Setting \(\varepsilon=T-t\) gives
\[
\boxed{
L\otimes_kL\cong L[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^p).
}
\]
This ring is nonreduced, so \((X^\nu)_L=X_L\) is not normal.

The naive formula
\[
(X^\nu)_L\stackrel?{\cong}(X_L)^\nu
\]
therefore cannot hold. In fact the right side is not even defined by the chapter's integral-scheme
normalization until one removes the nilpotent thickening. After reduction,
\[
(X_L)_{\mathrm{red}}=\Spec L,
\]
whose normalization is itself. Thus the natural normal object after reduction is \(\Spec L\), whereas base
change of the original normalization is the nonreduced scheme
\[
\Spec L[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^p).
\]
This explicitly exhibits the failure of normalization to commute with arbitrary purely inseparable base change
and explains why geometric normality requires additional hypotheses.
\end{solution}
