\label{ch:reduced-nonreduced-geometry}
\providecommand{\Nil}{\operatorname{Nil}}

\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

The preceding chapters developed the topology of prime spectra.  That topology is powerful,
but it deliberately forgets one kind of algebraic information: nilpotent elements.  The
simplest warning is already striking:
\[
\Spec k[t]/(t)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\Spec k[t]/(t^2)
\]
have the same underlying one-point topological space, even though the two rings are not
isomorphic.  The second ring contains a nonzero element whose square is zero.  Scheme
theory remembers this infinitesimal information even when ordinary point-set topology does
not.

This chapter makes that phenomenon systematic.  We study reduced rings, the nilradical,
the reduced quotient, radical ideals, nilpotent thickenings, and the way reduction interacts
with localization.  We also separate three notions that are often confused:
\emph{reduced}, \emph{irreducible}, and \emph{integral}.  Finally, we preview a genuinely
scheme-theoretic phenomenon: reducedness may be destroyed by a field extension in positive
characteristic.

The chapter remains centered on affine spectra and quotient rings.  The full local-ring
criterion belongs to VI/11, the global structure sheaf to VI/17, affine schemes as locally
ringed spaces to VI/18, and integral schemes/function fields to VI/27.

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item recognize nilpotent elements and compute the nilradical;
\item decide whether a ring or affine quotient is reduced;
\item construct and use the reduction $A_{\mathrm{red}}$;
\item prove the universal property of reduction;
\item explain precisely why $\Spec A$ and $\Spec A_{\mathrm{red}}$ have the same topology;
\item translate radical ideals into reduced closed affine structures;
\item distinguish reduced, irreducible, and integral affine geometry;
\item analyze doubled points, double lines, and higher nilpotent thickenings;
\item prove that reduction commutes with localization;
\item test reducedness locally on a principal-open cover;
\item understand how products, polynomial extensions, and quotients affect reducedness;
\item recognize why base change can create nilpotents in positive characteristic;
\item identify mathematical information that is invisible to the underlying spectrum.
\end{itemize}

\section{Nilpotents and the nilradical}

\begin{definition}[Nilpotent element]
An element $a\in A$ is \emph{nilpotent} if
\[
a^n=0
\]
for some integer $n\ge 1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Nilradical]
The \emph{nilradical} of $A$ is
\[
\Nil(A)=\sqrt{(0)}=\{a\in A:a^n=0\text{ for some }n\ge1\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The nilpotent elements form an ideal.  Indeed, if $a^m=0$ and $b^n=0$, then every term in
$(a+b)^{m+n}$ contains either at least $m$ factors of $a$ or at least $n$ factors of $b$,
so $(a+b)^{m+n}=0$.  Also $(ra)^m=r^m a^m=0$ for every $r\in A$.

From VI/08 we retain the fundamental identity
\[
\Nil(A)=\bigcap_{\mathfrak p\in\Spec A}\mathfrak p.
\]
Thus nilpotents lie in every prime ideal.  Equivalently, no point of the spectrum can
separate a nilpotent element from zero.

\begin{definition}[Reduced ring]
A commutative ring $A$ is \emph{reduced} if it has no nonzero nilpotent elements, or equivalently if
\[
\Nil(A)=0.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
For a ring $A$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $A$ is reduced;
\item $(0)$ is a radical ideal;
\item $a^n=0$ implies $a=0$ for every $a\in A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\section{The reduced quotient}

Every ring has a canonical reduced quotient.

\begin{definition}[Reduction of a ring]
Define
\[
A_{\mathrm{red}}:=A/\Nil(A)=A/\sqrt{(0)}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
The ring $A_{\mathrm{red}}$ is reduced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $\bar a\in A/\Nil(A)$ is nilpotent.  Then $\bar a^n=0$ for some $n$, so
$a^n\in\Nil(A)$.  Hence $(a^n)^m=0$ for some $m$, and therefore $a^{nm}=0$.  Thus
$a\in\Nil(A)$ and $\bar a=0$.
\end{proof}

The reduction is canonical in a stronger sense.

\begin{theorem}[Universal property of reduction]
Let $B$ be reduced and let $\varphi:A\to B$ be a ring homomorphism.  Then $\varphi$ factors
uniquely through the quotient map $\pi:A\to A_{\mathrm{red}}$:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large]
A \arrow[r,"\varphi"] \arrow[d,two heads,"\pi"'] & B\\
A_{\mathrm{red}} \arrow[ur,dashed,"\bar\varphi"'] &
\end{tikzcd}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $a\in\Nil(A)$, then $a^n=0$ for some $n$, so $\varphi(a)^n=0$.  Because $B$ is
reduced, $\varphi(a)=0$.  Therefore $\Nil(A)\subseteq\ker\varphi$, and the universal
property of quotients gives a unique map $\bar\varphi:A/\Nil(A)\to B$.
\end{proof}

\section{Why topology cannot see nilpotents}

The quotient map $A\to A_{\mathrm{red}}$ does not change the prime spectrum.

\begin{theorem}[Topological invariance under reduction]
The quotient map induces a natural homeomorphism
\[
\Spec A_{\mathrm{red}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Spec A.
\]
Under this homeomorphism, a prime $\mathfrak p/\Nil(A)$ corresponds to
$\mathfrak p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Prime ideals of $A/\Nil(A)$ correspond to prime ideals of $A$ containing $\Nil(A)$.
Every prime ideal of $A$ contains the nilradical, so this gives a bijection on points.
For any ideal $J\supseteq\Nil(A)$,
\[
V_{A/\Nil(A)}(J/\Nil(A))
\longleftrightarrow
V_A(J),
\]
so the bijection preserves closed sets and is a homeomorphism.
\end{proof}

Thus the underlying topological space of an affine spectrum remembers all prime ideals and
all specialization relations, but forgets nilpotent thickness.

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_doubled_point.tex}

\begin{remark}
The word ``same'' must be used carefully.  Reduction preserves the underlying spectrum as a
topological space, but it changes the ring of functions whenever $A$ has nonzero
nilpotents.  The full scheme therefore changes even though its points and closed subsets do
not.
\end{remark}

\section{Radical ideals and reduced quotients}

Nilpotent structure appears naturally in quotients.

\begin{theorem}
Let $I\subseteq A$ be an ideal.  Then
\[
A/I\text{ is reduced}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
I\text{ is radical}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
(A/I)_{\mathrm{red}}\cong A/\sqrt I.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The nilradical of $A/I$ is
\[
\Nil(A/I)=\sqrt{(0)}_{A/I}=\sqrt I/I.
\]
Thus $A/I$ is reduced exactly when $\sqrt I=I$.  Quotienting $A/I$ by its nilradical
gives
\[
(A/I)/(\sqrt I/I)\cong A/\sqrt I.
\]
\end{proof}

The topological equality
\[
V(I)=V(\sqrt I)
\]
therefore has a scheme-theoretic interpretation: $A/I$ and $A/\sqrt I$ define the same
underlying closed subset, but the former may carry nilpotent structure.

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_radical_quotient.tex}

\section{Reduced, irreducible, and integral are different notions}

A reduced ring need not be a domain.  The basic example is
\[
k[x,y]/(xy),
\]
which is reduced but has zero divisors.  Its spectrum is the union of two irreducible
components.

Conversely, an irreducible spectrum need not come from a reduced ring.  For example,
\[
A=k[x,y]/(x^2)
\]
has reduction $k[y]$, so $\Spec A$ is homeomorphic to the affine line and is irreducible,
but $\bar x\neq0$ and $\bar x^2=0$.

\begin{theorem}[Affine integral criterion]
For a ring $A$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $A$ is an integral domain;
\item $A$ is reduced and $\Spec A$ is irreducible.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $A$ is a domain, then it is reduced and $(0)$ is prime, so $\Spec A$ is irreducible.
Conversely, if $A$ is reduced, then $\Nil(A)=0$.  If $\Spec A$ is irreducible, VI/05
shows that $\Nil(A)$ is prime.  Hence $(0)$ is prime, so $A$ is a domain.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_property_dictionary.tex}

The corresponding theorem for arbitrary schemes is a substantive legacy problem and is
reserved for VI/27, where the definition of an integral scheme and its function field are
developed globally.

\section{Nilpotent thickenings}

The quotient $A/I$ depends on more than the support $V(I)$.  If $I$ is not radical, then
$A/I$ contains nilpotents and should be thought of as a \emph{thickened} version of the
reduced closed set defined by $\sqrt I$.

The simplest family is
\[
A_n=k[t]/(t^n),\qquad n\ge1.
\]
Every $A_n$ has a one-point spectrum, but for $n>1$ the class of $t$ is a nonzero
nilpotent.  The integer $n$ records a depth of infinitesimal structure invisible to the
underlying point.

A geometric two-variable example is the double line
\[
k[x,y]/(y^2).
\]
Its reduction is
\[
k[x,y]/(y)\cong k[x].
\]
Both have the same topological support $y=0$, but only the first remembers a first-order
normal thickening of that line.

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_double_line.tex}

\begin{definition}[Nilpotent thickening, affine form]
Let $I\subseteq A$ be a nilpotent ideal, meaning $I^N=0$ for some $N$.  The quotient map
\[
A\twoheadrightarrow A/I
\]
induces a homeomorphism
\[
\Spec(A/I)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Spec A.
\]
We call this an affine nilpotent thickening relationship.
\end{definition}

The nilpotence hypothesis is stronger than necessary for the homeomorphism: it is enough
that every element of $I$ be nilpotent, i.e. $I\subseteq\Nil(A)$.  In Noetherian rings the
nilradical is nilpotent, but in arbitrary rings it need not be; Challenge VI.10.C1 explores
this distinction.

\section{Reduction and localization}

Reducedness behaves well under localization.

\begin{proposition}
If $A$ is reduced and $S\subseteq A$ is multiplicatively closed, then $S^{-1}A$ is reduced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose $(a/s)^n=0$ in $S^{-1}A$.  Then there exists $u\in S$ such that
$ua^n=0$.  Hence $(ua)^n=u^n a^n=0$.  Since $A$ is reduced, $ua=0$, so $a/s=0$ in
$S^{-1}A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reduction commutes with localization]
For $f\in A$, there is a natural isomorphism
\[
(A_{\mathrm{red}})_{\bar f}
\cong
(A_f)_{\mathrm{red}}.
\]
More generally, for every multiplicative set $S$,
\[
S^{-1}(A_{\mathrm{red}})\cong (S^{-1}A)_{\mathrm{red}}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Localization commutes with quotients:
\[
S^{-1}(A/\Nil(A))
\cong
S^{-1}A/S^{-1}\Nil(A).
\]
The nilradical of $S^{-1}A$ is exactly $S^{-1}\Nil(A)$.  Therefore the quotient on the
right is the reduction of $S^{-1}A$.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_localization_square.tex}

Reducedness is also local in the Zariski topology.

\begin{theorem}[Local criterion for reducedness]
For a ring $A$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $A$ is reduced;
\item $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is reduced for every prime $\mathfrak p$;
\item there exists a principal-open cover
      $\Spec A=\bigcup_{i=1}^r D(f_i)$ such that each $A_{f_i}$ is reduced.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $A$ is reduced, then every localization of $A$ is reduced, so (1) implies (2).

Assume (2), and let $a\in A$ satisfy $a^n=0$.  Then $a/1=0$ in $A_{\mathfrak p}$ for
every prime $\mathfrak p$.  If $a\ne0$, its annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(a)$ is a
proper ideal, so it is contained in some prime $\mathfrak p$.  But $a/1=0$ in
$A_{\mathfrak p}$ means that some $s\notin\mathfrak p$ satisfies $sa=0$, hence
$s\in\operatorname{Ann}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak p$, a contradiction.  Thus $a=0$, and
(2) implies (1).

Clearly (1) implies (3), for instance by the one-set principal-open cover
$D(1)=\Spec A$.  Finally assume (3).  If $a^n=0$ in $A$, then $a/1=0$ in every
$A_{f_i}$.  Hence for each $i$ there exists $N_i$ with $f_i^{N_i}a=0$.  Since the
$D(f_i)$ cover $\Spec A$, the ideal $(f_1,\dots,f_r)$ has radical $(1)$.  Therefore
some power of $(f_1,\dots,f_r)$ is contained in the ideal
$(f_1^{N_1},\dots,f_r^{N_r})$ after enlarging the exponents if necessary; because the
radical is $(1)$, this latter ideal is the unit ideal.  Thus a linear combination of
elements annihilating $a$ equals $1$, and consequently $a=0$.  Hence (3) implies (1).
\end{proof}

\section{Basic constructions and reducedness}

\begin{proposition}[Products]
A finite product
\[
A_1\times\cdots\times A_r
\]
is reduced if and only if every $A_i$ is reduced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An element $(a_1,\dots,a_r)$ is nilpotent exactly when each coordinate $a_i$ is nilpotent.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Polynomial extensions]
The polynomial ring $A[t]$ is reduced if and only if $A$ is reduced.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $A[t]$ is reduced then its subring of constant polynomials is reduced.  Conversely,
assume $A$ is reduced and $f(t)^n=0$.  Every prime $\mathfrak p$ of $A$ gives a map
\[
A[t]\to (A/\mathfrak p)[t],
\]
and the target is a domain.  Hence the image of $f$ is zero for every prime
$\mathfrak p$, so every coefficient of $f$ lies in every prime of $A$, hence in
$\Nil(A)=0$.  Thus $f=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{warning}[Quotients]
A quotient of a reduced ring need not be reduced.  For example, $k[t]$ is reduced but
\[
k[t]/(t^2)
\]
is not.  The correct criterion is that the quotient ideal be radical.
\end{warning}

\section{Finite reduced algebras and discrete geometry}

Reducedness becomes especially concrete in finite-dimensional algebras.

\begin{proposition}
Let $k$ be algebraically closed and let $A$ be a finite-dimensional commutative
$k$-algebra.  If $A$ is reduced, then
\[
A\cong k^r
\]
for some $r\ge0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A finite-dimensional $k$-algebra is Artinian, so it has finitely many maximal ideals
$\mathfrak m_1,\dots,\mathfrak m_r$, and its nilradical equals the intersection of these
maximal ideals.  Since $A$ is reduced,
\[
\bigcap_i\mathfrak m_i=0.
\]
Distinct maximal ideals are comaximal, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives
\[
A\cong\prod_i A/\mathfrak m_i.
\]
Each quotient is a finite field extension of the algebraically closed field $k$, hence is
$k$.
\end{proof}

Thus a finite reduced affine $k$-scheme over an algebraically closed field is simply a
finite collection of reduced points.  Nonreduced finite algebras are exactly where
infinitesimal multiplicity enters.

\section{Infinitesimal information carried by dual numbers}

The dual numbers
\[
k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)
\]
have only one point, but they can record first-order variation.  A $k$-algebra map
\[
k[t]\longrightarrow k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)
\]
is determined by
\[
t\longmapsto a+b\varepsilon.
\]
All values of $b$ induce the same map on underlying topological points, namely the point
$t=a$, but different values of $b$ are different ring maps.  The coefficient $b$ is an
infinitesimal direction invisible to point-set topology.

and cotangent-space calculations.

\section{Reducedness and base change: a warning}

Localization and polynomial extension preserve reducedness, but arbitrary field extension
need not.

Let
\[
k=\FF_p(s),
\qquad
A=k[x]/(x^p-s).
\]
The polynomial $x^p-s$ is irreducible over $k$, so $A$ is a field and hence reduced.
Now extend scalars to
\[
K=\FF_p(s^{1/p}).
\]
Writing $\alpha=s^{1/p}\in K$, characteristic $p$ gives
\[
x^p-s=(x-\alpha)^p.
\]
Therefore
\[
A\otimes_k K
\cong
K[x]/((x-\alpha)^p),
\]
which is nonreduced.

\input{chapters/ch10_reduced_nonreduced_geometry/figures/vi10_base_change_failure.tex}

This motivates a later refinement: a $k$-algebra is \emph{geometrically reduced} if it
remains reduced after suitable field extensions.  The systematic theory belongs with base
change in VI/24.

\section{Comparison chart and common pitfalls}

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.22}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.18\textwidth}p{0.20\textwidth}p{0.28\textwidth}}
\toprule
Ring & Reduced? & Irreducible? & Key feature\\
\midrule
$k[t]$ & yes & yes & domain\\
$k[t]/(t^2)$ & no & yes & doubled point\\
$k[x,y]/(xy)$ & yes & no & two reduced components\\
$k[x,y]/(x^2)$ & no & yes & thickened line\\
$k\times k$ & yes & no & two disconnected points\\
$\ZZ/12\ZZ$ & no & no & nilpotents and several primes\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Keep the following distinctions explicit:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``Reduced'' means no nonzero nilpotents.  It says nothing by itself about the number
      of irreducible components.
\item ``Irreducible spectrum'' is a topological condition.  It does not force the ring to
      be reduced.
\item The spectrum cannot detect whether an ideal is $I$ or $\sqrt I$; quotient rings can.
\item A one-point spectrum need not be a field and need not be reduced.
\item A quotient of a reduced ring need not be reduced.
\item Reducedness survives localization but can fail after inseparable base change.
\item In a nonreduced irreducible ring, the generic point is $\Nil(A)$, not necessarily
      $(0)$.
\end{itemize}
