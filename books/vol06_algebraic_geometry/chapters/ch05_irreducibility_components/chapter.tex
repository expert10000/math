\chapter{Irreducibility, Components and Connectedness}
\label{ch:irreducibility-components-connectedness}

\section*{Purpose and learning goals}

Algebraic sets can split into simpler pieces.  Some decompositions are genuine and
intrinsic, while others merely reflect a poor choice of equations.  This chapter develops
two complementary notions that measure whether a space splits:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{irreducibility}, which forbids decomposition into two proper closed pieces;
  \item \emph{connectedness}, which forbids decomposition into two disjoint nonempty open
        pieces.
\end{itemize}

For affine algebraic sets, irreducibility is controlled by prime ideals and integral
domains, while connectedness is controlled by idempotents and product decompositions of
coordinate rings.  The two notions are related but not equivalent.

Throughout, \(k\) is a field and
\[
X\subseteq \mathbb A_k^n
\]
is an affine algebraic set.  When the connectedness--idempotent correspondence is invoked
in its strongest classical point-set form, we explicitly assume that \(k\) is algebraically
closed.

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item characterize irreducible spaces using nonempty open subsets;
  \item prove that nonempty open subsets of irreducible spaces are dense and irreducible;
  \item translate irreducibility of \(X\) into primality of \(I(X)\) and domain structure
        of \(k[X]\);
  \item prove existence and uniqueness of irreducible components in affine algebraic sets;
  \item distinguish irreducible components from connected components;
  \item characterize disconnected affine algebraic sets using nontrivial idempotents;
  \item pass between minimal primes and irreducible components;
  \item use component-intersection graphs to test connectedness;
  \item analyze explicit reducible algebraic sets by factoring equations and computing
        minimal components.
\end{itemize}

\section{Irreducible topological spaces}

\begin{definition}[Irreducible space]
A nonempty topological space \(X\) is \emph{irreducible} if whenever
\[
X=F\cup G
\]
with \(F,G\subseteq X\) closed, then
\[
F=X
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
G=X.
\]
Equivalently, \(X\) cannot be written as a union of two proper closed subsets.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Open-set criterion]
\label{prop:vi05-open-criterion}
For a nonempty topological space \(X\), the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(X\) is irreducible;
  \item every two nonempty open subsets of \(X\) intersect;
  \item every nonempty open subset of \(X\) is dense.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(X\) is irreducible and let \(U,V\subseteq X\) be nonempty open subsets.  If
\(U\cap V=\varnothing\), then
\[
X=(X\setminus U)\cup(X\setminus V)
\]
is a union of two proper closed subsets, contradiction.  Hence (1) implies (2).

Assume (2), and let \(U\subseteq X\) be nonempty open.  If
\[
\overline U\neq X,
\]
then
\[
X\setminus\overline U
\]
is a nonempty open set disjoint from \(U\), contradicting (2).  Thus \(U\) is dense, so
(2) implies (3).

Finally, assume every nonempty open subset is dense.  If
\[
X=F\cup G
\]
with \(F,G\) proper closed subsets, then
\[
U=X\setminus F
\]
is nonempty open.  Since \(U\subseteq G\) and \(G\) is closed,
\[
\overline U\subseteq G\neq X,
\]
contradicting density.  Thus (3) implies (1).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:vi05-nonempty-open-irreducible}
Every nonempty open subset of an irreducible space is irreducible and dense in the ambient
space.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Density is part of \cref{prop:vi05-open-criterion}.  Let \(U\subseteq X\) be nonempty
open and let \(U_1,U_2\subseteq U\) be nonempty open subsets in the subspace topology.
Write
\[
U_i=U\cap V_i
\]
with \(V_i\) open in \(X\).  Then \(U\cap V_1\) and \(U\cap V_2\) are nonempty open
subsets of \(X\), so their intersection is nonempty.  Hence \(U_1\cap U_2\neq\varnothing\),
and \(U\) is irreducible.
\end{proof}

\section{Closure preserves irreducibility}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vi05-closure-irreducible}
If \(Y\) is an irreducible subset of a topological space \(X\), then its closure
\[
\overline Y
\]
is irreducible.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose
\[
\overline Y=F\cup G
\]
with \(F,G\) closed in \(\overline Y\).  Then
\[
Y=(Y\cap F)\cup(Y\cap G).
\]
The subsets \(Y\cap F\) and \(Y\cap G\) are closed in \(Y\).  Since \(Y\) is irreducible,
one of them is all of \(Y\).  If, for example, \(Y\subseteq F\), then by closedness of
\(F\) in \(\overline Y\),
\[
\overline Y\subseteq F.
\]
Hence \(F=\overline Y\).
\end{proof}

This fact is especially useful for parametrized subsets: if the parameter space is
irreducible and the image is dense in its closure, the closure is irreducible.

\section{Irreducibility and coordinate rings}

The topological definition becomes algebraic for affine algebraic sets.

\begin{theorem}[Prime-ideal criterion]
\label{thm:vi05-prime-criterion}
For an affine algebraic set \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(X\) is irreducible;
  \item \(I(X)\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is prime;
  \item \(k[X]\) is an integral domain.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The equivalence of (2) and (3) is the standard quotient criterion:
\[
k[X]=k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I(X)
\]
is a domain exactly when \(I(X)\) is prime.

Assume \(X\) is irreducible.  Suppose
\[
fg\in I(X).
\]
Then \(fg\) vanishes on \(X\), so
\[
X\subseteq V(fg)=V(f)\cup V(g).
\]
Inside \(X\),
\[
X=V_X(f)\cup V_X(g).
\]
Irreducibility implies
\[
X=V_X(f)
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
X=V_X(g).
\]
Thus
\[
f\in I(X)
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
g\in I(X),
\]
so \(I(X)\) is prime.

Conversely, assume \(I(X)\) is prime and suppose
\[
X=Y\cup Z
\]
with \(Y,Z\) closed in \(X\).  We may write
\[
Y=V_X(J),
\qquad
Z=V_X(K).
\]
If both are proper, choose
\[
f\in I(Y)\setminus I(X),
\qquad
g\in I(Z)\setminus I(X).
\]
Since every point of \(X\) lies in \(Y\) or \(Z\),
\[
fg
\]
vanishes on all of \(X\).  Hence
\[
fg\in I(X).
\]
Primality forces \(f\in I(X)\) or \(g\in I(X)\), contradiction.  Thus one of \(Y,Z\)
equals \(X\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:vi05-affine-space-irreducible}
If \(k\) is infinite, then
\[
\mathbb A_k^n
\]
is irreducible.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For infinite \(k\),
\[
I(\mathbb A_k^n)=(0),
\]
and the polynomial ring \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is a domain.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Classical terminology]
Many texts reserve the word \emph{affine variety} for an irreducible affine algebraic set.
Under that convention,
\[
X\text{ is an affine variety}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k[X]\text{ is a domain}.
\]
Other texts allow ``variety'' to be reducible.  We will always state irreducibility
explicitly when it matters.
\end{remark}

\section{Reducibility and zero divisors}

The contrapositive of \cref{thm:vi05-prime-criterion} is often computationally useful.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:vi05-reducible-zero-divisors}
An affine algebraic set \(X\) is reducible if and only if its coordinate ring \(k[X]\)
has nonzero zero divisors.
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}
For
\[
X=V(xy)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2,
\]
the classes \(\bar x,\bar y\in k[X]\) are nonzero and satisfy
\[
\bar x\bar y=0.
\]
Geometrically,
\[
X=V(x)\cup V(y),
\]
the union of the two coordinate axes.
\end{example}

Zero divisors detect reducibility, but they do \emph{not} by themselves detect
disconnectedness.  The crossing axes are reducible yet connected.

\section{Noetherianity of affine algebraic sets}

To obtain a finite decomposition into irreducible components, we use the Noetherian property.

\begin{definition}[Noetherian topological space]
A topological space \(X\) is \emph{Noetherian} if every descending chain of closed subsets
\[
F_1\supseteq F_2\supseteq F_3\supseteq\cdots
\]
stabilizes.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:vi05-affine-noetherian}
Every affine algebraic set is a Noetherian topological space in the Zariski topology.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n.
\]
A descending chain of closed subsets of \(X\),
\[
F_1\supseteq F_2\supseteq\cdots,
\]
gives an ascending chain of vanishing ideals
\[
I_X(F_1)\subseteq I_X(F_2)\subseteq\cdots
\]
inside \(k[X]\).  The ring \(k[X]\) is a quotient of
\(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), hence is Noetherian by Hilbert's basis theorem.  Therefore the ideal
chain stabilizes.  Applying \(V_X\) shows that the closed-set chain stabilizes.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Quasi-compactness]
\label{cor:vi05-quasicompact}
Every affine algebraic set is quasi-compact: every open cover has a finite subcover.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
This is true for every Noetherian topological space.  If an open cover had no finite
subcover, the complements would yield a family of closed sets with the finite intersection
property but empty total intersection.  Equivalently, one can choose a maximal closed subset
that cannot be finitely covered and obtain a contradiction by covering one of its points.
\end{proof}

\section{Irreducible components}

\begin{definition}[Irreducible component]
An \emph{irreducible component} of a topological space \(X\) is a maximal irreducible
closed subset of \(X\).
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Existence of a finite irreducible decomposition]
\label{thm:vi05-component-existence}
Every nonempty affine algebraic set \(X\) can be written as a finite union
\[
X=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_r
\]
where each \(X_i\) is irreducible and no \(X_i\) is contained in another.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Because \(X\) is Noetherian, consider the collection of closed subsets that cannot be written
as a finite union of irreducible closed subsets.  If this collection were nonempty, choose a
minimal member \(F\).  The set \(F\) is not irreducible, so
\[
F=F_1\cup F_2
\]
with \(F_1,F_2\subsetneq F\) closed.  By minimality, each \(F_i\) is a finite union of
irreducible closed subsets, hence so is \(F\), contradiction.  Thus every closed subset,
and in particular \(X\), has a finite irreducible decomposition.  Removing redundant pieces
gives the noncontainment condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of irreducible components]
\label{thm:vi05-component-uniqueness}
Suppose
\[
X=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_r
=
Y_1\cup\cdots\cup Y_s
\]
are two decompositions into irreducible closed subsets with no redundant containments.
Then
\[
r=s,
\]
and after reindexing,
\[
X_i=Y_i
\]
for all \(i\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix \(i\).  Since
\[
X_i\subseteq Y_1\cup\cdots\cup Y_s
\]
and \(X_i\) is irreducible, repeated use of the defining property shows
\[
X_i\subseteq Y_j
\]
for some \(j\).  Similarly, \(Y_j\subseteq X_\ell\) for some \(\ell\).  Therefore
\[
X_i\subseteq X_\ell.
\]
The noncontainment assumption forces \(\ell=i\), hence
\[
X_i=Y_j.
\]
Thus each \(X_i\) appears among the \(Y_j\).  By symmetry, each \(Y_j\) appears among the
\(X_i\).  The two collections therefore coincide up to reordering.
\end{proof}

\section{Minimal primes and components}

Assume now that \(k\) is algebraically closed.

For a radical ideal
\[
I=I(X)\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n],
\]
one can write
\[
I=\mathfrak p_1\cap\cdots\cap\mathfrak p_r
\]
where the \(\mathfrak p_i\) are the minimal prime ideals containing \(I\).

\begin{theorem}[Components from minimal primes]
\label{thm:vi05-minimal-primes}
The irreducible components of \(X=V(I)\) are exactly
\[
V(\mathfrak p_1),\dots,V(\mathfrak p_r),
\]
where \(\mathfrak p_1,\dots,\mathfrak p_r\) are the minimal primes over \(I\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each \(V(\mathfrak p_i)\) is irreducible by
\cref{thm:vi05-prime-criterion}.  Since
\[
I=\bigcap_i\mathfrak p_i,
\]
we have
\[
V(I)=\bigcup_iV(\mathfrak p_i).
\]
Minimality of the primes corresponds, under order reversal, to maximality of the associated
irreducible closed subsets.
\end{proof}

This gives a practical algebraic method: find the minimal primes of \(I(X)\), then take their
zero sets.

\section{Connectedness}

\begin{definition}[Connected space]
A topological space \(X\) is \emph{connected} if it cannot be written as a disjoint union
\[
X=U\sqcup V
\]
of two nonempty open subsets.
\end{definition}

Equivalently, the only subsets of \(X\) that are both open and closed are
\[
\varnothing
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
X.
\]

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vi05-irreducible-connected}
Every irreducible space is connected.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If
\[
X=U\sqcup V
\]
with \(U,V\) nonempty open, then \(U\) and \(V\) are also closed, because each is the
complement of the other.  Thus
\[
X=U\cup V
\]
is a union of two proper closed subsets, contradicting irreducibility.
\end{proof}

The converse fails.

\begin{example}[Reducible but connected]
The algebraic set
\[
X=V(xy)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2
\]
is the union
\[
V(x)\cup V(y)
\]
of two irreducible components.  They meet at the origin, so \(X\) is connected.
\end{example}

\section{Idempotents and disconnected affine algebraic sets}

We now assume that \(k\) is algebraically closed.

\begin{definition}[Idempotent]
An element \(e\) of a ring \(A\) is \emph{idempotent} if
\[
e^2=e.
\]
It is \emph{nontrivial} if
\[
e\neq0,1.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Connectedness--idempotent criterion]
\label{thm:vi05-idempotent-criterion}
Let \(X\) be an affine algebraic set over an algebraically closed field \(k\).  Then the
following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(X\) is disconnected;
  \item \(k[X]\) contains a nontrivial idempotent;
  \item there exist nonzero \(k\)-algebras \(A_1,A_2\) such that
        \[
        k[X]\cong A_1\times A_2.
        \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume \(e\in k[X]\) is a nontrivial idempotent.  At every point \(x\in X\),
\[
e(x)^2=e(x),
\]
so
\[
e(x)\in\{0,1\}.
\]
Define
\[
X_0=V_X(e),
\qquad
X_1=V_X(e-1).
\]
Then
\[
X=X_0\sqcup X_1.
\]
Both subsets are closed.  Each is also open because it is the complement of the other.
Nontriviality of \(e\) ensures both are nonempty.  Hence \(X\) is disconnected.

Conversely, suppose
\[
X=Y\sqcup Z
\]
with \(Y,Z\) nonempty and clopen.  Since they are closed algebraic subsets,
\[
V(I(Y)+I(Z))=Y\cap Z=\varnothing.
\]
By the Nullstellensatz,
\[
I(Y)+I(Z)=k[x_1,\dots,x_n].
\]
Choose
\[
f\in I(Y),
\qquad
g\in I(Z)
\]
with
\[
f+g=1.
\]
On \(Y\), \(f=0\) and hence \(g=1\); on \(Z\), \(g=0\).  Therefore the class
\[
e=[g]\in k[X]
\]
takes only the values \(0\) and \(1\), so
\[
e^2=e.
\]
It is nontrivial because both \(Y\) and \(Z\) are nonempty.

Finally, if \(e\) is a nontrivial idempotent in a ring \(A\), then
\[
A\longrightarrow eA\times(1-e)A,
\qquad
a\longmapsto(ea,(1-e)a)
\]
is an isomorphism.  Conversely, in a nontrivial product
\[
A_1\times A_2,
\]
the element
\[
(1,0)
\]
is a nontrivial idempotent.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:vi05-domain-connected}
If \(k[X]\) is a domain, then \(X\) is connected.
\end{corollary}

This also follows from irreducibility, but the idempotent criterion gives a different
algebraic perspective.

\section{Connected components versus irreducible components}

Irreducible components need not be disjoint.  Connected components, by contrast, form a
partition into maximal connected subsets.

For affine algebraic sets, there are finitely many irreducible components.  Their incidence
controls connectedness.

\begin{definition}[Component-intersection graph]
Let
\[
X=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_r
\]
be the irreducible-component decomposition.  The \emph{component-intersection graph}
\(\Gamma_X\) has one vertex for each \(X_i\), with an edge between \(X_i\) and \(X_j\)
when
\[
X_i\cap X_j\neq\varnothing.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Intersection-graph criterion]
\label{thm:vi05-intersection-graph}
An affine algebraic set \(X\) is connected if and only if its component-intersection graph
\(\Gamma_X\) is connected.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(\Gamma_X\) is disconnected.  Partition its vertices into two nonempty sets
\(A,B\) with no edges between them.  Let
\[
Y=\bigcup_{i\in A}X_i,
\qquad
Z=\bigcup_{j\in B}X_j.
\]
These are finite unions of closed sets, hence closed.  The absence of edges gives
\[
Y\cap Z=\varnothing,
\]
and
\[
X=Y\sqcup Z.
\]
Thus \(X\) is disconnected.

Conversely, suppose
\[
X=U\sqcup V
\]
is a separation into nonempty clopen subsets.  Each irreducible component \(X_i\) is
connected, hence must lie entirely in \(U\) or entirely in \(V\).  If
\[
X_i\cap X_j\neq\varnothing,
\]
then \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) must lie on the same side.  Therefore no graph edge crosses from
the collection of components in \(U\) to those in \(V\).  Since both collections are
nonempty, \(\Gamma_X\) is disconnected.
\end{proof}

This criterion makes the difference between irreducible and connected geometry very
concrete: connectedness allows several irreducible pieces, provided the pieces are linked
through intersections.

\section{Common pitfalls}

\begin{warning}[Reducible does not mean disconnected]
The crossing axes
\[
V(xy)
\]
are reducible but connected.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Connected does not mean irreducible]
Connectedness only forbids a separation into disjoint clopen pieces.  Components may still
overlap.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Zero divisors and idempotents detect different phenomena]
Zero divisors detect reducibility of \(k[X]\); nontrivial idempotents detect
disconnectedness.  A ring may have zero divisors but no nontrivial idempotents.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Irreducible components are maximal, not merely any irreducible subsets]
A line contained in an irreducible surface is irreducible, but it is not an irreducible
component of the surface.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Classical point sets versus schemes]
Later, \(\operatorname{Spec}A\) may contain generic points and nilpotent structure not
visible in the classical set \(V(I)\subseteq k^n\).  The scheme-theoretic notions of
irreducibility and connectedness will be revisited in that broader setting.
\end{warning}

\section{Chapter summary}

Irreducibility is the strongest basic notion of ``one piece'' in algebraic geometry.  For
an affine algebraic set,
\[
X\text{ irreducible}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
I(X)\text{ prime}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k[X]\text{ a domain}.
\]

Every affine algebraic set is Noetherian and therefore decomposes into finitely many
irreducible components,
\[
X=X_1\cup\cdots\cup X_r,
\]
uniquely up to order.

Connectedness is weaker:
\[
X\text{ irreducible}
\Longrightarrow
X\text{ connected},
\]
but not conversely.  Over an algebraically closed field,
\[
X\text{ disconnected}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k[X]\text{ has a nontrivial idempotent}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
k[X]\cong A_1\times A_2.
\]

Finally, if \(X_1,\dots,X_r\) are the irreducible components, then \(X\) is connected
exactly when the graph recording which components intersect is connected.

The next chapters change viewpoint from \(k\)-rational zero sets to prime ideals as
geometric points, beginning the passage from classical affine algebraic geometry to
\(\operatorname{Spec}\) and affine schemes.
