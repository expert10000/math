\begin{problem}[Why the plus construction is applied twice]
For a presheaf $\mathcal F$ of abelian groups, define $\mathcal F^+(U)$ as compatible families of sections on an open cover of $U$, modulo common refinement. Prove: (a) $\mathcal F^+$ is separated; (b) if $\mathcal F$ is separated then $\mathcal F^+$ is a sheaf; (c) consequently $\mathcal F^{++}$ is a sheaf.
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
This is the standard algebraic alternative to the espace-etale construction and explains the traditional ``two pluses theorem.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
Use the universal property, stalk preservation, and the fact that a morphism of sheaves is an isomorphism when it is so on every stalk.  Keep the distinction between a presheaf statement on sections and a local statement on germs explicit.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
For separatedness, refine two representatives until they agree locally. For gluing, refine all local representatives to one common cover and use separatedness to remove choice ambiguities.

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\begin{solution}
A plus-section is represented by a cover $U=\bigcup U_i$ and compatible $s_i\in\mathcal F(U_i)$; two representatives are equivalent if they agree after passing to a common refinement. If two plus-sections agree after restricting to a cover of $U$, choose representatives and refine simultaneously over that cover. The local equivalences then combine into a common refinement of $U$, so the two classes are equal. Thus $\mathcal F^+$ is separated.

Assume now that $\mathcal F$ is separated and let plus-sections on $U_a$ agree on overlaps. Choose representatives by original sections on covers $U_{a,i}$. Refine the overlaps so that representatives of the two plus-sections agree there. Separatedness of $\mathcal F$ guarantees that these agreements are independent of the refinement and satisfy the cocycle compatibility needed to regard all $s_{a,i}$ as one compatible family on the combined cover of $U$. Its equivalence class gives the required glued plus-section. Uniqueness follows from separatedness of $\mathcal F^+$. Hence $\mathcal F^+$ is a sheaf whenever $\mathcal F$ is separated.

For arbitrary $\mathcal F$, part (a) says $\mathcal F^+$ is separated; applying part (b) to it shows $\mathcal F^{++}$ is a sheaf.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
The first plus enforces uniqueness/locality; the second enforces existence of gluings.

\paragraph{Alternative approaches.}
Whenever possible one may replace the espace-etale model by the two-plus construction, or conversely replace a cover/refinement argument by the universal property.  The two approaches agree because both construct the same associated sheaf.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
Compare $\mathcal F^{++}$ with the espace-etale sheafification by universal properties.
