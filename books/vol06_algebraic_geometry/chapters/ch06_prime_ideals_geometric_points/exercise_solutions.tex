\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-prime-quotient}.}
If \(\mathfrak p\) is prime and \((a+\mathfrak p)(b+\mathfrak p)=0\), then
\(ab\in\mathfrak p\), so one of \(a,b\) lies in \(\mathfrak p\).  Thus the quotient has
no zero divisors.  Conversely, if the quotient is a domain and \(ab\in\mathfrak p\), then
the product of the two residue classes is zero, so one residue class is zero.  Hence one
factor belongs to \(\mathfrak p\).

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-maximal-prime}.}
If \(\mathfrak m\) is maximal, \(A/\mathfrak m\) is a field and therefore a domain, so
\(\mathfrak m\) is prime.  The ideal \((0)\subset k[t]\) is prime because \(k[t]\) is a
domain, but it is not maximal because \(k[t]\) is not a field.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-zero-ideal}.}
Since \(A/(0)\cong A\), the quotient criterion gives that \((0)\) is prime exactly when
\(A\) is a domain.  Therefore \((0)\) is prime in \(\mathbb Z\) and \(k[t]\), but not in
\(k[x,y]/(xy)\), where \(\bar x\bar y=0\) with both factors nonzero.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-point-ideal}.}
Evaluation at \(a\) is surjective because constants are attained.  Its kernel consists of
polynomials vanishing at \(a\), which is the ideal
\((x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n)\).  The first isomorphism theorem gives quotient \(k\), a field,
so the kernel is maximal.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-k-t-primes}.}
Every ideal of \(k[t]\) is principal.  A nonzero proper ideal \((f)\) is prime exactly when
\(f\) is irreducible, because \(k[t]/(f)\) is a domain exactly when \(f\) is irreducible.
Together with \((0)\), these are all primes.  If \(k\) is algebraically closed, every
irreducible polynomial is linear, so the primes are \((0)\) and \((t-a)\).

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-real-line}.}
The prime ideals are \((0)\), the linear ideals \((t-a)\), and ideals generated by
irreducible quadratics.  In particular \(t^2+1\) is irreducible, so its ideal is maximal and
prime.  It is not \((t-a)\) for real \(a\), since it has no real root.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-chain-plane}.}
The quotients are \(k[x,y]\), \(k[y]\), and \(k\), so the first two ideals are prime and
the last is maximal.  Their zero sets are the plane, the line \(x=0\), and the origin,
respectively, giving the reverse chain.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-order-reversal}.}
If \(\mathfrak p\subseteq\mathfrak q\), then order reversal of \(V\) gives
\(V(\mathfrak q)\subseteq V(\mathfrak p)\).  Conversely, the inclusion of zero sets gives
\[
\mathfrak p=I(V(\mathfrak p))\subseteq I(V(\mathfrak q))=\mathfrak q,
\]
using Nullstellensatz and the fact that primes are radical.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-xy-primes}.}
Since \(xy\in\mathfrak p\), primality gives \(x\in\mathfrak p\) or
\(y\in\mathfrak p\).  Geometrically, an irreducible locus contained in the union
\(V(x)\cup V(y)\) must lie in one axis.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-quotient-correspondence}.}
If \(I\subseteq\mathfrak p\), then
\((A/I)/(\mathfrak p/I)\cong A/\mathfrak p\), so \(\mathfrak p/I\) is prime exactly when
\(\mathfrak p\) is.  The inverse image of a prime under \(A\to A/I\) is prime and contains
\(I\).  The two constructions are inverse and plainly preserve inclusion.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-irred-prime}.}
If \(Y\) is irreducible and \(fg\in I(Y)\), then
\(Y\subseteq V(f)\cup V(g)\), so irreducibility forces \(Y\subseteq V(f)\) or
\(Y\subseteq V(g)\); hence one factor lies in \(I(Y)\).  Conversely, if \(I(Y)\) is prime,
then the coordinate ring is a domain, and the VI/05 criterion gives irreducibility.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-maximal-points}.}
Let \(\mathfrak m\) be maximal.  By the weak Nullstellensatz,
\(k[x,y]/\mathfrak m\cong k\).  The images of \(x,y\) are scalars \(a,b\in k\), so
\(x-a,y-b\in\mathfrak m\).  Maximality then gives
\(\mathfrak m=(x-a,y-b)\).  Uniqueness follows from evaluating the generators.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-Z-six}.}
The primes of \(\mathbb Z\) are \((0)\) and \((p)\) for prime integers \(p\).  A prime
\((p)\) contains \((n)\) exactly when \(p\mid n\).  Thus \((6)\) is contained in \((2)\)
and \((3)\); \((30)\) is contained in \((2),(3),(5)\); and every prime contains \((0)\).
The geometry records prime divisors.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-radical-not-prime}.}
In the UFD \(k[x,y]\), \((x)\cap(y)=(xy)\).  Since prime ideals are radical and an
intersection of radical ideals is radical, \((xy)\) is radical.  It is not prime because
\(xy\in(xy)\) while neither \(x\) nor \(y\) belongs to \((xy)\).

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-minimal-primes-cross}.}
Primes of \(A\) correspond to primes of \(k[x,y]\) containing \((xy)\).  Any such prime
contains \(x\) or \(y\), so the minimal choices are \((x)\) and \((y)\).  Their images are
therefore the minimal primes \((\bar x)\) and \((\bar y)\), corresponding to the two axes.

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-preimage-prime}.}
If \(ab\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak q)\), then
\(\varphi(a)\varphi(b)=\varphi(ab)\in\mathfrak q\).  Since \(\mathfrak q\) is prime,
one of \(\varphi(a),\varphi(b)\) lies in \(\mathfrak q\), so one of \(a,b\) lies in the
inverse image.  The inverse image is proper because a unital map sends \(1\) to \(1\notin\mathfrak q\).

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-products-ideals}.}
Suppose \(I\not\subseteq\mathfrak p\) and choose \(a\in I\setminus\mathfrak p\).  For every
\(b\in J\), \(ab\in IJ\subseteq\mathfrak p\).  Primality and \(a\notin\mathfrak p\)
force \(b\in\mathfrak p\).  Hence \(J\subseteq\mathfrak p\).

\paragraph{Solution to Exercise~\ref{ex:vi06-complex-splitting}.}
Over \(\mathbb R\), \(t^2+1\) is irreducible, so its ideal is maximal.  Over \(\mathbb C\),
\[
t^2+1=(t-i)(t+i),
\]
and neither factor belongs to \((t^2+1)\), so that ideal is not prime.  The two maximal
ideals are \((t-i)\) and \((t+i)\).
