\chapter{Algebraic Sets}
\label{ch:vi-algebraic-sets}

\section{Geometry from polynomial equations}
\label{sec:vi01-motivation}

Algebraic geometry begins with a simple operation: write down polynomial equations and
study their common solutions.  The decisive change of viewpoint is that we do not regard a
single equation in isolation.  We allow arbitrary families of polynomials, remember the
field over which solutions are sought, and organize the resulting solution sets so that
algebraic operations on polynomials translate into geometric operations on sets.

Let \(k\) be a field.  Throughout this chapter,
\[
  k[x_1,\dots,x_n]
\]
denotes the polynomial ring in \(n\) variables over \(k\), and
\[
  \mathbb A_k^n := k^n
\]
denotes affine \(n\)-space over \(k\).

The set \(\mathbb A_k^n\) is initially just a set of \(n\)-tuples.  Its geometry will be
created by declaring polynomial zero sets to be the distinguished subsets.

\subsection*{Learning goals}

After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item form the common zero locus of a family of polynomials;
  \item pass freely between a family of equations and the ideal it generates;
  \item prove the fundamental identities satisfied by algebraic sets;
  \item construct the vanishing ideal of an arbitrary subset of affine space;
  \item explain the order-reversing relation between equations and solution sets;
  \item recognize how the geometry changes when the ground field changes;
  \item distinguish a set of equations from the geometric set that it defines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Affine space and polynomial functions}
\label{sec:vi01-affine-space}

\begin{definition}[Affine \(n\)-space]
For a field \(k\), the \emph{affine \(n\)-space over \(k\)} is
\[
  \mathbb A_k^n = k^n.
\]
A point \(a\in \mathbb A_k^n\) is written
\[
  a=(a_1,\dots,a_n).
\]
\end{definition}

Every polynomial
\[
  f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]
\]
determines a function
\[
  f:\mathbb A_k^n\longrightarrow k,
  \qquad
  a\longmapsto f(a).
\]

The distinction between a polynomial as a formal algebraic expression and the function it
induces is harmless over infinite fields for many elementary purposes, but it becomes
important over finite fields; see \cref{sec:vi01-field-dependence}.

\begin{example}
In \(\mathbb A_{\mathbb R}^2\), the polynomial
\[
  f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1
\]
vanishes exactly on the unit circle.  The same formula over \(\mathbb C\) defines a
different geometric object: its points now have complex coordinates.
\end{example}

\section{Common zero loci}
\label{sec:vi01-zero-loci}

\begin{definition}[Vanishing set]
Let \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\).  The \emph{vanishing set} or \emph{zero locus} of
\(S\) is
\[
  V(S)
  :=
  \left\{
    a\in\mathbb A_k^n:
    f(a)=0\text{ for every }f\in S
  \right\}.
\]
If \(S=\{f_1,\dots,f_r\}\), we also write
\[
  V(f_1,\dots,f_r).
\]
\end{definition}

Thus \(V(S)\) is the simultaneous solution set of all equations
\[
  f=0,\qquad f\in S.
\]

\begin{proposition}[Only the generated ideal matters]
\label{prop:vi01-generated-ideal}
For every subset \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
  V(S)=V((S)),
\]
where \((S)\) is the ideal generated by \(S\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since \(S\subseteq (S)\), every point that annihilates all polynomials in \((S)\) certainly
annihilates all elements of \(S\).  Hence
\[
  V((S))\subseteq V(S).
\]

Conversely, let \(a\in V(S)\).  Every \(g\in(S)\) has the form
\[
  g=h_1f_1+\cdots+h_rf_r
\]
for finitely many \(f_i\in S\) and \(h_i\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\).  Evaluating at \(a\),
\[
  g(a)
  =
  h_1(a)f_1(a)+\cdots+h_r(a)f_r(a)
  =0.
\]
Therefore \(a\in V((S))\).
\end{proof}

This proposition is the first indication that ideals, rather than isolated equations, are the
natural algebraic objects of affine algebraic geometry.

\section{Algebraic sets}
\label{sec:vi01-algebraic-sets}

\begin{definition}[Algebraic set]
A subset \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) is called an \emph{algebraic set} if there exists a
subset \(S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) such that
\[
  X=V(S).
\]
Equivalently, by \cref{prop:vi01-generated-ideal}, there is an ideal
\(I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) with
\[
  X=V(I).
\]
\end{definition}

The term \emph{affine algebraic set} is also common.  Later chapters will distinguish
irreducible algebraic sets, affine varieties, affine schemes, and more general schemes.  At
this stage, no irreducibility or smoothness assumption is imposed.

\begin{example}[A line]
For \(a,b,c\in k\), not both \(a,b\) zero,
\[
  V(ax+by-c)\subseteq \mathbb A_k^2
\]
is an affine line.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A parabola]
In \(\mathbb A_k^2\),
\[
  V(y-x^2)
\]
is the parabola \(y=x^2\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A reducible-looking equation]
The equation
\[
  xy=0
\]
defines
\[
  V(xy)=V(x)\cup V(y),
\]
the union of the two coordinate axes.  The algebraic reason for this union formula is proved
in \cref{prop:vi01-union}.
\end{example}

\section{Set-theoretic identities}
\label{sec:vi01-identities}

The operation \(V\) reverses containment: more equations mean fewer solutions.

\begin{proposition}[Order reversal]
\label{prop:vi01-order-reversal-V}
If \(I\subseteq J\) are ideals of \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), then
\[
  V(J)\subseteq V(I).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A point satisfying every equation in \(J\) satisfies, in particular, every equation in
\(I\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Extreme cases]
\label{prop:vi01-extremes}
In \(\mathbb A_k^n\),
\[
  V((0))=\mathbb A_k^n,
  \qquad
  V((1))=\varnothing.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The zero polynomial vanishes everywhere, while the constant polynomial \(1\) vanishes
nowhere.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Arbitrary intersections]
\label{prop:vi01-intersections}
For any family of ideals \(\{I_\lambda\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\),
\[
  \bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda} V(I_\lambda)
  =
  V\!\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda} I_\lambda\right).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A point lies in every \(V(I_\lambda)\) precisely when every polynomial belonging to every
\(I_\lambda\) vanishes there.  This is equivalent to the vanishing of every finite sum of
such polynomials, hence to vanishing of the ideal \(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Finite unions]
\label{prop:vi01-union}
For ideals \(I,J\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
  V(I)\cup V(J)
  =
  V(IJ)
  =
  V(I\cap J).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Because \(IJ\subseteq I\cap J\), order reversal gives
\[
  V(I\cap J)\subseteq V(IJ).
\]
Also \(I\cap J\subseteq I\) and \(I\cap J\subseteq J\), so
\[
  V(I)\cup V(J)\subseteq V(I\cap J).
\]
It remains to prove \(V(IJ)\subseteq V(I)\cup V(J)\).

Take \(a\notin V(I)\cup V(J)\).  Then there exist \(f\in I\) and \(g\in J\) with
\(f(a)\neq0\) and \(g(a)\neq0\).  Since \(fg\in IJ\),
\[
  (fg)(a)=f(a)g(a)\neq0.
\]
Thus \(a\notin V(IJ)\).  Taking contrapositives yields
\[
  V(IJ)\subseteq V(I)\cup V(J).
\]
\end{proof}

These identities will become the axioms of the Zariski topology in the next chapter:
arbitrary intersections of algebraic sets are algebraic, and finite unions of algebraic sets
are algebraic.

\section{The ideal of a subset}
\label{sec:vi01-vanishing-ideal}

We can reverse the construction.  Instead of starting with equations and asking for their
solutions, start with points and ask for all polynomial equations that vanish on them.

\begin{definition}[Vanishing ideal]
For a subset \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), define
\[
  I(X)
  :=
  \left\{
    f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]:
    f(a)=0\text{ for every }a\in X
  \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vi01-IX-ideal}
For every \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), the set \(I(X)\) is an ideal of
\(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The zero polynomial vanishes on \(X\).  If \(f,g\in I(X)\), then
\[
  (f+g)(a)=f(a)+g(a)=0
\]
for every \(a\in X\), so \(f+g\in I(X)\).  If \(h\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) and
\(f\in I(X)\), then
\[
  (hf)(a)=h(a)f(a)=0
\]
for every \(a\in X\), hence \(hf\in I(X)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Order reversal for vanishing ideals]
\label{prop:vi01-order-reversal-I}
If \(X\subseteq Y\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), then
\[
  I(Y)\subseteq I(X).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A polynomial vanishing on all of \(Y\) certainly vanishes on the smaller set \(X\).
\end{proof}

\section{The \(V\)-\(I\) correspondence}
\label{sec:vi01-VI-correspondence}

The two constructions
\[
  I\longmapsto V(I),
  \qquad
  X\longmapsto I(X)
\]
are both order reversing.

\begin{proposition}[Closure inequalities]
\label{prop:vi01-closure-inequalities}
For every ideal \(J\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) and every subset
\(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\),
\[
  J\subseteq I(V(J))
\]
and
\[
  X\subseteq V(I(X)).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every \(f\in J\) vanishes by definition at every point of \(V(J)\), so
\(f\in I(V(J))\).  This proves the first inclusion.

For the second, let \(a\in X\).  Every polynomial in \(I(X)\) vanishes at every point of
\(X\), hence in particular at \(a\).  Therefore \(a\in V(I(X))\).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Smallest algebraic set containing a subset]
\label{prop:vi01-smallest-algebraic}
For every \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\), the set
\[
  V(I(X))
\]
is the smallest algebraic set containing \(X\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:vi01-closure-inequalities}, it contains \(X\).  Suppose
\(X\subseteq V(J)\) for some ideal \(J\).  Since every element of \(J\) vanishes on \(X\),
\[
  J\subseteq I(X).
\]
Order reversal for \(V\) then gives
\[
  V(I(X))\subseteq V(J).
\]
\end{proof}

This construction anticipates Zariski closure.  In Chapter VI/02, once algebraic sets
are declared closed, the closure of \(X\) will be exactly \(V(I(X))\).

\section{Radicals appear naturally}
\label{sec:vi01-radicals}

Different ideals can define exactly the same algebraic set.

\begin{example}
In \(\mathbb A_k^1\),
\[
  V(x)=V(x^2)=V(x^m)
\]
for every integer \(m\ge1\).
\end{example}

The reason is that taking powers does not change whether a value is zero.

\begin{definition}[Radical of an ideal]
For an ideal \(I\) in a commutative ring,
\[
  \sqrt I
  :=
  \{f: f^m\in I\text{ for some }m\ge1\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vi01-radical-same-zero-set}
For every ideal \(I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
  V(I)=V(\sqrt I).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since \(I\subseteq\sqrt I\), we have
\[
  V(\sqrt I)\subseteq V(I).
\]
Conversely, take \(a\in V(I)\) and \(f\in\sqrt I\).  Then \(f^m\in I\) for some
\(m\ge1\), hence
\[
  0=(f^m)(a)=f(a)^m.
\]
Because \(k\) is a field, \(f(a)=0\).  Thus every \(f\in\sqrt I\) vanishes at \(a\), so
\(a\in V(\sqrt I)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:vi01-radical-contained}
For every ideal \(I\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\),
\[
  \sqrt I\subseteq I(V(I)).
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If \(f\in\sqrt I\), then \(f\) vanishes on \(V(I)=V(\sqrt I)\).
\end{proof}

Over an algebraically closed field, Hilbert's Nullstellensatz gives the deep converse
\[
  I(V(I))=\sqrt I.
\]
We do not use that theorem here.  Its role is to explain precisely which ideals encode the
same affine algebraic set.

\section{Finitely many equations}
\label{sec:vi01-finite-equations}

The definition of \(V(S)\) allows an arbitrary family \(S\) of polynomial equations.  In
finite-dimensional affine space over a field, this apparent infinitude is unnecessary.

\begin{proposition}[Finite description, using Hilbert's basis theorem]
\label{prop:vi01-finite-description}
Every algebraic subset of \(\mathbb A_k^n\) is the common zero locus of finitely many
polynomials.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(X=V(S)\).  By \cref{prop:vi01-generated-ideal},
\[
  X=V((S)).
\]
Hilbert's basis theorem says that \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is Noetherian, so the ideal \((S)\)
is finitely generated:
\[
  (S)=(f_1,\dots,f_r).
\]
Therefore
\[
  X=V(f_1,\dots,f_r).
\]
\end{proof}

The Noetherian theorem belongs naturally to the commutative-algebra volume; here we record
its geometric consequence.

\section{Dependence on the ground field}
\label{sec:vi01-field-dependence}

The notation \(V(f)\) is incomplete unless the field is understood.

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
  f(x)=x^2+1.
\]
Then
\[
  V_{\mathbb R}(f)=\varnothing,
\]
while
\[
  V_{\mathbb C}(f)=\{i,-i\}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
In two variables,
\[
  V_{\mathbb R}(x^2+y^2)=\{(0,0)\},
\]
but over \(\mathbb C\),
\[
  x^2+y^2=(x+iy)(x-iy),
\]
so the zero locus is the union of two complex lines.
\end{example}

Finite fields produce another phenomenon.  Over \(\mathbb F_p\), the nonzero polynomial
\[
  x^p-x
\]
vanishes at every point of \(\mathbb A_{\mathbb F_p}^1\).  Thus distinct polynomials may
determine the same polynomial function on a finite field.

These examples are not pathologies.  They show that the chosen base field is part of the
geometric data.

\section{A reusable dictionary}
\label{sec:vi01-dictionary}

The basic algebra--geometry dictionary developed so far is:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Algebra} & \text{Geometry}\\
\hline
S\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n] & V(S)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\\
\text{ideal generated by }S & \text{same zero locus as }S\\
I\subseteq J & V(J)\subseteq V(I)\\
I+J & V(I)\cap V(J)\\
IJ\text{ or }I\cap J & V(I)\cup V(J)\\
\sqrt I & \text{same zero locus as }I\\
X\subseteq Y & I(Y)\subseteq I(X)\\
I(X) & \text{all equations true on }X\\
V(I(X)) & \text{smallest algebraic set containing }X
\end{array}
\]

The reversal of arrows is fundamental: adding algebraic constraints removes geometric
points, while adding geometric points removes polynomial equations.

\section{Common pitfalls}
\label{sec:vi01-pitfalls}

\begin{warning}[One equation is not one component]
The zero locus of one polynomial can be a union of several pieces.  The example
\(V(xy)=V(x)\cup V(y)\) is the simplest case.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Different ideals may define the same set]
The equality \(V(x)=V(x^2)\) shows that geometric zero sets forget multiplicity and
nilpotent information.  Schemes will later restore precisely this information.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[The field matters]
A polynomial may have no real zeros and many complex zeros.  Always keep the base field
visible when changing fields.
\end{warning}

\begin{warning}[Do not confuse \(V(I)\) with \(\Spec\)]
This chapter studies solutions in \(k^n\).  Prime spectra introduce a different space whose
points are prime ideals.  That transition is postponed to Chapters VI/06--VI/08.
\end{warning}

\section{Chapter summary}
\label{sec:vi01-summary}

An affine algebraic set is a common zero locus
\[
  V(I)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n
\]
of polynomial equations.  The ideal generated by a family of equations does not change its
zero locus.  Arbitrary intersections and finite unions of algebraic sets remain algebraic:
\[
  \bigcap_\lambda V(I_\lambda)
  =
  V\!\left(\sum_\lambda I_\lambda\right),
  \qquad
  V(I)\cup V(J)=V(IJ).
\]
Every subset \(X\) has a vanishing ideal \(I(X)\), and
\[
  V(I(X))
\]
is the smallest algebraic set containing \(X\).  The operations \(V\) and \(I\) both reverse
inclusions, and radicals do not alter zero loci:
\[
  V(I)=V(\sqrt I).
\]

These facts supply exactly the closure properties needed for the next chapter, where
algebraic sets become the closed sets of the Zariski topology.
