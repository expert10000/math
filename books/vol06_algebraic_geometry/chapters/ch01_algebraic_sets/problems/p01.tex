% Problem dossier: VI.01.P1
\subsection*{Problem VI.01.P1 --- The three coordinate axes and their vanishing ideal}
\label{prob:vi01-p1}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Assume \(k\) is infinite and let
\[
X=V(xy,xz,yz)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^3.
\]
Prove first that \(X\) is exactly the union of the three coordinate axes.  Then prove the
stronger statement
\[
I(X)=(xy,xz,yz).
\]

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
Describing a zero set is usually easier than recovering all equations that vanish on it.
This problem makes the passage \(X\mapsto I(X)\) concrete and shows how monomials record
which coordinate directions occur together.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
Use the field property to analyze the equations pointwise.  For the vanishing ideal, expand
a polynomial into monomials and restrict it separately to the three axes.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
\textbf{Hint 1.} From \(xy=xz=yz=0\), show that at most one coordinate can be nonzero.

\textbf{Hint 2.} If \(f\in I(X)\), then \(f(t,0,0)=0\) for every \(t\in k\).  What does
this say about the constant term and all pure powers of \(x\)?  Repeat for \(y\) and \(z\).

\textbf{Hint 3.} Every remaining monomial involves at least two distinct variables.

\paragraph{Solution.}
If \((a,b,c)\in X\), then
\[
ab=ac=bc=0.
\]
If two of \(a,b,c\) were nonzero, their product would be nonzero because \(k\) is a field,
a contradiction.  Thus at most one coordinate is nonzero, so \(X\) is the union of the
three coordinate axes.  Conversely, every point on any coordinate axis clearly annihilates
\(xy,xz,yz\).

Since the generators vanish on \(X\),
\[
(xy,xz,yz)\subseteq I(X).
\]
For the reverse inclusion, let
\[
f=\sum_{a,b,c\ge0}\lambda_{abc}x^ay^bz^c\in I(X).
\]
Restricting to the \(x\)-axis gives
\[
f(t,0,0)=\sum_{a\ge0}\lambda_{a00}t^a=0
\]
for every \(t\in k\).  Because \(k\) is infinite, this one-variable polynomial is zero, so
all coefficients \(\lambda_{a00}\) vanish.  Restriction to the \(y\)- and \(z\)-axes
similarly kills every pure \(y\)-term and pure \(z\)-term.  Hence every monomial occurring
in \(f\) involves at least two distinct variables.  Such a monomial is divisible by one of
\(xy,xz,yz\).  Therefore
\[
f\in(xy,xz,yz),
\]
which proves the equality.

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
The equations \(xy,xz,yz\) say that two coordinates are never simultaneously active.  The
infinite-field hypothesis then lets restriction to each axis detect and eliminate every
monomial supported on only one variable.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Generalize the result to the union of the \(n\) coordinate axes in \(\mathbb A_k^n\).
\item Find equations for the union of all coordinate \(r\)-planes.
\item Investigate what changes over a finite field.
\end{enumerate}
