% Problem dossier: VI.01.P3
\subsection*{Problem VI.01.P3 --- The two closure machines}
\label{prob:vi01-p3}

\paragraph{Problem.}
For subsets \(X\subseteq\mathbb A_k^n\) and ideals \(J\subseteq k[x_1,\dots,x_n]\), define
\[
C(X):=V(I(X)),
\qquad
R(J):=I(V(J)).
\]
Prove that both \(C\) and \(R\) are extensive, monotone, and idempotent.  Prove also that
\[
X=C(X)
\]
if and only if \(X\) is algebraic, while
\[
J=R(J)
\]
if and only if \(J\) is the vanishing ideal of some subset of affine space.

\ifdefined\FullProblemDossiers
\paragraph{Why this problem matters.}
This packages the chapter into a genuine order-theoretic structure.  The two antitone maps
\(V\) and \(I\) form a Galois connection; composing them produces closure operations on
both sides.

\paragraph{Useful ideas before starting.}
Start from the equivalence
\[
X\subseteq V(J)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J\subseteq I(X).
\]
Use it repeatedly instead of chasing points and polynomials from scratch.

\paragraph{Guided hints.}
\textbf{Hint 1.} Extensivity is exactly the pair of closure inequalities already proved.

\textbf{Hint 2.} For idempotence of \(C\), prove \(I(C(X))=I(X)\).  There is a symmetric
argument for \(R\).

\textbf{Hint 3.} If \(X\) is algebraic, write \(X=V(J)\) and compare it with \(C(X)\).

\paragraph{Solution.}
The basic inclusions give
\[
X\subseteq C(X),
\qquad
J\subseteq R(J).
\]
If \(X\subseteq Y\), then \(I(Y)\subseteq I(X)\), hence
\[
C(X)=V(I(X))\subseteq V(I(Y))=C(Y).
\]
Thus \(C\) is monotone.  If \(I\subseteq J\), then \(V(J)\subseteq V(I)\), so
\[
R(I)=I(V(I))\subseteq I(V(J))=R(J).
\]
Thus \(R\) is monotone.

For idempotence, \(X\subseteq C(X)\) gives
\[
I(C(X))\subseteq I(X).
\]
But applying the ideal closure inequality to \(I(X)\) gives
\[
I(X)\subseteq I(V(I(X)))=I(C(X)).
\]
Therefore \(I(C(X))=I(X)\), and
\[
C(C(X))=V(I(C(X)))=V(I(X))=C(X).
\]
The ideal side is analogous: from \(J\subseteq R(J)\) we get
\(V(R(J))\subseteq V(J)\), while every element of \(R(J)\) already vanishes on \(V(J)\),
so in fact \(V(R(J))=V(J)\).  Applying \(I\) gives
\[
R(R(J))=R(J).
\]

If \(X\) is algebraic, say \(X=V(J)\), then \(X\subseteq C(X)\) and the smallest-algebraic-
set property gives \(C(X)\subseteq V(J)=X\), hence equality.  Conversely \(C(X)\) is always
algebraic, so \(X=C(X)\) implies \(X\) is algebraic.

Finally, if \(J=I(Y)\) for some \(Y\), then
\[
R(J)=I(V(I(Y)))=I(Y)=J.
\]
Conversely, if \(J=R(J)\), then \(J=I(V(J))\) is visibly a vanishing ideal.

\paragraph{What happened mathematically.}
Although \(V\) and \(I\) reverse order, their composites restore order and close data until
it becomes geometrically/algebraically stable.  Over an algebraically closed field,
Nullstellensatz later identifies \(R(J)\) with \(\sqrt J\).

\paragraph{Extensions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Prove directly the Galois equivalence
\(X\subseteq V(J)\iff J\subseteq I(X)\).
\item Show that algebraic sets ordered by inclusion are anti-isomorphic to vanishing ideals
ordered by inclusion.
\item Over an algebraically closed field, combine this with Nullstellensatz to recover the
usual anti-equivalence with radical ideals.
\end{enumerate}
