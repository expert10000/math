\subsection*{Challenge 1: Existence from gluing}
\begin{solution}
Let $f:X\to S$ and $g:Y\to S$.  We construct the product from affine pieces and then prove its
universal property.

Choose an affine open cover $S=\bigcup_iU_i$, $U_i=\Spec A_i$.  Cover the inverse images by
affines
\[
f^{-1}(U_i)=\bigcup_jV_{ij},\qquad V_{ij}=\Spec B_{ij},
\]
\[
g^{-1}(U_i)=\bigcup_kW_{ik},\qquad W_{ik}=\Spec C_{ik}.
\]
For each triple define
\[
P_{ijk}:=V_{ij}\times_{U_i}W_{ik}
=\Spec(B_{ij}\otimes_{A_i}C_{ik}).
\]

We must identify overlaps.  Suppose two pieces meet.  By refining the intersection of the two base
charts and the intersections of the two factor charts, VI/20 lets us cover the common region by
opens that are principal in every affine chart involved.  On one such common principal refinement,
localization gives
\[
(B\otimes_AC)_h
\cong
B_h\otimes_{A_h}C_h.
\]
Thus the two restrictions are affine fiber products of the same restricted diagrams.  The
universal property gives a unique projection-compatible isomorphism between them.

On a triple overlap, there are two possible composites of these transition maps.  Both are maps
between the same local fiber products and both commute with the two projections.  Uniqueness of
fiber products therefore forces the composites to be equal.  Hence the transition maps satisfy
the cocycle condition.  By the gluing theorem of VI/20 the $P_{ijk}$ glue to a scheme $P$.

The local projections $P_{ijk}\to V_{ij}\to X$ agree on overlaps, so they glue to
$p_X:P\to X$; similarly we obtain $p_Y:P\to Y$.  Their composites to $S$ agree locally and hence
globally.

Now let $T$ be a scheme with a compatible pair $a:T\to X$, $b:T\to Y$.  Cover $T$ by open sets
whose images lie in some $V_{ij}$ and $W_{ik}$ over a common $U_i$.  On each such open the affine
fiber-product theorem gives a unique map to $P_{ijk}$.  On overlaps, the local maps have the same
two projections, so uniqueness makes them agree.  They glue to a global $u:T\to P$.  Any second
such $u$ agrees with the first on this open cover and therefore globally.  Hence $P$ satisfies the
fiber-product universal property.

This proves existence of fiber products of arbitrary schemes entirely from affine tensor products,
localization compatibility, uniqueness, and VI/20 gluing.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge 2: Residue-field point classification}
\begin{solution}
Let $z\in X\times_SY$ map to $x\in X$ and $y\in Y$, with common image $s\in S$.  The scheme
morphisms induce field homomorphisms
\[
\kappa(s)\longrightarrow\kappa(x),
\qquad
\kappa(s)\longrightarrow\kappa(y).
\]
They are part of the data; one should not silently identify these fields with literal subfields of
a common ambient field.

Fix the compatible pair $(x,y)$.  Consider the canonical maps
\[
\Spec\kappa(x)\to X,\qquad \Spec\kappa(y)\to Y.
\]
Their product over $\Spec\kappa(s)$ is
\[
\Spec\kappa(x)\times_{\Spec\kappa(s)}\Spec\kappa(y)
\cong
\Spec\!\left(\kappa(x)\otimes_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(y)\right).
\]
A point of $X\times_SY$ lying over $(x,y)$ determines, and is determined by, a prime of this
tensor product.  Concretely, a prime
\[
\mathfrak r\subseteq\kappa(x)\otimes_{\kappa(s)}\kappa(y)
\]
gives a field $\kappa(\mathfrak r)$ and compatible field maps from $\kappa(x)$ and $\kappa(y)$;
these compose with the canonical point morphisms to produce the corresponding product point.

Thus the fiber of the map
\[
|X\times_SY|\to |X|\times_{|S|}|Y|
\]
over $(x,y)$ is naturally the point set of the displayed spectrum.  It may have several points.
For example
\[
\mathbb C\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C\cong\mathbb C\times\mathbb C,
\]
so the single compatible pair of points of two copies of $\Spec\mathbb C$ over
$\Spec\mathbb R$ lifts to two product points.

The phrase ``the maps are not embeddings in a naive sense'' is best interpreted as follows.
A unital homomorphism between fields is injective, but the source and target are abstract residue
fields and there is no canonical common ambient field in which both are already sitting.  The
tensor product must be formed using the actual homomorphisms induced by the scheme maps.  Replacing
them by an informal common-subfield picture can erase automorphisms and splitting behavior.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge 3: Diagonal and equalizers}
\begin{solution}
Let $f,g:T\rightrightarrows X$ be morphisms over $S$.  Form
\[
E:=T\times_{X\times_SX}X,
\]
where $T\to X\times_SX$ is $(f,g)$ and $X\to X\times_SX$ is the diagonal.  For every test
scheme $Q$,
\[
\Hom(Q,E)
=
\{h:Q\to T: f h=g h\}.
\]
Indeed, a map to the pullback is a pair $(h,q)$ with
$(fh,gh)=(q,q)$, and then $q$ is uniquely forced.  Thus $E$ is the scheme-theoretic equalizer.

Now suppose $S=\Spec R$, $X=\Spec A$, and $T=\Spec B$.  Let the two maps correspond to
$R$-algebra maps $\alpha,\beta:A\to B$.  Then
\[
X\times_SX=\Spec(A\otimes_RA),
\]
the diagonal is induced by multiplication $\mu:A\otimes_RA\to A$, and $(f,g)$ is induced by
\[
\gamma:A\otimes_RA\to B,\qquad
a\otimes a'\mapsto\alpha(a)\beta(a').
\]
Therefore
\[
E=\Spec\!\left(B\otimes_{A\otimes_RA}A\right).
\]
This ring is canonically
\[
B/J,\qquad
J=(\alpha(a)-\beta(a):a\in A).
\]
To see this, in the tensor product the relation coming from
$a\otimes1-1\otimes a$ becomes $\alpha(a)-\beta(a)=0$, and these relations generate exactly the
kernel needed to force the two maps to coincide.

Hence the scheme-theoretic equalizer is cut out by the differences of the two pullbacks of all
functions.  Its point set is $V(J)$.  This can be smaller than the naive set-theoretic equalizer
of the underlying continuous maps, because two scheme morphisms may send a point to the same
prime while inducing different maps on residue fields or local functions.  The scheme equalizer
remembers the full morphism, not only its point map.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge 4: Tensor products and geometric surprises}
\begin{solution}
We give three examples in which both input schemes are reduced.

\emph{(a) Reducible.}
Take
\[
X=Y=\Spec\mathbb C,\qquad S=\Spec\mathbb R.
\]
All three schemes are reduced and $X,Y$ are irreducible.  Nevertheless
\[
\mathbb C\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C\cong\mathbb C\times\mathbb C,
\]
so
\[
X\times_SY\cong\Spec(\mathbb C\times\mathbb C)
\]
is the disjoint union of two points and is reducible.  The algebraic mechanism is splitting of a
separable field extension after tensoring with itself.

\emph{(b) Nonreduced.}
Let $k=\mathbb F_p(t)$ and let
\[
K=k(u),\qquad u^p=t.
\]
Since $t$ is not a $p$th power in $k$, $K$ is a field and hence reduced.  But
\[
K\otimes_kK
\cong
K[v]/(v^p-t)
=
K[v]/(v^p-u^p)
=
K[v]/((v-u)^p),
\]
which contains the nonzero nilpotent class of $v-u$.  Thus
$\Spec K\times_{\Spec k}\Spec K$ is nonreduced.  The mechanism is purely inseparable
ramification: after extending scalars, a polynomial becomes a repeated $p$th power.

\emph{(c) Empty.}
Let $A=k\times k$, and let $B=k$ and $C=k$ be the two quotient $A$-algebras given by the first
and second projections.  All rings are reduced and $B,C$ are nonzero.  If $e=(1,0)\in A$, then
$e$ acts as $1$ on $B$ and as $0$ on $C$, so
\[
b\otimes c=be\otimes c=b\otimes ec=0
\]
for every pure tensor.  Therefore $B\otimes_AC=0$ and the fiber product is empty.  The mechanism
is incompatible support over different connected components of the base.

These examples show that reducedness and irreducibility are not automatically preserved by an
arbitrary tensor product.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge 5: Functorial calculus without coordinates}
\begin{solution}
All five identities follow from the same principle: describe maps from an arbitrary test scheme
and let uniqueness force the canonical morphism.

\emph{Symmetry.}
A $T$-point of $X\times_SY$ is a compatible pair $(a,b)$.  Swapping the pair gives a $T$-point of
$Y\times_SX$.  The swap is natural in $T$, hence represented by a canonical isomorphism, and
swapping twice is the identity.

\emph{Associativity.}
Either bracketing
\[
(X\times_SY)\times_SZ,\qquad X\times_S(Y\times_SZ)
\]
represents triples $(a,b,c)$ with one common composite to $S$.  Therefore the bracketings are
canonically isomorphic.

\emph{Identity.}
A $T$-point of $X\times_SS$ is $(a,b)$ with $b=fa$, so it is exactly a map $a:T\to X$.
Hence $X\times_SS\cong X$.

\emph{Graph.}
A map $T\to X\times_SY$ factors through $\Gamma_f$ precisely when its two components are
$(a,fa)$.  Equivalently, the graph is the pullback of $\Delta_{Y/S}$ along the map
$X\times_SY\to Y\times_SY$ with components $(fp_X,p_Y)$.

\emph{Diagonal-change square.}
A $T$-point of $X\times_TY$ is a pair whose maps to the coarser base $T$ agree.  Pulling back along
$S\to S\times_TS$ forces the two induced maps to $S$ to be equal.  The resulting datum is exactly
a $T$-point of $X\times_SY$.

These arguments are more robust than coordinates because they are invariant under arbitrary
isomorphisms and affine refinements, work for nonaffine schemes without modification, and
automatically produce coherence identities.  In coordinates one must separately prove that
different local formulas agree and that choices of charts or parenthesizations do not matter;
the universal property makes those equalities consequences of uniqueness.
\end{solution}
