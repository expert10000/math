\label{ch:vi41-divisor-class-groups}

\providecommand{\CaDiv}{\operatorname{CaDiv}}
\providecommand{\Pic}{\operatorname{Pic}}

VI/39 attached an integer multiplicity to every codimension-one prime and constructed Weil divisors. VI/40 developed Cartier divisors from local rational equations and proved that, on a normal Noetherian integral scheme, every Cartier divisor determines a Weil divisor. The present chapter takes the decisive quotient: divisors differing by the divisor of one rational function represent the same class.

The resulting group
\[
  \Cl(X)=\Div(X)/\Prin(X)
\]
measures a global obstruction. On an affine normal Noetherian domain it detects the failure of unique factorization; on an open subset it records exactly which codimension-one classes survive localization; and on projective space it recovers the integer degree generated by a hyperplane. The quadric cone
\[
  X=\Spec k[x,y,z]/(xy-z^2)
\]
is the running example. Its prime divisor \(L=V(x,z)\) satisfies \(2L=\operatorname{div}(x)\) but is not principal, and the full computation gives
\[
  \Cl(X)\cong \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
\]
This single example distinguishes normality from factoriality, shows how torsion enters the class group, and explains why the singular vertex matters even though the punctured cone is smooth.

The migration ledger assigns three canonical rules to VI/41: the AG9 class-group/divisor-class section and its inherited theorem-like and exercise/problem descendants. The accessible repository does not expose the raw AG9 prose as a canonical chapter source, so the ledger is used as provenance and scope evidence rather than as a basis for claiming verbatim recovery. The full Picard-group equivalence is deliberately deferred to VI/42.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item define linear equivalence of Weil divisors and construct \(\Cl(X)\);
\item interpret \(\Cl(A)\) in terms of height-one primes of a normal Noetherian domain;
\item prove that a normal Noetherian domain is a UFD exactly when its class group vanishes;
\item distinguish factoriality from local factoriality and explain the role of Cartier divisors;
\item prove the localization exact sequence for divisor class groups;
\item compute \(\Cl(A_f)\) by killing the classes of height-one primes containing \(f\);
\item compute the divisor class group of the quadric cone as \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\);
\item prove rigorously that the cone divisor \(L=V(x,z)\) is not principal;
\item explain why deleting a codimension-at-least-two subset does not change the Weil divisor class group of a normal scheme;
\item compute \(\Cl(\mathbb A^n)=0\) and \(\Cl(\mathbb P^n)\cong\mathbb Z\);
\item recognize torsion divisor classes and the first appearance of \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier phenomena;
\item identify the precise bridge from divisor classes to the Picard group developed in VI/42.
\end{itemize}

\section{Linear equivalence and principal divisors}

Let \(X\) be a normal Noetherian integral scheme with function field \(K(X)\). By VI/39,
\[
  \Div(X)=\bigoplus_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\mathbb Z[Z],
\]
where \(X^{(1)}\) denotes the codimension-one points, equivalently the prime divisors.

Every \(f\in K(X)^\times\) determines the principal Weil divisor
\[
  \operatorname{div}(f)
  =
  \sum_{Z\in X^{(1)}}\operatorname{ord}_Z(f)[Z].
\]
The valuation identity
\[
  \operatorname{div}(fg)
  =
  \operatorname{div}(f)+\operatorname{div}(g)
\]
shows that principal divisors form a subgroup.

\begin{definition}[Principal divisor subgroup]\label{def:vi41-principal-subgroup}
Define
\[
  \Prin(X)
  :=
  \{\operatorname{div}(f):f\in K(X)^\times\}
  \subseteq
  \Div(X).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Linear equivalence]\label{def:vi41-linear-equivalence}
Two Weil divisors \(D,E\in\Div(X)\) are \emph{linearly equivalent}, written
\[
  D\sim E,
\]
if
\[
  D-E=\operatorname{div}(f)
\]
for some \(f\in K(X)^\times\).
\end{definition}

Thus \(D\sim0\) exactly when \(D\) is principal. Linear equivalence is an equivalence relation compatible with addition.

\begin{example}[The affine line]\label{ex:vi41-a1-points}
On \(\AA^1_k=\Spec k[t]\), if \(a,b\in k\), then
\[
  \operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{t-a}{t-b}\right)=[a]-[b].
\]
Hence every two \(k\)-rational closed points are linearly equivalent. In fact every Weil divisor on \(\AA^1_k\) is principal because \(k[t]\) is a PID.
\end{example}

\section{The divisor class group}

\begin{definition}[Divisor class group]\label{def:vi41-class-group}
The \emph{divisor class group} of \(X\) is
\[
  \boxed{\Cl(X):=\Div(X)/\Prin(X).}
\]
The class of a divisor \(D\) is denoted \([D]\).
\end{definition}

Equivalently,
\[
 [D]=[E]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\sim E,
\]
and
\[
 [D]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\ \text{is principal}.
\]

The definition packages the exact sequence
\[
 K(X)^\times
 \xrightarrow{\operatorname{div}}
 \Div(X)
 \longrightarrow
 \Cl(X)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
The kernel of the divisor map consists of rational functions having no zeros or poles in codimension one. On many familiar complete varieties this kernel is just the constant functions; on affine schemes it may be larger because nonconstant units can occur.

\input{chapters/ch41_divisor_class_groups/figures/figure_02}

\begin{remark}[What the class group forgets]
The group \(\Div(X)\) remembers every codimension-one prime separately. Passing to \(\Cl(X)\) imposes exactly the relations produced by rational functions. It does not identify divisors merely because they have the same support, the same degree, or the same local multiplicities at selected points.
\end{remark}

\section{The affine description by height-one primes}

Let \(A\) be a normal Noetherian domain and \(X=\Spec A\). Prime divisors correspond to height-one prime ideals, so
\[
  \Div(A)
  =
  \bigoplus_{\height\mathfrak p=1}\mathbb Z[\mathfrak p].
\]
For \(f\in\Frac(A)^\times\),
\[
  \operatorname{div}_A(f)
  =
  \sum_{\height\mathfrak p=1}
  v_{\mathfrak p}(f)[\mathfrak p],
\]
where \(A_{\mathfrak p}\) is a DVR.

We write
\[
  \Cl(A):=\Cl(\Spec A).
\]

\begin{example}[A PID]\label{ex:vi41-pid}
If \(A\) is a PID, every height-one prime is generated by one prime element. Hence every prime divisor is principal and
\[
  \Cl(A)=0.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Polynomial rings]\label{ex:vi41-polynomial-ring}
The ring \(k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) is a UFD. Therefore
\[
  \Cl(\AA^n_k)=0.
\]
The connection between UFDs and the class group will now be proved in general.
\end{example}

\section{Factoriality and the class group}

\begin{theorem}[Class-group criterion for unique factorization]\label{thm:vi41-ufd-criterion}
Let \(A\) be a normal Noetherian domain. Then
\[
  \boxed{
  A\text{ is a UFD}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \Cl(A)=0.
  }
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose first that \(A\) is a UFD. Every height-one prime \(\mathfrak p\) is generated by a prime element \(p\). The principal divisor \(\operatorname{div}(p)\) has coefficient one at \(\mathfrak p\) and zero at every other height-one prime, so
\[
  \operatorname{div}(p)=[\mathfrak p].
\]
Since the height-one prime divisors freely generate \(\Div(A)\), every Weil divisor is principal and \(\Cl(A)=0\).

Conversely, assume \(\Cl(A)=0\). Then every height-one prime divisor \([\mathfrak p]\) is principal as a Weil divisor. Thus there exists \(f\in\Frac(A)^\times\) with
\[
  \operatorname{div}(f)=[\mathfrak p].
\]
The Krull-domain identity
\[
  A=\bigcap_{\height\mathfrak q=1}A_{\mathfrak q}
  \subseteq\Frac(A)
\]
shows that \(f\in A\): all height-one valuations of \(f\) are nonnegative. If \(a\in\mathfrak p\), then \(v_{\mathfrak p}(a)\ge1\), so \(a/f\) has nonnegative valuation at every height-one prime and hence lies in \(A\). Therefore \(\mathfrak p=(f)\).

A Noetherian domain is a UFD exactly when every height-one prime ideal is principal. Hence \(A\) is a UFD.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Affine factoriality]\label{cor:vi41-affine-factorial}
For a normal Noetherian affine scheme \(X=\Spec A\),
\[
 X\text{ factorial}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \Cl(X)=0.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{warning}[Normality is not optional]
The class group used here is the Weil divisor class group, whose clean valuation theory depends on normality. The slogan
\[
  \Cl(A)=0\Longleftrightarrow A\text{ is a UFD}
\]
should not be applied blindly to arbitrary nonnormal domains.
\end{warning}

\section{Factorial versus locally factorial}

\begin{definition}[Locally factorial]\label{def:vi41-locally-factorial}
A scheme \(X\) is \emph{locally factorial} if every local ring \(\OO_{X,x}\) is a UFD.
\end{definition}

If \(A\) is a UFD, every localization \(A_{\mathfrak p}\) is a UFD, so an affine factorial scheme is locally factorial. The converse fails: local principalness does not imply that a divisor is globally principal.

VI/40 proved that on a normal Noetherian integral scheme,
\[
  X\text{ locally factorial}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \text{every Weil divisor is Cartier}.
\]
Thus there are two different obstructions:
\[
\begin{array}{ccl}
\text{Weil divisor not Cartier}
&\Longleftrightarrow&
\text{failure of local factoriality},\\[3pt]
\text{Cartier divisor not principal}
&\Longleftrightarrow&
\text{global line-bundle obstruction}.
\end{array}
\]
The second obstruction will become the Picard group in VI/42.

\begin{example}[Smooth does not force global factoriality]\label{ex:vi41-smooth-not-global}
A smooth variety is locally factorial because regular local rings are UFDs. Nevertheless its global divisor class group can be nonzero. Projective space is the basic example:
\[
  \Cl(\PP^n_k)\cong\mathbb Z.
\]
Thus local factoriality and vanishing of the global class group are fundamentally different statements.
\end{example}

\section{Localization of divisor classes}

Let \(A\) be a normal Noetherian domain and \(S\subseteq A\) a multiplicative subset. The localization \(S^{-1}A\) is again normal. A height-one prime \(\mathfrak p\) survives as a height-one prime of \(S^{-1}A\) exactly when
\[
  \mathfrak p\cap S=\varnothing.
\]
If \(\mathfrak p\cap S\neq\varnothing\), then its divisor disappears after localization.

\begin{theorem}[Localization exact sequence]\label{thm:vi41-localization}
There is a natural exact sequence
\[
 \bigoplus_{\substack{\height\mathfrak p=1\\ \mathfrak p\cap S\neq\varnothing}}
 \mathbb Z[\mathfrak p]
 \longrightarrow
 \Cl(A)
 \longrightarrow
 \Cl(S^{-1}A)
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 \boxed{
 \Cl(S^{-1}A)
 \cong
 \Cl(A)\Big/
 \left\langle
 [\mathfrak p]:
 \height\mathfrak p=1,\ 
 \mathfrak p\cap S\neq\varnothing
 \right\rangle.
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every height-one prime of \(S^{-1}A\) is uniquely of the form \(S^{-1}\mathfrak p\) for a height-one prime \(\mathfrak p\) disjoint from \(S\). Thus restriction of divisors gives a surjection
\[
  \Div(A)\twoheadrightarrow\Div(S^{-1}A)
\]
whose kernel is the free subgroup generated by height-one primes meeting \(S\).

For \(f\in\Frac(A)^\times=\Frac(S^{-1}A)^\times\), the principal divisor in the localization is obtained by deleting from \(\operatorname{div}_A(f)\) the components meeting \(S\). Hence restriction sends principal divisors to principal divisors and induces a surjection on class groups. Its kernel is precisely the subgroup generated by the deleted prime-divisor classes.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch41_divisor_class_groups/figures/figure_04}

\begin{corollary}[Principal localization]\label{cor:vi41-principal-localization}
For \(f\in A\),
\[
 \Cl(A_f)
 \cong
 \Cl(A)\Big/
 \left\langle
 [\mathfrak p]:
 \height\mathfrak p=1,\ f\in\mathfrak p
 \right\rangle.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{example}[Laurent polynomial rings]\label{ex:vi41-laurent}
Since \(k[x]\) is a UFD,
\[
 \Cl(k[x,x^{-1}])=0.
\]
More generally, localization of a UFD is a UFD, exactly as the class-group localization theorem predicts.
\end{example}

\section{Open subsets and deleted divisors}

The geometric form of localization is equally important.

\begin{theorem}[Class group of an open subset]\label{thm:vi41-open-subset}
Let \(X\) be a normal Noetherian integral scheme and \(j:U\hookrightarrow X\) a nonempty open subset. If \(D_1,\ldots,D_r\) are the codimension-one irreducible components of \(X\setminus U\), then
\[
 \bigoplus_{i=1}^r\mathbb Z[D_i]
 \longrightarrow
 \Cl(X)
 \xrightarrow{j^*}
 \Cl(U)
 \longrightarrow0
\]
is exact.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Removing codimension at least two]\label{cor:vi41-codim-two-invariance}
If
\[
  \operatorname{codim}_X(X\setminus U)\ge2,
\]
then restriction induces an isomorphism
\[
  \boxed{\Cl(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Cl(U).}
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
There are no codimension-one components in the complement, so the leftmost direct sum in \cref{thm:vi41-open-subset} is zero.
\end{proof}

This codimension-two invariance is one of the characteristic features of Weil divisor theory: Weil divisors only see codimension one.

\section{The quadric cone: setup and normality}

Let
\[
  A=k[x,y,z]/(xy-z^2),
  \qquad
  X=\Spec A.
\]
The ring has dimension two. Its singular point is the vertex
\[
  o=V(x,y,z).
\]

\begin{proposition}[Normality of the quadric cone]\label{prop:vi41-cone-normal}
The ring \(A\) is a normal domain.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The polynomial \(xy-z^2\) is irreducible, so \(A\) is a domain. As a hypersurface, \(A\) is Cohen--Macaulay, hence satisfies Serre's condition \(S_2\). Away from the vertex one of \(x,y,z\) is nonzero, and the corresponding local chart is regular; thus the singular locus has codimension two. Hence \(A\) satisfies \(R_1\). Serre's criterion \(R_1+S_2\) gives normality.
\end{proof}

Define two height-one primes
\[
  \mathfrak p_L=(x,z),
  \qquad
  \mathfrak p_M=(y,z),
\]
with corresponding prime divisors
\[
  L=V(x,z),
  \qquad
  M=V(y,z).
\]

\input{chapters/ch41_divisor_class_groups/figures/figure_05}

\section{The three key divisor computations}

\begin{proposition}[Coordinate divisors on the cone]\label{prop:vi41-cone-coordinate-divisors}
On \(X\),
\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{div}(x)=2L,\qquad
 \operatorname{div}(y)=2M,\qquad
 \operatorname{div}(z)=L+M.
 }
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At the generic point of \(L\), the element \(y\) is a unit and
\[
 x=\frac{z^2}{y}.
\]
The DVR \(A_{\mathfrak p_L}\) has uniformizer \(z\), so
\[
 v_L(z)=1,\qquad v_L(x)=2.
\]
The zero locus of \(x\) has only the codimension-one component \(L\), hence
\[
 \operatorname{div}(x)=2L.
\]
By symmetry,
\[
 \operatorname{div}(y)=2M.
\]

Finally, \(z=0\) together with \(xy=z^2\) gives \(xy=0\), so the codimension-one zero locus of \(z\) is \(L\cup M\). At the generic point of either component, \(z\) is a uniformizer, giving multiplicity one. Thus
\[
 \operatorname{div}(z)=L+M.
\]
\end{proof}

Consequently,
\[
 2[L]=0,
 \qquad
 2[M]=0,
 \qquad
 [L]+[M]=0.
\]
Since \(2[L]=0\), we have \(-[L]=[L]\), and therefore
\[
 [M]=[L].
\]

The computation already shows that the class of \(L\), if nonzero, has order exactly two.

\section{Why the cone class group is generated by \(L\)}

Localize at \(x\). Since \(xy=z^2\),
\[
  y=\frac{z^2}{x}
\]
in \(A_x\), and therefore
\[
  A_x\cong k[x,x^{-1},z].
\]
This is a Laurent polynomial ring, hence a UFD, so
\[
  \Cl(A_x)=0.
\]

Which height-one primes disappear when \(x\) is inverted? The radical of \((x)\) is
\[
  \sqrt{(x)}=(x,z)=\mathfrak p_L,
\]
so \(L\) is the unique height-one prime containing \(x\).

Applying \cref{cor:vi41-principal-localization} gives
\[
  \Cl(A_x)
  \cong
  \Cl(A)/\langle[L]\rangle.
\]
Since the left side is zero,
\[
  \boxed{\Cl(A)=\langle[L]\rangle.}
\]

Thus it remains only to prove that \([L]\neq0\).

\section{Why \(L\) is not principal}

The relation
\[
  \operatorname{div}(x)=2L
\]
does not by itself exclude \(L\) from being principal. We now prove nontriviality.

\begin{theorem}[Nonprincipality of the ruling divisor]\label{thm:vi41-L-nonprincipal}
The prime divisor \(L=V(x,z)\) is not principal as a Weil divisor. Hence
\[
  [L]\neq0\in\Cl(A).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose
\[
  L=\operatorname{div}(f)
\]
for some \(f\in K(X)^\times\). Then
\[
 \operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{f^2}{x}\right)
 =
 2L-2L
 =
 0.
\]
For a normal affine domain, an element of the fraction field whose valuations at every height-one prime are all zero is a unit: both it and its inverse lie in
\[
 A=\bigcap_{\height\mathfrak p=1}A_{\mathfrak p}.
\]
The ring \(A\) is a connected positively graded \(k\)-domain, so
\[
 A^\times=k^\times.
\]
Therefore
\[
  f^2=cx
\]
for some \(c\in k^\times\).

But
\[
  K(X)=\Frac(A)=k(x,z),
\]
because \(y=z^2/x\). In the rational function field \(k(x,z)\), the discrete valuation associated with the irreducible polynomial \(x\) satisfies
\[
  v_x(cx)=1.
\]
Every square has even \(v_x\)-valuation. Hence \(cx\) cannot be a square in \(k(x,z)\), a contradiction.

Therefore \(L\) is not principal.
\end{proof}

This proof is stronger than the informal observation that the parametrizing square root of \(x\) is missing from the coordinate ring: it rules out every rational function, not merely every regular function, as a possible equation for \(L\).

\section{The class group of the quadric cone}

Combining generation, the relation \(2[L]=0\), and nontriviality gives the central computation.

\begin{theorem}[Class group of the quadric cone]\label{thm:vi41-cone-class-group}
For
\[
 A=k[x,y,z]/(xy-z^2),
\]
\[
 \boxed{
 \Cl(A)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,
 }
\]
with generator \([L]\), where \(L=V(x,z)\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Localization at \(x\) shows that \([L]\) generates \(\Cl(A)\). The principal divisor
\[
  \operatorname{div}(x)=2L
\]
gives \(2[L]=0\). By \cref{thm:vi41-L-nonprincipal}, \([L]\neq0\). Therefore \([L]\) has exact order two.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Normal but not factorial]\label{cor:vi41-cone-not-factorial}
The quadric cone is normal but not factorial.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Normality is \cref{prop:vi41-cone-normal}; nonfactoriality follows from
\[
  \Cl(A)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\neq0
\]
and \cref{thm:vi41-ufd-criterion}.
\end{proof}

\section{The singular vertex and the punctured cone}

Let
\[
 U=X\setminus\{o\}.
\]
Since \(X\) has dimension two, the removed vertex has codimension two. Hence
\[
  \Cl(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}\Cl(U)
\]
by \cref{cor:vi41-codim-two-invariance}. Therefore
\[
  \boxed{\Cl(U)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.}
\]

At the same time, \(U\) is smooth, so it is locally factorial. Thus every Weil divisor on \(U\) is Cartier, yet the class group is still nonzero. This is the cleanest warning against confusing local factoriality with global factoriality.

The two facts
\[
 \Cl(D(x))=0,
 \qquad
 \Cl(U)=\mathbb Z/2
\]
are not contradictory. The principal open \(D(x)\) deletes the codimension-one divisor \(L\), so its class is killed. The punctured cone deletes only the codimension-two vertex, so no Weil prime divisor is removed.

\begin{remark}[Preview of VI/42]
Because \(U\) is locally factorial, every Weil divisor class on \(U\) is represented by a Cartier divisor. VI/42 will identify this class with a nontrivial line bundle and obtain
\[
  \Pic(U)\cong\Cl(U)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
\]
The present chapter needs only the divisor-class side of that statement.
\end{remark}

\section{The local class group at the vertex}

Let
\[
  \mathfrak m=(x,y,z)
\]
be the vertex. Localizing at \(\mathfrak m\) gives the normal local domain \(A_{\mathfrak m}\).

\begin{proposition}[Local obstruction at the vertex]\label{prop:vi41-local-cone-class}
The class of \(\mathfrak p_LA_{\mathfrak m}\) is nonzero of order two in
\[
  \Cl(A_{\mathfrak m}).
\]
In fact
\[
  \Cl(A_{\mathfrak m})\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The localization map
\[
  \Cl(A)\twoheadrightarrow\Cl(A_{\mathfrak m})
\]
is surjective, so the local class group is a quotient of \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\). It is therefore enough to show that the image of \([L]\) is nonzero.

Set \(I=(x,z)A_{\mathfrak m}\). Modulo \(\mathfrak m I\), the classes of \(x\) and \(z\) are linearly independent over the residue field \(k\): every element of \(\mathfrak m I\) has order at least two in the standard grading, while a nontrivial \(k\)-linear combination \(ax+bz\) has degree one. Hence
\[
  \dim_k I/\mathfrak m I=2.
\]
By Nakayama's lemma, \(I\) needs two generators and is not principal. Thus \(L\) is not principal in the local ring, so its local class is nonzero. The only nonzero quotient of \(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\) is itself, giving
\[
  \Cl(A_{\mathfrak m})\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
\]
\end{proof}

Thus the failure of local factoriality is concentrated at the singular point.

\section{Projective space}

The affine space calculation
\[
  \Cl(\AA^n)=0
\]
should be contrasted with projective space.

\begin{theorem}[Class group of projective space]\label{thm:vi41-projective-space}
Let \(k\) be a field and \(n\ge1\). Then
\[
  \boxed{
  \Cl(\PP^n_k)\cong\mathbb Z,
  }
\]
generated by the class \(H\) of a hyperplane.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let
\[
  U=D_+(x_0)\cong\AA^n_k.
\]
Its class group is zero. The complement is the single hyperplane
\[
  H=V_+(x_0).
\]
The open-subset exact sequence gives a surjection
\[
  \mathbb Z[H]\twoheadrightarrow\Cl(\PP^n_k).
\]
It remains to show that no nonzero multiple of \(H\) is principal.

For a nonzero rational function
\[
  f=\frac{F}{G}
\]
with \(F,G\) homogeneous of the same degree after clearing common factors, the divisor of \(f\) has total degree zero: the zero divisor and pole divisor have the same homogeneous degree. Hence a principal divisor cannot equal \(mH\) unless \(m=0\). Therefore \([H]\) has infinite order and generates the class group.
\end{proof}

\input{chapters/ch41_divisor_class_groups/figures/figure_07}

\begin{corollary}[Hypersurface classes]\label{cor:vi41-hypersurface-class}
If \(F\) is a nonzero homogeneous polynomial of degree \(d\), then
\[
  [V_+(F)]=d[H]
\]
in \(\Cl(\PP^n_k)\), with factor multiplicities included.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Choose a linear form \(\ell\) not dividing \(F\). The rational function
\[
  \frac{F}{\ell^d}
\]
has principal divisor
\[
  \operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{F}{\ell^d}\right)
  =
  V_+(F)-dV_+(\ell).
\]
Since \(V_+(\ell)\sim H\), the claim follows.
\end{proof}

Thus degree is the class-group coordinate on projective space:
\[
  \deg:\Cl(\PP^n_k)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb Z.
\]

\section{Cartier classes inside the Weil class group}

On a normal Noetherian integral scheme, VI/40 constructed an injective homomorphism
\[
  \CaDiv(X)\hookrightarrow\Div(X)
\]
compatible with principal divisors. Passing to equivalence classes therefore gives a natural map from Cartier divisor classes into \(\Cl(X)\).

If \(X\) is locally factorial, every Weil divisor is Cartier, so every class in \(\Cl(X)\) has a Cartier representative. The remaining question is what quotient of Cartier divisors by principal Cartier divisors means intrinsically. VI/42 answers:
\[
  \{\text{Cartier divisors}\}/\{\text{principal Cartier divisors}\}
  \cong
  \Pic(X).
\]

Consequently, on a normal locally factorial integral scheme, VI/42 will give
\[
  \Pic(X)\cong\Cl(X).
\]
We record this here only as a bridge, not as a substitute for the line-bundle theory.

\section{Torsion classes and \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier divisors}

The cone generator satisfies
\[
  2[L]=0.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
  2L
\]
is principal and therefore Cartier even though \(L\) itself is not Cartier at the vertex.

\begin{definition}[\(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier divisor]\label{def:vi41-q-cartier}
A Weil divisor \(D\) on a normal integral scheme is \emph{\(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier} if there exists an integer \(m>0\) such that \(mD\) is Cartier.
\end{definition}

Thus the cone divisor \(L\) is \(\mathbb Q\)-Cartier of index two. This is the first appearance of a notion that becomes central in birational geometry: a singularity may fail to be locally factorial while still allowing rational multiples of divisors to behave like Cartier divisors.

\begin{warning}[Torsion in \(\Cl(X)\) versus \(\mathbb Q\)-factoriality]
A torsion class in the global class group means some multiple is \emph{principal}, which is stronger than merely being Cartier. In general, \(\mathbb Q\)-factoriality is a local Cartier condition and should not be identified with the statement that the entire global class group is torsion.
\end{warning}

\section{A computation strategy}

For an affine normal domain \(A\), explicit class-group computations often follow the same pattern:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose a convenient element \(f\in A\);
\item prove \(A_f\) is a UFD, so \(\Cl(A_f)=0\);
\item list the height-one primes containing \(f\);
\item conclude from localization that their classes generate \(\Cl(A)\);
\item compute principal divisors of selected functions to obtain relations;
\item prove the remaining candidate generators are nonprincipal.
\end{enumerate}

For the quadric cone this becomes
\[
\boxed{
A_x\text{ UFD}
\Longrightarrow
\Cl(A)=\langle[L]\rangle,
\qquad
\operatorname{div}(x)=2L
\Longrightarrow
2[L]=0,
\qquad
L\text{ nonprincipal}
\Longrightarrow
\Cl(A)=\mathbb Z/2.
}
\]

\section*{Bridge to VI/42}

The divisor sequence is now complete:
\[
\boxed{
K(X)^\times
\xrightarrow{\operatorname{div}}
\Div(X)
\longrightarrow
\Cl(X)
\longrightarrow0.
}
\]
For affine normal Noetherian domains,
\[
\boxed{
\Cl(A)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A\text{ is a UFD}.
}
\]
For the quadric cone,
\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{div}(x)=2L,
\qquad
[L]\neq0,
\qquad
\Cl(X)\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
}
\]

VI/42 moves from divisors to invertible sheaves. It will identify Cartier divisors modulo principal Cartier divisors with the Picard group, prove the locally factorial comparison
\[
  \Pic(X)\cong\Cl(X),
\]
and reinterpret the order-two class on the punctured cone as an order-two line bundle.
