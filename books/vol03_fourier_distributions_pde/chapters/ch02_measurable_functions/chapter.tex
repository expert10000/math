\chapter{Measurable Functions}
\label{ch:iii02}

Measurable functions are those whose inverse images respect the measurable structure. Their closure under algebra, limits, and monotone simple approximation makes them the natural domain of Lebesgue integration.

\section*{Learning goals}
After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item verify measurability of a function from inverse images of Borel sets or from level-set criteria;
\item prove that algebraic combinations and pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable under the stated hypotheses;
\item construct simple-function approximations to nonnegative measurable functions;
\item compute measurable level sets for explicit functions and use them to analyze distribution functions;
\item distinguish almost-everywhere equality from pointwise equality in measure-theoretic arguments;
\item produce examples of nonmeasurable behavior by identifying where the measurable-structure hypotheses fail;
\end{itemize}

\section{Inverse-image definition}
A real-valued function is measurable when inverse images of Borel sets are measurable.

\section{Ray criteria}
It is enough to test sets of the form $\{f<a\}$ or $\{f>a\}$ for all real $a$ because such rays generate the Borel sigma-algebra.

\section{Algebraic operations}
Sums, products, absolute values, maxima, and minima of measurable real-valued functions remain measurable.

\section{Pointwise limits}
Countable suprema, infima, limsup, liminf, and pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable.

\section{Simple functions}
A simple measurable function has finite range and measurable level sets; it is the finite-data model for integration.

\section{Approximation from below}
Every nonnegative measurable function is the increasing pointwise limit of nonnegative simple functions obtained by dyadic quantization and truncation.

\section{Almost-everywhere equivalence}
On complete measure spaces, changing a measurable function on a null set preserves measurability.

\section{Composition}
Composing a measurable map with a continuous or Borel measurable scalar map preserves measurability.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Pointwise limit theorem]\label{thm:iii02-01}
A pointwise limit of measurable real-valued functions is measurable.
\begin{proof}
Represent the limit using limsup and liminf, each built from countable suprema and infima of measurable functions.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Algebra closure]\label{thm:iii02-02}
If $f$ and $g$ are measurable, then $f+g$ and $fg$ are measurable.
\begin{proof}
The map $x\mapsto(f(x),g(x))$ is measurable into $\mathbb R^2$, and addition and multiplication are continuous Borel maps.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Simple approximation]\label{thm:iii02-03}
If $f\ge0$ is measurable, there exist simple $\phi_n\uparrow f$.
\begin{proof}
Truncate at height $n$ and replace values by the lower endpoints of dyadic intervals of width $2^{-n}$; measurability of the level sets gives simple functions and the mesh tends to zero.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}
