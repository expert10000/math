\chapter{Canonical Forms}
\label{ch:i12-canonical-forms}

When diagonalization fails, canonical forms measure the failure rather than hiding it. Jordan form over an algebraically closed field records nilpotent chains attached to each eigenvalue, while rational canonical form works over arbitrary fields using invariant factors. Both arise from viewing a vector space as a module over the polynomial ring via $p\cdot v=p(T)v$.

\section*{Learning goals}
After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item compute generalized eigenspaces using kernels of powers of \(T-\lambda I\);
\item construct Jordan chains and assemble them into a Jordan basis when the scalar field permits;
\item recover Jordan-block sizes from the growth of \(\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)^k\);
\item write the Jordan form of a nilpotent operator from its chain lengths;
\item use canonical form to compute powers, exponentials, or polynomial functions of an operator;
\item explain which data are invariant under similarity and therefore belong to the operator rather than a chosen basis;
\end{itemize}
\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Generalized eigenvectors}
A vector lies in the generalized eigenspace for $\lambda$ if $(T-\lambda I)^k v=0$ for some $k$.

\section{Nilpotent operators}
Nilpotent operators organize into chains $v,Nv,\ldots$. Their Jordan blocks encode chain lengths.

\section{Jordan blocks}
A Jordan block $J_r(\lambda)$ has $\lambda$ on the diagonal and ones on the superdiagonal. Its minimal polynomial is $(t-\lambda)^r$.


% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I12-example-01
\begin{example}[Recovering Jordan blocks from kernels]\label{ex:i12-expand-01}
Let \(N\) be nilpotent on a four-dimensional space with
\[
\dim\ker N=2,\qquad \dim\ker N^2=3,\qquad \dim\ker N^3=4.
\]
There are two Jordan blocks because \(\dim\ker N=2\). The growth from \(2\) to
\(3\) to \(4\) shows one chain has length \(3\), while the other has length
\(1\). Hence the nilpotent Jordan type is \(3+1\).

Kernel growth therefore reconstructs block sizes without first finding a
Jordan basis.
\end{example}
% END VOL01-EXPANSION I12-example-01
\section{Jordan canonical form}
Over an algebraically closed field, every operator is similar to a direct sum of Jordan blocks, unique up to block order.

\section{Primary decomposition}
If the minimal polynomial factors into pairwise coprime powers, the space decomposes into invariant primary components.

\section{Rational canonical form}
Over any field, invariant factors determine a block diagonal companion-matrix form. Jordan form is the split refinement of this module-theoretic structure.


% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I12-example-02
\begin{example}[A companion matrix realizes a polynomial]\label{ex:i12-expand-02}
For
\[
p(t)=t^3+2t+1,
\]
one companion convention gives
\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&-1\\
1&0&-2\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its characteristic polynomial is \(p(t)\). The matrix packages multiplication
by \(t\) on the cyclic module \(\mathbb F[t]/(p)\).

This is why companion blocks are natural rather than ad hoc: they turn a
polynomial relation into a linear operator.
\end{example}
% END VOL01-EXPANSION I12-example-02

% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I12-example-03
\begin{example}[Why rational form does not require eigenvalues]\label{ex:i12-expand-03}
A real rotation by \(90^\circ\) has characteristic polynomial \(t^2+1\), which
does not split over \(\mathbb R\). Jordan form over \(\mathbb R\) is therefore
not available in the usual eigenvalue sense. Rational canonical form, however,
uses invariant factors over the original field and needs no roots.

Canonical-form choice is field-sensitive: Jordan form emphasizes eigenvalues
after splitting; rational form remains intrinsic over the base field.
\end{example}

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Generalized eigenspace decomposition]\label{thm:i12-01}
If $m_T(t)=\prod_i(t-\lambda_i)^{r_i}$ with distinct $\lambda_i$, then $V=\bigoplus_i\ker(T-\lambda_iI)^{r_i}$.
\begin{proof}
The factors are pairwise coprime. Bézout identities in $\mathbb F[t]$ produce polynomial projectors whose images are the primary components.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Jordan block invariants]\label{thm:i12-02}
For a nilpotent operator, the numbers $\dim\ker N^k$ determine the Jordan block sizes.
\begin{proof}
The increment $\dim\ker N^k-\dim\ker N^{k-1}$ counts blocks of size at least $k$; successive differences recover the exact sizes.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Single Jordan block]\label{ex:i12-worked-01}
$J_3(0)$ is nilpotent of index $3$; kernel dimensions are $1,2,3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two blocks]\label{ex:i12-worked-02}
$J_2(0)\oplus J_1(0)$ has kernel dimensions $2,3$, revealing two blocks, one of which has length at least $2$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Companion matrix]\label{ex:i12-worked-03}
The companion matrix of a monic polynomial has that polynomial as both characteristic and minimal polynomial when it represents a cyclic module.
\end{example}

\section{Solved dossiers}
These dossiers are longer worked problems. They complement the shorter exercise--hint--solution triads by combining several ideas in one argument.

\begin{problem}[Nilpotent chain]\label{prob:i12-dossier-01}
For $N=J_3(0)$ compute $\dim\ker N$, $\dim\ker N^2$, and $\dim\ker N^3$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The standard Jordan chain has lengths three. The kernels have dimensions $1,2,3$ respectively. The increments $1,1,1$ indicate one block of size at least $1,2,3$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Two nilpotent blocks]\label{prob:i12-dossier-02}
If a nilpotent operator has Jordan blocks of sizes $3$ and $1$, compute $\dim\ker N^k$ for $k=1,2,3$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
At $k=1$, each block contributes one kernel dimension, giving $2$. At $k=2$, the size-3 block contributes $2$ and the size-1 block $1$, giving $3$. At $k=3$ the total is $4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Geometric multiplicity in Jordan form]\label{prob:i12-dossier-03}
How is the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$ read from Jordan form?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Each Jordan block for $\lambda$ contributes exactly one eigenvector direction, so the geometric multiplicity is the number of Jordan blocks carrying $\lambda$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Minimal polynomial from blocks]\label{prob:i12-dossier-04}
If the largest Jordan block for eigenvalue $2$ has size $4$ and for eigenvalue $-1$ has size $2$, find the minimal polynomial.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The exponents are the largest block sizes, so $m_T(t)=(t-2)^4(t+1)^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Diagonalizability from blocks]\label{prob:i12-dossier-05}
When is a Jordan form diagonal?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Exactly when every Jordan block has size $1$. Equivalently every eigenvalue's generalized eigenspace is already its ordinary eigenspace.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Generalized eigenspace]\label{prob:i12-dossier-06}
Show $\ker(T-\lambda I)^k$ is $T$-invariant.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$T$ commutes with every polynomial in $T$, so $(T-\lambda I)^kT(v)=T(T-\lambda I)^kv$. If the latter is zero, $Tv$ lies in the same kernel.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Primary decomposition idea]\label{prob:i12-dossier-07}
Why do coprime factors of the minimal polynomial lead to a direct-sum decomposition?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Bézout identities between the coprime polynomial factors produce polynomial projectors in $T$ whose images are the corresponding primary kernels and whose sum is the identity.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Real versus complex canonical form]\label{prob:i12-dossier-08}
Why may Jordan form fail over $\mathbb R$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Jordan form requires the minimal polynomial to split into linear factors. A real operator can have irreducible quadratic factors, such as a planar rotation. Rational canonical form does not require splitting.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Companion matrix]\label{prob:i12-dossier-09}
What is the conceptual role of a companion matrix?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
It represents multiplication by $t$ on the cyclic module $\mathbb F[t]/(p)$. Its characteristic and minimal polynomial are both the defining monic polynomial $p$ when the module is cyclic.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Kernel increments]\label{prob:i12-dossier-10}
How do the increments $d_k=\dim\ker N^k-\dim\ker N^{k-1}$ encode block sizes?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$d_k$ counts the number of Jordan blocks of size at least $k$. Therefore $d_k-d_{k+1}$ counts blocks of size exactly $k$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Similarity invariants]\label{prob:i12-dossier-11}
Name data preserved by similarity that are visible in Jordan form.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Eigenvalues, algebraic and geometric multiplicities, minimal polynomial, and the multiset of Jordan block sizes are all similarity invariants. Jordan form packages them completely over an algebraically closed field.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Canonical-form synthesis]\label{prob:i12-dossier-12}
Why is the module viewpoint $p\cdot v=p(T)v$ natural?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
It turns invariant subspaces into submodules and polynomial identities into annihilators. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over $\mathbb F[t]$ then yields rational canonical form, with Jordan form as the split primary refinement.
\end{solution}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-01}
Compute the minimal polynomial of $J_4(\lambda)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute \(\ker(T-\lambda I)^k\) for successive \(k\); the growth records the generalized eigenvectors.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It is $(t-\lambda)^4$ because the nilpotent part has index $4$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-02}
What is the geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue in Jordan form?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. A Jordan chain satisfies \((T-\lambda I)v_1=0\) and \((T-\lambda I)v_{j+1}=v_j\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It equals the number of Jordan blocks carrying that eigenvalue.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-03}
How can kernel dimensions reveal nilpotent block sizes?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute whichever of rank or nullity is easier, then use \(\dim V=\operatorname{rank}T+\operatorname{nullity}T\) as the dimension check. For a nilpotent operator, the largest Jordan block size is the nilpotency index.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The increment from $\ker N^{k-1}$ to $\ker N^k$ counts blocks of size at least $k$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-04}
When is a Jordan matrix diagonal?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute kernels of successive powers of \(T-\lambda I\); their dimension growth reveals the generalized-eigenvector chains. Use differences of successive kernel dimensions to count how many Jordan chains have length at least \(k\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Exactly when every Jordan block has size $1$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-05}
Why is Jordan form not always available over $\mathbb R$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute kernels of successive powers of \(T-\lambda I\); their dimension growth reveals the generalized-eigenvector chains. After finding chain generators, order each chain from eigenvector to highest generalized eigenvector before forming the basis.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The characteristic/minimal polynomial may not split into linear factors over $\mathbb R$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-06}
State the advantage of rational canonical form.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
To compute a matrix function on a Jordan block, separate the scalar part \(\lambda I\) from the nilpotent part.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It exists over every field and does not require the characteristic polynomial to split.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-07}
Show that generalized eigenspaces are invariant.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Similarity invariants include the eigenvalues and Jordan-block sizes, not the individual basis vectors in the chains.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $(T-\lambda I)^k v=0$, then $(T-\lambda I)^kT(v)=T(T-\lambda I)^kv=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i12-08}
Relate the largest Jordan block for $\lambda$ to the minimal polynomial.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute kernels of successive powers of \(T-\lambda I\); their dimension growth reveals the generalized-eigenvector chains. Check the total sizes of all blocks against \(\dim V\); this catches missing generalized eigenvectors.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Its size is the exponent of $(t-\lambda)$ in the minimal polynomial.
\end{solution}

\section{Graded supplementary exercises}
The following exercises extend the original exercise set without replacing it. They are grouped by mathematical role and increase in synthesis.

\subsection*{Standard computations}

\begin{exercise}[Jordan nilpotent block]\label{exr:i12-expand-01}
What are \(\dim\ker N\) and \(\dim\ker N^2\) for one \(3\times3\) nilpotent Jordan block?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Follow the Jordan chain.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\dim\ker N=1\), \(\dim\ker N^2=2\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Jordan block count]\label{exr:i12-expand-02}
For a nilpotent operator, what does \(\dim\ker N\) count?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Each Jordan block contributes one terminal kernel vector.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It equals the number of nilpotent Jordan blocks.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Largest block]\label{exr:i12-expand-03}
If \(N^4=0\) but \(N^3\neq0\), what is the largest nilpotent Jordan block size?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use nilpotency index.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The largest block has size \(4\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Companion polynomial]\label{exr:i12-expand-04}
Write a companion matrix for \(t^2+at+b\) using the convention with ones below the diagonal.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Place negatives of coefficients in the last column.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\begin{pmatrix}0&-b\\1&-a\end{pmatrix}\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Invariant factors]\label{exr:i12-expand-05}
What divisibility relation is required among invariant factors?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Recall the rational canonical chain.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(f_1\mid f_2\mid\cdots\mid f_r\).
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Proofs}

\begin{exercise}[Jordan uniqueness]\label{exr:i12-expand-06}
Explain what data determine Jordan form up to block permutation.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
For each eigenvalue, record all block sizes.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The multiset of Jordan block sizes for each eigenvalue determines the form.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Kernel-growth proof idea]\label{exr:i12-expand-07}
Why does \(\dim\ker N^k-\dim\ker N^{k-1}\) count blocks of size at least \(k\)?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Inspect one Jordan block at a time.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A block of size \(s\) contributes one new kernel dimension at stage \(k\) exactly when \(s\ge k\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Minimal polynomial from Jordan]\label{exr:i12-expand-08}
How is the exponent of \(t-\lambda\) in the minimal polynomial read from Jordan form?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Look at the longest \(\lambda\)-Jordan chain.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It is the size of the largest Jordan block for eigenvalue \(\lambda\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Characteristic polynomial from blocks]\label{exr:i12-expand-09}
Why does the characteristic polynomial multiply the block polynomials?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Jordan or companion canonical forms are block diagonal.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The determinant of a block diagonal \(tI-A\) is the product of block determinants.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Counterexamples}

\begin{exercise}[Counterexample: eigenvalues]\label{exr:i12-expand-10}
Why do eigenvalues alone not determine Jordan form?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compare \(I_2\) and a Jordan shear.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
They have the same eigenvalue multiset but different block structures.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Counterexample: Jordan over base field]\label{exr:i12-expand-11}
Why may real Jordan form fail for a real matrix?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Jordan blocks require eigenvalues in the field.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A \(90^\circ\) rotation has eigenvalues \(\pm i\), not real.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Counterexample: characteristic only]\label{exr:i12-expand-12}
Can the characteristic polynomial alone determine rational canonical form?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compare operators with same characteristic polynomial but different minimal polynomials.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
No; invariant factors depend on finer module structure than the characteristic polynomial.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Applications}

\begin{exercise}[Application: nilpotent dynamics]\label{exr:i12-expand-13}
How does Jordan block size affect powers of a nilpotent operator?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
A size-\(s\) nilpotent block dies exactly at power \(s\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The longest block determines the nilpotency index and how many iterates can remain nonzero.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Application: cyclic vector]\label{exr:i12-expand-14}
If \(V\) has a cyclic vector for \(T\), what shape does rational canonical form take?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
A cyclic module needs only one invariant factor.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The rational canonical form is a single companion block for the minimal/characteristic polynomial.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenges}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: reconstruct partition]\label{exr:i12-expand-15}
If a nilpotent operator on dimension \(6\) has kernel dimensions \(3,5,6\) for powers \(1,2,3\), find the block sizes.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Successive increments \(3,2,1\) count blocks of size at least \(1,2,3\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
There are three blocks, two of size at least \(2\), one of size at least \(3\); the sizes are \(3,2,1\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Challenge: primary decomposition]\label{exr:i12-expand-16}
If \(m_T=(t-1)^2(t+2)^3\), what invariant primary subspaces are suggested?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use coprime factors.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The space decomposes into the generalized \(1\)-eigenspace \(\ker(T-I)^2\) and generalized \(-2\)-eigenspace \(\ker(T+2I)^3\) under the usual primary-decomposition hypotheses.
\end{solution}

\section*{Chapter summary}
When diagonalization fails, canonical forms measure the failure rather than hiding it. Jordan form over an algebraically closed field records nilpotent chains attached to each eigenvalue, while rational canonical form works over arbitrary fields using invariant factors. Both arise from viewing a vector space as a module over the polynomial ring via $p\cdot v=p(T)v$.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
