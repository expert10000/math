\chapter{Invariant Subspaces and Triangularization}
\label{ch:i10-invariant-subspaces-and-triangularization}

Invariant subspaces let a large operator be studied through smaller pieces. Triangularization is the systematic expression of this idea: once an invariant flag is chosen, the matrix becomes triangular and its diagonal reveals the eigenvalues. Over an algebraically closed field, every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional space admits such a form.

\section*{Learning goals}
After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item test whether a subspace is invariant under a given operator;
\item construct a basis adapted to an invariant subspace and derive the resulting block or triangular matrix form;
\item read eigenvalues from the diagonal of a triangular matrix;
\item prove that an operator is triangularizable when its characteristic polynomial splits over the scalar field;
\item build an invariant flag one subspace at a time from eigenvectors and quotient-space reasoning;
\item distinguish triangularizability from diagonalizability by giving explicit examples;
\end{itemize}
\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Invariant subspaces}
A subspace $U$ is $T$-invariant when $T(U)\subseteq U$. The restriction $T|_U$ is then an operator on $U$.

\section{Quotient operators}
If $U$ is invariant, $T$ induces an operator on $V/U$ by $v+U\mapsto T(v)+U$.

\section{Invariant flags}
A chain $0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V$ with $\dim V_k=k$ and each $V_k$ invariant is a complete invariant flag.

\section{Triangular matrices and flags}
An operator is upper triangular in a basis exactly when the spans of its initial basis segments form an invariant flag.

\section{Triangularization theorem}
Over an algebraically closed field, every operator on a finite-dimensional nonzero vector space is triangularizable.

\section{Block triangular form}
Even without a complete flag, a single invariant subspace produces a block upper-triangular matrix, separating the restriction and quotient actions.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Flag criterion for triangularity]\label{thm:i10-01}
A basis $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ gives an upper-triangular matrix for $T$ if and only if $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)$ is invariant for every $k$.
\begin{proof}
Upper triangularity says $T(v_k)$ lies in the span of the first $k$ basis vectors. This is exactly the invariant-flag condition.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Triangularization over an algebraically closed field]\label{thm:i10-02}
Every operator on a finite-dimensional complex vector space has an upper-triangular matrix in some basis.
\begin{proof}
Choose an eigenvector, use its span as the first invariant subspace, pass to the quotient, and induct on dimension.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Nilpotent shift]\label{ex:i10-worked-01}
The map $N(e_1)=0$, $N(e_k)=e_{k-1}$ preserves the standard flag and is strictly upper triangular in the reversed standard convention.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Polynomial operator]\label{ex:i10-worked-02}
Differentiation preserves the nested polynomial spaces $\mathcal P_0\subset\mathcal P_1\subset\cdots$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Real rotation]\label{ex:i10-worked-03}
A planar rotation through $90^\circ$ has no invariant real line, so it cannot be triangularized over $\mathbb R$.
\end{example}

\section{Diagnostic checklist}
Before moving on, it is useful to distinguish four levels of understanding:
\begin{enumerate}
\item recognize the definitions in unfamiliar examples;
\item prove the core structural statements without coordinates when possible;
\item translate the same statement into a matrix or coordinate computation;
\item know which hypotheses are essential and produce a counterexample when one is removed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Solved dossiers}
These dossiers are longer worked problems. They complement the shorter exercise--hint--solution triads by combining several ideas in one argument.

\begin{problem}[Invariant line]\label{prob:i10-dossier-01}
For $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$, show $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$ is invariant.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$Ae_1=2e_1$, so every scalar multiple of $e_1$ is mapped back into the same line. It is in fact an eigenspace.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Block form from invariance]\label{prob:i10-dossier-02}
If $U$ is invariant under $T$, why does a basis beginning with a basis of $U$ give a block upper-triangular matrix?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Images of basis vectors in $U$ stay in $U$, so their coordinate columns have zero components in the complementary basis directions. That forces the lower-left block to vanish.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Quotient operator]\label{prob:i10-dossier-03}
Define the induced operator on $V/U$ when $U$ is $T$-invariant and prove it is well-defined.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Set $\overline T(v+U)=T(v)+U$. If $v-w\in U$, invariance gives $T(v-w)\in U$, so $T(v)+U=T(w)+U$. Thus the definition is independent of representative.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Flag criterion]\label{prob:i10-dossier-04}
Explain why an upper-triangular matrix preserves the coordinate flag $V_k=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k)$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The first $k$ columns of an upper-triangular matrix have zeros below row $k$, so the image of any vector supported in the first $k$ coordinates remains supported there.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Triangularization over $\mathbb C$]\label{prob:i10-dossier-05}
Outline the induction proving every complex operator is triangularizable.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Choose an eigenvector and its invariant line. Pass to the quotient, which has one lower dimension, triangularize the induced operator there by induction, then lift the quotient basis and prepend the eigenvector.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Failure over $\mathbb R$]\label{prob:i10-dossier-06}
Why is rotation by $90^\circ$ not triangularizable over $\mathbb R$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
A real upper-triangular matrix has real diagonal entries and hence real eigenvalues. The rotation has characteristic polynomial $t^2+1$ and no real eigenvalue, so no real triangular form exists.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Polynomial invariant subspaces]\label{prob:i10-dossier-07}
Show $\mathcal P_k$ is invariant under differentiation on $\mathcal P_n$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
If $\deg p\le k$, then $\deg p'\le k-1\le k$ (or $p'=0$). Hence differentiation maps $\mathcal P_k$ into itself.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Invariant complement need not exist]\label{prob:i10-dossier-08}
Give a $2\times2$ example with an invariant line but no invariant complementary line.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The shear $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ preserves $\operatorname{span}(e_1)$. Its only eigenspace is that line, so no distinct invariant line can complement it.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Restriction spectrum]\label{prob:i10-dossier-09}
If $U$ is invariant, why must every eigenvalue of $T|_U$ also be an eigenvalue of $T$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
An eigenvector of the restriction is a nonzero vector $u\in U$ with $T(u)=\lambda u$, which is also an eigenvector of $T$ on the whole space.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Nested kernels]\label{prob:i10-dossier-10}
For a nilpotent $N$, show $\ker N\subseteq\ker N^2\subseteq\cdots$ are invariant.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
If $N^kv=0$, then $N^k(Nv)=N^{k+1}v=0$, so $Nv\in\ker N^k$ after adjusting indices appropriately; each kernel is invariant under $N$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Diagonal form as strongest triangular form]\label{prob:i10-dossier-11}
How does diagonalization strengthen triangularization?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Triangularization produces a complete invariant flag but can retain couplings above the diagonal. Diagonalization decomposes the space into one-dimensional invariant eigenspaces, eliminating all off-diagonal couplings.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Invariant-subspace synthesis]\label{prob:i10-dossier-12}
Why are invariant subspaces the natural mechanism for induction in operator theory?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
They let one split the problem into a restriction on a smaller subspace and an induced operator on a lower-dimensional quotient. Structural information can then be assembled from these two simpler pieces.
\end{solution}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-01}
Show every eigenspace is invariant.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Test invariance by applying \(T\) to a basis of the subspace; every image must remain in the subspace.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $v\in E_\lambda$, then $T(v)=\lambda v\in E_\lambda$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-02}
If $U$ is invariant, prove the quotient operator is well-defined.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Apply the operator to a basis of the proposed subspace; invariance means every resulting vector stays in its span. Choose a basis of the invariant subspace first and extend it to a basis of the whole space.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $v+U=w+U$, then $v-w\in U$, so $T(v-w)\in U$ and $T(v)+U=T(w)+U$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-03}
Why does an upper-triangular matrix preserve the coordinate flag?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Choose a basis adapted to the invariant flag; the vanishing entries below the diagonal encode preservation of the successive subspaces. With an adapted basis, entries below the relevant block vanish because vectors in the invariant subspace cannot leave it.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The first $k$ columns have zeros below row $k$, so vectors supported in the first $k$ coordinates map back into that subspace.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-04}
Give an invariant subspace of a diagonal operator.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Apply the operator to a basis of the proposed subspace; invariance means every resulting vector stays in its span. For a triangular matrix, the characteristic polynomial is the product of \((\lambda-a_{ii})\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The span of any subset of eigenbasis vectors is invariant.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-05}
Why may triangularization fail over $\mathbb R$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Build the invariant flag one eigenvector at a time; then pass to the induced map on a quotient.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The induction starts by choosing an eigenvector; a real operator can have no real eigenvalue.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-06}
Show the characteristic polynomial of a triangular matrix is the product of $t-a_{ii}$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Reduce to triangular form while recording how each row operation changes the determinant. Triangularizable does not mean diagonalizable: look for a nonzero Jordan off-diagonal entry.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The matrix $tI-A$ is triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-07}
Describe the block form associated with an invariant subspace $U$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Apply the operator to a basis of the proposed subspace; invariance means every resulting vector stays in its span. Over a field where the characteristic polynomial splits, start with one eigenvector and induct on dimension.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Choose a basis of $U$ and extend it to $V$. The lower-left block is zero because $T(U)\subseteq U$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i10-08}
Explain why triangularization already reveals all eigenvalues.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Check whether the proposed subspace is generated by eigenvectors; such a span is automatically invariant.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The characteristic polynomial is the product of $t$ minus the diagonal entries, so the diagonal entries are precisely the eigenvalues with multiplicity.
\end{solution}

\section*{Chapter summary}
Invariant subspaces let a large operator be studied through smaller pieces. Triangularization is the systematic expression of this idea: once an invariant flag is chosen, the matrix becomes triangular and its diagonal reveals the eigenvalues. Over an algebraically closed field, every operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional space admits such a form.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
