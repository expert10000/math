\chapter{Scalars, Vectors and Linear Combinations}
\label{ch:i01-scalars-vectors-and-linear-combinations}

Linear algebra begins with two operations: adding vectors and scaling them by elements of a field. The point of the abstract definition is not to hide geometry but to identify exactly which arguments depend only on those operations. This chapter moves back and forth between concrete coordinate vectors, functions and polynomials so that linear combinations become a working language rather than a formal slogan.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}

\item explain and use vector spaces in basis-free and coordinate form;

\item explain and use affine versus linear geometry in basis-free and coordinate form;

\end{itemize}

\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Fields and scalars}
We work over a field $\mathbb F$, usually $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. The field laws allow addition, subtraction, multiplication and division by nonzero scalars. Every vector-space identity ultimately rests on these laws together with the vector-space axioms.

\section{Vector spaces}
A vector space $V$ over $\mathbb F$ is an abelian group under addition equipped with scalar multiplication $\mathbb F\times V\to V$ satisfying distributivity, compatibility, and $1v=v$. The zero vector is unique, and $0v=0$, $a0=0$, and $(-1)v=-v$ follow from the axioms.

\section{Linear combinations}
Given $v_1,\ldots,v_r\in V$, a linear combination is $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_rv_r$ with $a_i\in\mathbb F$. Finite linear combinations are the algebraic mechanism for building new vectors from old ones.

\section{Concrete models}
The spaces $\mathbb F^n$, polynomial spaces $\mathbb F[x]$, matrix spaces $M_{m,n}(\mathbb F)$, and function spaces $\mathbb F^X$ all satisfy the same vector-space axioms. Coordinatewise formulas are examples of a common structure.

\section{Affine versus linear geometry}
Linear combinations with arbitrary coefficients preserve the origin. Affine combinations impose $a_1+\cdots+a_r=1$ and describe points in affine hulls. Convex combinations add $a_i\ge0$ and describe convex hulls.

\section{Complex scalars and realification}
A complex vector space is automatically a real vector space after restricting scalars, but its real dimension doubles when the complex dimension is finite. Forgetting part of the scalar field changes the linear relations that are allowed.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness consequences of the axioms]\label{thm:i01-01}
For every vector space $V$, the zero vector and additive inverse of each vector are unique; moreover $0v=0$, $a0=0$, and $(-a)v=-(av)$ for all $a\in\mathbb F$.
\begin{proof}
Each identity follows by adding a suitable additive inverse and using distributivity. For example, $0v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$; cancelling $0v$ gives $0v=0$.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Linear-combination regrouping]\label{thm:i01-02}
A linear combination of linear combinations is again a linear combination of the original vectors.
\begin{proof}
Substitute each inner expression and use associativity and distributivity to collect the coefficient of each original vector.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Polynomials]\label{ex:i01-worked-01}
In $\mathbb R[x]$, the polynomial $3-2x+5x^3$ is a linear combination of $1,x,x^3$. The same expression may be viewed inside any larger polynomial space without changing its coefficients.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Functions]\label{ex:i01-worked-02}
If $f,g:X\to\mathbb R$, then $2f-3g$ is the function $x\mapsto 2f(x)-3g(x)$. Function spaces make clear that vectors need not be arrows.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Matrices]\label{ex:i01-worked-03}
The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ is a linear combination of the four matrix units $E_{ij}$. Matrix units play the role of coordinate directions.
\end{example}

Before moving on, it is useful to distinguish four levels of understanding:

\item translate the same statement into a matrix or coordinate computation;
\item know which hypotheses are essential and produce a counterexample when one is removed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Solved dossiers}
These dossiers are longer worked problems. They complement the shorter exercise--hint--solution triads by combining several ideas in one argument.

\begin{problem}[A hidden dependence in three vectors]\label{prob:i01-dossier-01}
In $\mathbb R^3$ let $u=(1,1,0)$, $v=(0,1,1)$ and $w=(1,2,1)$. Determine all triples $(a,b,c)$ such that $au+bv+cw=0$ and interpret the answer.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Since $w=u+v$, the relation is $(a+c)u+(b+c)v=0$. The vectors $u,v$ are independent, so $a+c=b+c=0$. Hence $(a,b,c)=t(-1,-1,1)$. The one-dimensional relation space records exactly one redundancy: $w$ contributes no new direction.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Changing the scalar field]\label{prob:i01-dossier-02}
Regard $\mathbb C$ first as a vector space over $\mathbb C$ and then over $\mathbb R$. Compare the linear combination $i\cdot 1$ in the two structures and explain why the dimensions differ.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Over $\mathbb C$, multiplication by $i$ is scalar multiplication, so $i=i\cdot1$ and $\{1\}$ is a basis. Over $\mathbb R$, $i$ is not an available scalar; one needs both $1$ and $i$, and every $z=a+bi$ is the real linear combination $a\cdot1+b\cdot i$. Thus the dimensions are $1$ over $\mathbb C$ and $2$ over $\mathbb R$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Polynomial coordinates without a basis theorem]\label{prob:i01-dossier-03}
Express $p(x)=2-3x+5x^2$ as a linear combination of $q_1=1+x$, $q_2=x+x^2$, and $q_3=1-x^2$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Seek $p=aq_1+bq_2+cq_3$. Comparing coefficients gives $a+c=2$, $a+b=-3$, and $b-c=5$. The third equation follows from the first two, so there are infinitely many representations: taking $a=t$ gives $b=-3-t$ and $c=2-t$. This nonuniqueness reveals that $q_1,q_2,q_3$ are dependent.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Affine but not linear]\label{prob:i01-dossier-04}
Show that the line $L=\{(x,y):x+y=1\}$ is closed under affine combinations but is not a vector subspace of $\mathbb R^2$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
If $p,q\in L$ and $\alpha+\beta=1$, then the coordinate sum of $\alpha p+\beta q$ is $\alpha\cdot1+\beta\cdot1=1$, so $L$ is affine-combination closed. But $(0,0)\notin L$, so $L$ cannot be a vector subspace.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Matrix units as building blocks]\label{prob:i01-dossier-05}
Write $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ as a linear combination of the matrix units $E_{11},E_{12},E_{21},E_{22}$ and explain why the representation is unique.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$A=2E_{11}-E_{12}+3E_{21}+4E_{22}$. If a linear combination of the four matrix units is zero, inspection of the four entries forces every coefficient to vanish. Thus the units are independent, and entrywise comparison also shows they span $M_2(\mathbb R)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Functions as vectors]\label{prob:i01-dossier-06}
Let $f(t)=1$, $g(t)=t$, and $h(t)=1+t$ in the vector space of real functions on $[0,1]$. Find every relation $af+bg+ch=0$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The identity means $(a+c)+(b+c)t=0$ for every $t$. Hence $a+c=0$ and $b+c=0$, so $(a,b,c)=s(-1,-1,1)$. Again $h=f+g$, illustrating that dependence is structural and not tied to coordinate vectors.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Convex versus affine combinations]\label{prob:i01-dossier-07}
Can $(3,0)$ be written as a convex combination of $(0,0)$ and $(2,0)$? Can it be written as an affine combination?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
A convex combination has the form $(2\beta,0)$ with $\alpha+\beta=1$ and $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, so $0\le2\beta\le2$; $(3,0)$ is impossible. For an affine combination, nonnegativity is dropped: choose $\beta=3/2$ and $\alpha=-1/2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Complex linearity test]\label{prob:i01-dossier-08}
Define $T:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ by $T(z)=\overline z$. Is $T$ linear over $\mathbb R$? Over $\mathbb C$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For real scalars $a,b$, conjugation satisfies $T(az+bw)=aT(z)+bT(w)$, so it is real-linear. But $T(iz)=-i\overline z$ while $iT(z)=i\overline z$, so it is not complex-linear unless $z=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[A parameterized linear combination]\label{prob:i01-dossier-09}
For which real $t$ does $(1,t,t^2)$ belong to the span of $(1,0,1)$ and $(0,1,1)$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
A general span vector is $(a,b,a+b)$. Matching gives $a=1$, $b=t$, and therefore $t^2=1+t$. Thus $t$ must solve $t^2-t-1=0$, so $t=(1\pm\sqrt5)/2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Why finite sums are enough]\label{prob:i01-dossier-10}
Explain why the definition of span uses finite rather than arbitrary infinite linear combinations, even in an infinite-dimensional vector space.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Vector-space axioms define only binary addition and its finite iteration. No notion of convergence or infinite summation is part of a general vector space. Infinite sums require extra topological or analytic structure, so algebraic span is necessarily built from finite combinations.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Direct verification from axioms]\label{prob:i01-dossier-11}
Prove from the vector-space axioms that if $av=0$ and $a\ne0$, then $v=0$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Multiply the equation by $a^{-1}$. Compatibility of scalar multiplication gives $v=1v=(a^{-1}a)v=a^{-1}(av)=a^{-1}0=0$. This cancellation principle is used constantly in linear algebra.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[A geometric synthesis]\label{prob:i01-dossier-12}
Describe the set of all linear combinations of two nonparallel vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ and compare it with their affine hull.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The linear combinations form the plane through the origin spanned by the two vectors. Their affine hull as two points consists only of the line through those two points. The distinction is that span treats the vectors as directions from the origin, whereas affine hull treats them as points with coefficients summing to one.
\end{solution}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-01}
Show directly from the axioms that $a(-v)=-(av)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Use $v+(-v)=0$, multiply by $a$, and use distributivity. The resulting equation $av+a(-v)=0$ says that $a(-v)$ is the additive inverse of $av$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-02}
Write $(2,-1,3)$ as a linear combination of the standard vectors of $\mathbb R^3$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
$(2,-1,3)=2e_1-e_2+3e_3$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-03}
Give a vector-space structure on the set of real-valued continuous functions on $[0,1]$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Use pointwise addition and scalar multiplication. Continuity is preserved by both operations, so the operations remain inside the set.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-04}
Why is the unit circle in $\mathbb R^2$ not a vector space?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It does not contain the zero vector and is not closed under scalar multiplication; for example $2(1,0)=(2,0)$ is not on the unit circle.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-05}
Over which scalars is $\mathbb C$ naturally a one-dimensional vector space? What is its dimension over $\mathbb R$?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It is one-dimensional over $\mathbb C$, with basis $\{1\}$, and two-dimensional over $\mathbb R$, with basis $\{1,i\}$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-06}
Find an affine combination of $(0,0)$ and $(2,0)$ giving $(3,0)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Take coefficients $-\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac32$; they sum to $1$ and give $(3,0)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-07}
Show that the set of all $2\times2$ real matrices is a vector space.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Matrix addition is an abelian group operation and scalar multiplication satisfies the vector-space laws entrywise.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i01-08}
Explain why a finite sum $\sum_i a_i v_i$ is independent of parentheses.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Associativity of vector addition allows any parenthesization; a formal induction on the number of summands proves the claim.
\end{solution}
