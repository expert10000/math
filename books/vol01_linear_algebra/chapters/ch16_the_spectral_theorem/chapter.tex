\chapter{The Spectral Theorem}
\label{ch:i16-the-spectral-theorem}

The spectral theorem is the central structure theorem for self-adjoint, symmetric and normal operators. It says that the right compatibility with the inner product upgrades triangularization to orthonormal diagonalization. Functional calculus and positive operators then become simple operations on eigenvalues.

\section*{Learning goals}
After this chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item recognize self-adjoint, normal, and unitary operators from matrix identities;
\item find an orthonormal eigenbasis for a concrete self-adjoint or normal matrix;
\item unitarily or orthogonally diagonalize an operator covered by the spectral theorem;
\item write a spectral decomposition in terms of eigenspace projections;
\item use orthogonal eigenspaces to simplify powers and functions of a normal operator;
\item identify precisely which hypothesis fails when an operator does not admit an orthonormal eigenbasis;
\end{itemize}
\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Adjoints}
The adjoint $T^*$ is characterized by $\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,T^*w\rangle$.

\section{Self-adjoint and normal operators}
$T$ is self-adjoint when $T=T^*$ and normal when $TT^*=T^*T$.


% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-01
\begin{example}[Self-adjoint, normal, or neither?]\label{ex:i16-expand-01}
For
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&i\\-i&3\end{pmatrix},
\]
the conjugate transpose is
\[
A^*=\begin{pmatrix}2&i\\-i&3\end{pmatrix}=A.
\]
Hence \(A\) is self-adjoint and therefore normal. Its characteristic polynomial
has real coefficients and real roots, as the spectral theorem predicts.
The first check should always be the structural identity \(A^*=A\), because it
immediately unlocks real spectrum and orthonormal diagonalization.
\end{example}
% END VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-01
\section{Reality of the spectrum}
Eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators are real, and eigenspaces for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

\section{Spectral theorem}
A finite-dimensional complex operator is normal if and only if it is unitarily diagonalizable. Over $\mathbb R$, symmetric operators are orthogonally diagonalizable.


% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-02
\begin{example}[Full orthogonal spectral decomposition]\label{ex:i16-expand-02}
For
\[
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},
\]
the eigenvalues are \(3\) and \(1\), with orthonormal eigenvectors
\[
q_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1),\qquad q_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1).
\]
Thus
\[
A=3q_1q_1^T+1q_2q_2^T.
\]
Multiplying the two rank-one projectors out recovers \(A\), which checks the
decomposition. The formula separates the operator into mutually orthogonal
spectral modes.
\end{example}
% END VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-02
\section{Functional calculus}
If $T=UDU^*$, define $f(T)=Uf(D)U^*$ for functions defined on the spectrum.


% BEGIN VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-03
\begin{example}[Functional calculus without repeated multiplication]\label{ex:i16-expand-03}
With the same matrix \(A\), compute \(A^{10}\). The spectral decomposition gives
\[
A^{10}=3^{10}q_1q_1^T+q_2q_2^T.
\]
No ten-fold matrix multiplication is needed. More generally, for any function
defined on the spectrum,
\[
f(A)=f(3)q_1q_1^T+f(1)q_2q_2^T.
\]
This is the computational force of the spectral theorem: once eigenspaces are
known, powers, exponentials, square roots and other functions act only on
eigenvalues.
\end{example}
% END VOL01-EXPANSION I16-example-03
\section{Positive operators}
A self-adjoint operator is positive semidefinite when $\langle Tv,v\rangle\ge0$; spectrally this means all eigenvalues are nonnegative.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Orthogonality of self-adjoint eigenspaces]\label{thm:i16-01}
Eigenvectors of a self-adjoint operator associated with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
\begin{proof}
If $Tv=\lambda v$ and $Tw=\mu w$, then $\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,Tw\rangle=\mu\langle v,w\rangle$.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Finite-dimensional spectral theorem]\label{thm:i16-02}
Every self-adjoint operator on a finite-dimensional inner-product space has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.
\begin{proof}
Choose an eigenvector, take its orthogonal complement, show that complement is invariant under self-adjointness, and induct on dimension.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Real symmetric matrix]\label{ex:i16-worked-01}
$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has orthogonal eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ with eigenvalues $3$ and $1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Positive square root]\label{ex:i16-worked-02}
For positive $A=UDU^*$, set $A^{1/2}=UD^{1/2}U^*$; then $A^{1/2}$ is positive and squares to $A$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Normal but not self-adjoint]\label{ex:i16-worked-03}
A nontrivial complex unitary rotation is normal but may not be self-adjoint.
\end{example}

\section{Diagnostic checklist}
Before moving on, it is useful to distinguish four levels of understanding:
\begin{enumerate}
\item recognize the definitions in unfamiliar examples;
\item prove the core structural statements without coordinates when possible;
\item translate the same statement into a matrix or coordinate computation;
\item know which hypotheses are essential and produce a counterexample when one is removed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Solved dossiers}
These dossiers are longer worked problems. They complement the shorter exercise--hint--solution triads by combining several ideas in one argument.

\begin{problem}[Adjoint matrix]\label{prob:i16-dossier-01}
In the standard complex inner product, what matrix represents the adjoint of $A$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The adjoint is represented by the conjugate transpose $A^*=\overline A^T$, because $\langle Ax,y\rangle=x^*A^*y=\langle x,A^*y\rangle$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Real eigenvalues]\label{prob:i16-dossier-02}
Prove an eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
If $Tv=\lambda v$, then $\lambda\|v\|^2=\langle Tv,v\rangle=\langle v,Tv\rangle=\overline\lambda\|v\|^2$. Since $v\ne0$, $\lambda=\overline\lambda$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Orthogonal eigenspaces]\label{prob:i16-dossier-03}
Why are eigenspaces of a self-adjoint operator for distinct eigenvalues orthogonal?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
If $Tv=\lambda v$ and $Tw=\mu w$, self-adjointness gives $\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=\langle Tv,w\rangle=\langle v,Tw\rangle=\mu\langle v,w\rangle$. Distinctness forces the inner product to vanish.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Diagonalizing a symmetric matrix]\label{prob:i16-dossier-04}
Diagonalize $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ orthogonally.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Eigenvalues are $3$ and $1$ with eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. Normalizing them gives an orthogonal matrix $Q$ with $Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Positive square root]\label{prob:i16-dossier-05}
How does the spectral theorem construct $A^{1/2}$ for positive semidefinite $A$?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Write $A=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)U^*$ with $\lambda_i\ge0$. Define $A^{1/2}=U\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_i})U^*$. It is positive and squares to $A$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Normal operator]\label{prob:i16-dossier-06}
Why is every unitary operator normal?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For unitary $U$, $U^*U=UU^*=I$, so $UU^*=U^*U$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Polynomial functional calculus]\label{prob:i16-dossier-07}
If $T=UDU^*$ is normal, compute $p(T)$.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
$p(T)=Up(D)U^*$, where $p(D)$ is obtained by applying $p$ to each diagonal eigenvalue. This follows because powers of $T$ preserve the same diagonalizing basis.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Positivity and spectrum]\label{prob:i16-dossier-08}
Why is a self-adjoint operator positive semidefinite iff all eigenvalues are nonnegative?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
In an orthonormal eigenbasis, $\langle Tv,v\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i|v_i|^2$. This is nonnegative for all $v$ exactly when each $\lambda_i\ge0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Invariant orthogonal complement]\label{prob:i16-dossier-09}
If $U$ is invariant under self-adjoint $T$, show $U^\perp$ is invariant.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For $w\in U^\perp$ and $u\in U$, $\langle Tw,u\rangle=\langle w,Tu\rangle=0$ because $Tu\in U$. Hence $Tw\in U^\perp$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Spectral norm]\label{prob:i16-dossier-10}
For self-adjoint $T$, what is $\|T\|_2$ in terms of eigenvalues?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Because the operator is unitarily diagonalizable, its singular values are $|\lambda_i|$. Therefore the operator norm is $\max_i|\lambda_i|$.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Spectral projector]\label{prob:i16-dossier-11}
How can the projection onto an eigenspace be expressed when eigenvalues are distinct?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Use Lagrange interpolation: $P_i=\prod_{j\ne i}(T-\lambda_jI)/(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)$. It acts as $1$ on the $\lambda_i$ eigenspace and $0$ on the others.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Spectral synthesis]\label{prob:i16-dossier-12}
Why is the spectral theorem stronger than ordinary diagonalization?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
It provides an orthonormal eigenbasis, not merely an arbitrary eigenbasis. Consequently the change-of-basis matrix is unitary, preserving the inner product and making geometric and numerical conclusions immediately available.
\end{solution}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-01}
Show eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator are real.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Check the adjoint identity first: self-adjoint means \(A^*=A\), normal means \(A^*A=AA^*\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $Tv=\lambda v$, then $\lambda\|v\|^2=\langle Tv,v\rangle=\langle v,Tv\rangle=\overline\lambda\|v\|^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-02}
Diagonalize $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ orthogonally.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. For a self-adjoint matrix, compute eigenspaces and then orthonormalize within repeated-eigenvalue eigenspaces.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Normalize eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ to form $Q$; then $Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-03}
Why is every unitary operator normal?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute \(A^*A\) and compare it with \(I\); this single identity packages preservation of the inner product. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator are orthogonal; use that before Gram--Schmidt.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
$UU^*=U^*U=I$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-04}
Show a positive semidefinite operator has nonnegative eigenvalues.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
First determine \(\lambda\) from \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the eigenspace. Write the operator as \(\sum_\lambda \lambda P_\lambda\), where \(P_\lambda\) projects onto the eigenspace.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
For an eigenvector $v$, $\langle Tv,v\rangle=\lambda\|v\|^2\ge0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-05}
Define $T^2$ using spectral functional calculus.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
To compute \(f(T)\), apply \(f\) to each eigenvalue in the spectral decomposition.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $T=UDU^*$ then $T^2=UD^2U^*$, corresponding to $f(t)=t^2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-06}
Why is the orthogonal complement of an invariant eigenspace also invariant for a self-adjoint operator?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use the orthogonal decomposition \(v=P_Uv+(v-P_Uv)\) and identify where the residual lies. Normality is exactly the hypothesis for unitary diagonalization over \(\mathbb C\).
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
If $w$ is orthogonal to the eigenspace and $v$ is in it, then $\langle Tw,v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle=\lambda\langle w,v\rangle=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-07}
What extra conclusion does normality give beyond triangularization?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute \(A^*A\) and compare it with \(I\); this single identity packages preservation of the inner product. Check that the chosen eigenvectors are not merely independent but orthonormal.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A normal operator can be diagonalized by a unitary basis change, so the basis can be chosen orthonormal.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i16-08}
Relate spectral theorem to quadratic forms.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Find the eigenspaces first and use orthogonality between distinct eigenspaces before assembling an orthonormal eigenbasis. If a matrix is triangular and normal, compare off-diagonal norm contributions to show it must be diagonal.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A real symmetric matrix can be orthogonally diagonalized, reducing its quadratic form to a sum of scalar multiples of squares.
\end{solution}

\section{Graded supplementary exercises}
These exercises extend the preserved foundational set and are graded by mathematical role.

\subsection*{Standard computations}

\begin{exercise}[Adjoint]\label{exr:i16-expand-01}
Compute \(A^*\) for \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\2&3i\end{pmatrix}\).
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Transpose and complex-conjugate every entry.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(A^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\-i&-3i\end{pmatrix}\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Self-adjoint test]\label{exr:i16-expand-02}
Is \(\begin{pmatrix}2&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\) self-adjoint?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compare the matrix with its conjugate transpose.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Yes; the off-diagonal entries are complex conjugates.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Orthogonal diagonalization]\label{exr:i16-expand-03}
Orthogonally diagonalize \(\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\).
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use eigenvectors \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\) and normalize them.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The eigenvalues are \(6,2\); with normalized eigenvectors as columns of \(Q\), \(Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(6,2)\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Spectral projector]\label{exr:i16-expand-04}
For distinct eigenvalues \(2,5\), write the projector onto the \(5\)-eigenspace as a polynomial in \(T\).
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use Lagrange interpolation.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(P_5=(T-2I)/(5-2)=(T-2I)/3\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Positive square root]\label{exr:i16-expand-05}
If \(A=Q\operatorname{diag}(9,4)Q^T\) is positive, write \(A^{1/2}\).
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Apply the square root to eigenvalues.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(A^{1/2}=Q\operatorname{diag}(3,2)Q^T\).
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Proofs}

\begin{exercise}[Real spectrum]\label{exr:i16-expand-06}
Prove every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compare \(\langle Tv,v\rangle\) with its conjugate using self-adjointness.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
For \(Tv=\lambda v\), \(\lambda\|v\|^2=\overline{\lambda}\|v\|^2\), so \(\lambda=\overline\lambda\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Orthogonal eigenspaces]\label{exr:i16-expand-07}
Prove eigenspaces of a self-adjoint operator for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compute \(\langle Tv,w\rangle\) in two ways.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\((\lambda-\mu)\langle v,w\rangle=0\); distinct eigenvalues force \(\langle v,w\rangle=0\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Invariant orthogonal complement]\label{exr:i16-expand-08}
If \(U\) is invariant under self-adjoint \(T\), prove \(U^\perp\) is invariant.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
For \(w\in U^\perp,u\in U\), move \(T\) across the inner product.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\langle Tw,u\rangle=\langle w,Tu\rangle=0\) because \(Tu\in U\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Positivity from spectrum]\label{exr:i16-expand-09}
Prove a self-adjoint operator is positive semidefinite iff all eigenvalues are nonnegative.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Expand a vector in an orthonormal eigenbasis.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\langle Tv,v\rangle=\sum\lambda_i|c_i|^2\), which is nonnegative for all coefficients exactly when every \(\lambda_i\ge0\).
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Counterexamples and hypothesis testing}

\begin{exercise}[Normal not self-adjoint]\label{exr:i16-expand-10}
Give a normal complex matrix that is not self-adjoint.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Use a non-real unitary diagonal matrix.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\operatorname{diag}(i,1)\) is unitary and normal but not equal to its adjoint.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Diagonalizable not normal]\label{exr:i16-expand-11}
Give a diagonalizable matrix that is not normal.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Conjugate a real diagonal matrix by a non-orthogonal basis change.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
For example \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) has distinct eigenvalues and is diagonalizable, but \(AA^T\ne A^TA\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Real spectral theorem limit]\label{exr:i16-expand-12}
Why does the complex normal spectral theorem not imply every real normal matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable over \(\mathbb R\)?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Consider a nontrivial planar rotation.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A \(90^\circ\) rotation is real normal but has no real eigenvalues; over \(\mathbb R\) it has a rotation block rather than a real diagonal form.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Applications and investigations}

\begin{exercise}[Matrix exponential]\label{exr:i16-expand-13}
If \(A=QDQ^T\) is symmetric, how do you compute \(e^A\)?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Apply \(e^t\) to each eigenvalue.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(e^A=Qe^DQ^T\), where \(e^D\) is diagonal with entries \(e^{\lambda_i}\).
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Covariance matrix]\label{exr:i16-expand-14}
Why does the spectral theorem underlie principal-component analysis for a covariance matrix?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
A covariance matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
It has orthonormal eigenvectors; leading eigenvectors give orthogonal directions of greatest variance.
\end{solution}

\subsection*{Challenge problems}

\begin{exercise}[Normal triangular matrix]\label{exr:i16-expand-15}
Prove an upper-triangular normal complex matrix is diagonal.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Compare squared norms of the first row and first column, then induct.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Normality forces the off-diagonal entries in the first row to vanish; remove that coordinate and iterate, leaving only diagonal entries.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}[Spectral norm]\label{exr:i16-expand-16}
For self-adjoint \(T\), prove \(\|T\|_2=\max_i|\lambda_i|\).
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Expand a unit vector in an orthonormal eigenbasis and bound the resulting weighted sum.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
\(\|Tv\|^2=\sum|\lambda_i|^2|c_i|^2\le(\max|\lambda_i|)^2\); equality occurs on an eigenvector attaining the maximum.
\end{solution}

\section*{Chapter summary}
The spectral theorem is the central structure theorem for self-adjoint, symmetric and normal operators. It says that the right compatibility with the inner product upgrades triangularization to orthonormal diagonalization. Functional calculus and positive operators then become simple operations on eigenvalues.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
