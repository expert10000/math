\\chapter{Kernels, Images and Isomorphisms}
\\label{ch:i06-kernels-images-and-isomorphisms}

Kernel and image measure exactly how a linear transformation fails to be injective or surjective. The rank–nullity theorem balances those two defects and turns structural questions into dimension counts. Quotient spaces then package the first isomorphism theorem in its natural form.

\\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\\boxed{\\text{definition}\\;\\longrightarrow\\;\\text{structural theorem}\\;\\longrightarrow\\;\\text{coordinate calculation}\\;\\longrightarrow\\;\\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\\section{Kernel}
$\ker T=\{v:T(v)=0\}$ is a subspace of the domain. A linear map is injective exactly when its kernel is zero.

\\section{Image and rank}
$\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}$ is a subspace of the codomain. Its dimension is the rank.

\\section{Rank–nullity}
For finite-dimensional $V$, $\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T$.

\\section{Injective versus surjective}
For a linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space, injective, surjective and bijective are equivalent.

\\section{Quotients}
For a subspace $U\subseteq V$, the quotient $V/U$ consists of cosets $v+U$. It has dimension $\dim V-\dim U$ in finite dimensions.

\\section{First isomorphism theorem}
The map $V/\ker T\to\operatorname{im}T$, $v+\ker T\mapsto T(v)$, is a well-defined linear isomorphism.

\\section{Core structural results}

\\begin{theorem}[Rank–nullity theorem]\\label{thm:i06-01}
If $T:V\to W$ and $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim V=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T$.
\\begin{proof}
Choose a basis of the kernel and extend it to a basis of $V$. The images of the added basis vectors form a basis of the image.
\\end{proof}
\\end{theorem}

\\begin{theorem}[First isomorphism theorem]\\label{thm:i06-02}
Every linear map induces an isomorphism $V/\ker T\cong\operatorname{im}T$.
\\begin{proof}
The induced map is well-defined because changing a representative by a kernel vector does not change its image; injectivity and surjectivity are immediate.
\\end{proof}
\\end{theorem}

\\section{Worked examples}

\\begin{example}[Differentiation]\\label{ex:i06-worked-01}
On $\mathcal P_n$, differentiation has kernel the constants and image $\mathcal P_{n-1}$, so nullity $1$ plus rank $n$ equals $n+1$.
\\end{example}

\\begin{example}[Projection]\\label{ex:i06-worked-02}
The projection $\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$, $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y)$, has one-dimensional kernel and rank $2$.
\\end{example}

\\begin{example}[Trace functional]\\label{ex:i06-worked-03}
$\operatorname{tr}:M_n\to\mathbb F$ is surjective; its kernel has dimension $n^2-1$.
\\end{example}

\\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-01}
Find the kernel of $T(x,y,z)=(x+y,y+z)$.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
Solve $x+y=0$ and $y+z=0$: $(x,y,z)=t(-1,1,-1)$, so the kernel is one-dimensional.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-02}
Find the rank of the preceding map.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
The domain has dimension $3$ and the kernel dimension is $1$, so rank–nullity gives rank $2$.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-03}
Prove that zero kernel is equivalent to injectivity.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
If $T(v)=T(w)$ then $T(v-w)=0$. Zero kernel forces $v=w$; the converse follows by comparing $T(v)=T(0)$.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-04}
Why does surjectivity imply rank equals codomain dimension?
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
The image equals the codomain, so their dimensions are equal.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-05}
Show $\dim(V/U)=\dim V-\dim U$ in finite dimensions.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
Extend a basis of $U$ to a basis of $V$; the cosets of the added basis vectors form a basis of the quotient.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-06}
Compute the nullity of the trace map on $M_3(\mathbb R)$.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
The domain has dimension $9$, the trace map has rank $1$, so nullity is $8$.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-07}
For an endomorphism of an $n$-dimensional space, show injective implies surjective.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
Injectivity gives nullity $0$, so rank–nullity gives rank $n$, which equals the codomain dimension.
\\end{solution}

\\begin{exercise}\\label{exr:i06-08}
Explain geometrically what quotienting $\mathbb R^3$ by the $z$-axis does.
\\end{exercise}
\\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\\end{hint}
\\begin{solution}
Vectors differing by a vertical displacement are identified; the quotient is two-dimensional and can be identified with the $xy$-plane.
\\end{solution}

\\section*{Chapter summary}
Kernel and image measure exactly how a linear transformation fails to be injective or surjective. The rank–nullity theorem balances those two defects and turns structural questions into dimension counts. Quotient spaces then package the first isomorphism theorem in its natural form.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
