\chapter{Linear Transformations}
\label{ch:i05-linear-transformations}

Linear transformations are the morphisms of vector spaces: they preserve exactly the operations that define linear structure. Once linearity is recognized, a map is determined by its action on a basis, compositions remain linear, and apparently different formulas become instances of the same operator calculus.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item explain and use definition and first consequences in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use determination by a basis in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use algebra of operators in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use composition in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use inverse maps in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use important operators in basis-free and coordinate form;
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Definition and first consequences}
A map $T:V\to W$ is linear if $T(av+bw)=aT(v)+bT(w)$. Then $T(0)=0$ and all finite linear combinations pass through $T$.

\section{Determination by a basis}
A linear map is uniquely determined by its values on a basis of the domain. Conversely, arbitrary images of basis vectors extend uniquely to a linear map.

\section{Algebra of operators}
For fixed $V,W$, the set $\mathcal L(V,W)$ is a vector space under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication. Endomorphisms $\mathcal L(V)$ also compose.

\section{Composition}
The composite of linear maps is linear. Composition is associative and distributes over addition but is generally not commutative.

\section{Inverse maps}
A bijective linear map has a linear inverse. Such maps are isomorphisms and identify vector spaces as having the same linear structure.

\section{Important operators}
Differentiation on polynomials, evaluation functionals, coordinate projections, inclusion maps, shifts, and matrix multiplication are canonical examples.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Extension from a basis]\label{thm:i05-01}
If $B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ is a basis of $V$ and $w_i\in W$ are arbitrary, there is a unique linear $T:V\to W$ with $T(v_i)=w_i$.
\begin{proof}
Write each $v$ uniquely as $\sum a_i v_i$ and define $T(v)=\sum a_iw_i$. Uniqueness follows because any linear map must use this formula.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Inverse of an isomorphism]\label{thm:i05-02}
If $T:V\to W$ is linear and bijective, then $T^{-1}:W\to V$ is linear.
\begin{proof}
Write $y_j=T(x_j)$. Then $T^{-1}(ay_1+by_2)=T^{-1}(T(ax_1+bx_2))=ax_1+bx_2$.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Differentiation]\label{ex:i05-worked-01}
$D:\mathcal P_n\to\mathcal P_{n-1}$, $D(p)=p'$, is linear because differentiation respects sums and scalar multiples.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Evaluation]\label{ex:i05-worked-02}
For fixed $a$, $E_a(p)=p(a)$ is a linear functional on a polynomial space.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Shear]\label{ex:i05-worked-03}
$T(x,y)=(x+y,y)$ is linear and invertible, with inverse $(u,v)\mapsto(u-v,v)$.
\end{example}

\section{Diagnostic checklist}
Before moving on, it is useful to distinguish four levels of understanding:
\begin{enumerate}
\item recognize the definitions in unfamiliar examples;
\item prove the core structural statements without coordinates when possible;
\item translate the same statement into a matrix or coordinate computation;
\item know which hypotheses are essential and produce a counterexample when one is removed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-01}
Show that $T(x,y)=(x+2y,3x-y)$ is linear.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Each output coordinate is a homogeneous linear expression in $x,y$, so direct substitution verifies preservation of linear combinations.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-02}
Is $T(x,y)=(x+1,y)$ linear?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
No. A linear map must send $0$ to $0$, but this map sends $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-03}
Construct the unique linear map with $T(e_1)=(1,1)$ and $T(e_2)=(2,0)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
For $(x,y)=xe_1+ye_2$, $T(x,y)=x(1,1)+y(2,0)=(x+2y,x)$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-04}
Show that composition of linear maps is linear.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Apply the inner map to a linear combination, then use linearity of the outer map.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-05}
Prove that two linear maps agreeing on a basis agree everywhere.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Every vector is a linear combination of basis vectors, and both maps distribute over that combination.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-06}
Give a nonzero nilpotent operator on $\mathbb R^2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Take $N(x,y)=(y,0)$. Then $N\ne0$ but $N^2=0$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-07}
Show that $\mathcal L(V,W)$ is a vector space.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Verify the vector-space axioms pointwise; the zero vector is the zero map.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i05-08}
Explain why a bijective nonlinear map need not be an isomorphism of vector spaces.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
An isomorphism must preserve addition and scalar multiplication; bijectivity alone preserves only set structure.
\end{solution}

\section*{Chapter summary}
Linear transformations are the morphisms of vector spaces: they preserve exactly the operations that define linear structure. Once linearity is recognized, a map is determined by its action on a basis, compositions remain linear, and apparently different formulas become instances of the same operator calculus.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
