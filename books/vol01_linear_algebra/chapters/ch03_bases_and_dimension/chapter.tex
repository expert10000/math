\chapter{Bases and Dimension}
\label{ch:i03-bases-and-dimension}

A basis is simultaneously a generating system and a coordinate system with no redundancy. Finite-dimensional vector spaces become computationally manageable because every vector then has a unique finite coordinate list. Dimension records the size of any basis and is independent of the particular basis chosen.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item explain and use bases in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use coordinates relative to a basis in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use extension and reduction in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use dimension in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use dimension of subspaces in basis-free and coordinate form;
\item explain and use dimension formula in basis-free and coordinate form;
\end{itemize}

\section*{Conceptual roadmap}
The chapter is organized around a repeated pattern:
\[
\boxed{\text{definition}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{structural theorem}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{coordinate calculation}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{application}.}
\]
Whenever possible, we separate the invariant mathematical object from a chosen coordinate representation.

\section{Bases}
A basis of $V$ is a linearly independent list that spans $V$. The two conditions guarantee existence and uniqueness of coordinates.

\section{Coordinates relative to a basis}
If $B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ is a basis, every $v$ has a unique representation $v=\sum a_i v_i$. The coordinate column is written $[v]_B$.

\section{Extension and reduction}
An independent list in a finite-dimensional space can be extended to a basis, while a spanning list can be reduced to a basis by deleting redundant vectors.

\section{Dimension}
All bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same number of vectors. That common number is $\dim V$.

\section{Dimension of subspaces}
If $W\subseteq V$ and $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\dim W\le\dim V$, with equality only when $W=V$.

\section{Dimension formula}
For finite-dimensional subspaces $U,W\subseteq V$, $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$.

\section{Core structural results}

\begin{theorem}[Basis uniqueness of coordinates]\label{thm:i03-01}
A list is a basis if and only if every vector has a unique representation as a linear combination of the list.
\begin{proof}
Spanning gives existence. If two representations exist, subtract them to obtain a dependence relation; independence forces equal coefficients.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Dimension formula for sums]\label{thm:i03-02}
For finite-dimensional $U,W$, one has $\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)$.
\begin{proof}
Choose a basis of $U\cap W$, extend it separately to bases of $U$ and $W$, and check that the union of the extensions spans $U+W$ and is independent.
\end{proof}
\end{theorem}

\section{Worked examples}

\begin{example}[Polynomial dimension]\label{ex:i03-worked-01}
The space $\mathcal P_n$ of polynomials of degree at most $n$ has basis $1,x,\ldots,x^n$ and dimension $n+1$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Symmetric matrices]\label{ex:i03-worked-02}
The real symmetric $n\times n$ matrices have dimension $n(n+1)/2$, one free entry for each diagonal or upper-triangular position.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Trace-zero matrices]\label{ex:i03-worked-03}
The trace-zero subspace of $M_n(\mathbb F)$ has dimension $n^2-1$.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises with complete solutions}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-01}
Show that $(1,0),(1,1)$ is a basis of $\mathbb R^2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The two vectors are not scalar multiples, so they are independent; two independent vectors in a two-dimensional space form a basis.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-02}
Find the coordinates of $(3,2)$ in the basis $((1,0),(1,1))$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Solve $a(1,0)+b(1,1)=(3,2)$. Then $b=2$ and $a=1$, so the coordinate column is $(1,2)^T$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-03}
Find a basis for polynomials in $\mathcal P_3$ satisfying $p(0)=0$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The condition kills the constant term, so $x,x^2,x^3$ is a basis.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-04}
Why can no four vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ be independent?
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Any independent list has length at most the dimension, which is $3$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-05}
If $U\subseteq V$ and $\dim U=\dim V<\infty$, prove $U=V$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A basis of $U$ is an independent list in $V$ of full dimension, hence already a basis of $V$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-06}
Compute the dimension of skew-symmetric $n\times n$ matrices over a field of characteristic not $2$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Diagonal entries are zero and entries below the diagonal are determined by those above it, giving $n(n-1)/2$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-07}
Suppose $\dim U=3$, $\dim W=4$, and $\dim(U\cap W)=2$. Find $\dim(U+W)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
The dimension formula gives $3+4-2=5$.
\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:i03-08}
Explain why every finite spanning set contains a basis.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}
Identify the definition or structural theorem in this chapter that directly controls the question, then reduce the calculation to that statement.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Repeatedly delete any vector lying in the span of the others. The process terminates and preserves span; the remaining list is independent.
\end{solution}

\section*{Chapter summary}
A basis is simultaneously a generating system and a coordinate system with no redundancy. Finite-dimensional vector spaces become computationally manageable because every vector then has a unique finite coordinate list. Dimension records the size of any basis and is independent of the particular basis chosen.
The important habit is to move fluently between invariant statements and concrete coordinates while remembering which conclusions depend on finite dimensionality, the scalar field, or the inner product.
