Volume~I has developed one language in several increasingly structured forms.
A vector space supplies addition and scalar multiplication; bases and dimension
make finite-dimensional structure explicit; linear maps and their matrices
encode transformations; eigenstructure and canonical forms reveal invariant
behavior; inner products introduce length, angle, orthogonality, adjoints, and
projection; Gram--Schmidt and QR turn those geometric ideas into a stable
computational workflow; the spectral theorem diagonalizes the operators most
compatible with the geometry; quadratic forms encode energies and signatures;
and the singular-value decomposition organizes arbitrary finite-dimensional
maps by orthogonal input directions, orthogonal output directions, and their
stretching factors.

\section*{Continuation routes}
The next volumes reuse this linear language rather than start over.

\begin{description}
\item[Volume II --- norms, metrics, and differentiation.]
Norms abstract the size information supplied here by inner products, metrics
turn norms into a language of convergence and continuity, and differentiation
replaces a nonlinear map locally by its best linear approximation. Operator
norms, kernels, images, coordinates, and conditioning all reappear naturally.

\item[Volume III --- Fourier, spectral, and PDE viewpoints.]
Orthogonal expansions generalize finite orthonormal coordinates, Fourier modes
play the role of preferred basis vectors, and spectral methods extend the idea
that a difficult operator may become simple in the right coordinates. The
finite-dimensional spectral theorem and SVD are prototypes for these analytic
viewpoints.

\item[Volume V --- modules and homological algebra.]
Vector spaces are replaced by modules, where bases need not exist and dimension
is no longer the whole story. Kernels, images, quotients, exact sequences, and
canonical constructions survive in more flexible algebraic form and become the
building blocks of homological algebra.

\item[Volume VII --- tangent spaces, metrics, and frames.]
Each point of a smooth space carries a tangent vector space. Bases become local
frames, inner products varying from point to point become metrics, and
orthonormalization, matrices of linear maps, determinants, and quadratic forms
become local tools for geometry.

\item[Volume VIII --- chain complexes and the linear algebra behind homology.]
Boundary maps form sequences of linear transformations with consecutive
compositions equal to zero. Kernels, images, quotients, rank, and change of
basis therefore become the elementary algebra behind cycles, boundaries, and
homology groups, before the same constructions are generalized further.
\end{description}
