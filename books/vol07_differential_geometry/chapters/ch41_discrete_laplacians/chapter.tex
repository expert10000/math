\chapter{Discrete Laplacians}
\label{ch:vii41}

The Laplace--Beltrami operator is the central second-order operator of
Riemannian geometry.  On a triangle mesh it becomes a sparse matrix assembled
from local geometry.  This chapter derives the cotangent finite-element
operator, explains the role of mass matrices, and separates three notions that
are often conflated in software: a stiffness matrix, a metric-dependent
Laplacian, and a graph Laplacian.

\section{From the smooth operator to a weak form}

We keep the convention from VII/40,
\[
\Delta f=\operatorname{div}(\nabla f),
\]
so \(\Delta\) is nonpositive on a closed Riemannian manifold.  For smooth
functions \(f,g\) on a closed surface,
\[
-\int_M g\,\Delta f\,dA
=
\int_M \langle\nabla g,\nabla f\rangle\,dA.
\]
The right-hand side is the Dirichlet bilinear form.  It is symmetric and
nonnegative when \(f=g\), which is why finite elements naturally produce a
positive-semidefinite matrix for \(-\Delta\).

\begin{definition}[Dirichlet energy]\label{def:vii41-dirichlet}
For a scalar function \(f\),
\[
E(f)=\frac12\int_M\|\nabla f\|^2\,dA.
\]
\end{definition}

\section{Piecewise-linear functions on a triangle mesh}

Let \(\mathcal T=(V,E,F)\) be a nondegenerate triangle mesh.  For each vertex
\(i\), let \(\varphi_i\) be the standard hat function: it equals one at vertex
\(i\), zero at all other vertices, and is affine on every incident triangle.
A piecewise-linear function is
\[
f_h=\sum_i f_i\varphi_i.
\]
On each face, \(\nabla\varphi_i\) and hence \(\nabla f_h\) are constant.

\begin{remark}[Intrinsic data suffice]\label{rem:vii41-intrinsic}
The stiffness entries depend only on triangle edge lengths and angles.  The mesh
may be embedded in \(\mathbb R^3\), but the scalar cotangent Laplacian is an
intrinsic operator once the triangulated metric is fixed.
\end{remark}

\section{The stiffness matrix}

\begin{definition}[Finite-element stiffness matrix]\label{def:vii41-stiffness}
Define
\[
K_{ij}=\int_M\langle\nabla\varphi_i,\nabla\varphi_j\rangle\,dA.
\]
Then
\[
f^T K f=\int_M\|\nabla f_h\|^2\,dA\ge0.
\]
\end{definition}

Thus \(K\) is symmetric positive semidefinite.  Because
\(\sum_i\varphi_i=1\), one has
\[
K\mathbf 1=0.
\]
On a connected mesh this is the expected constant null mode, subject to the
usual boundary-condition qualifications.

\section{Cotangent weights}

Consider an interior edge \(ij\) shared by two triangles.  Let \(\alpha_{ij}\)
and \(\beta_{ij}\) be the angles opposite that edge.  Define
\[
w_{ij}=\frac12\bigl(\cot\alpha_{ij}+\cot\beta_{ij}\bigr).
\]
For a boundary edge there is only one opposite angle and the corresponding
weight is \(\tfrac12\cot\alpha_{ij}\).

\begin{theorem}[Cotangent stiffness formula]\label{thm:vii41-cotan}
For \(i\ne j\),
\[
K_{ij}=-w_{ij}
\]
when \(ij\) is an edge and \(K_{ij}=0\) otherwise.  The diagonal is
\[
K_{ii}=\sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij}.
\]
Consequently every row sums to zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On one triangle, the gradients of barycentric basis functions are constant.
Their inner products can be written in terms of the angle opposite the
corresponding edge, giving a local off-diagonal contribution
\(-\tfrac12\cot\theta\).  Summing the one or two incident face contributions
produces \(-w_{ij}\); the identity \(\sum_jK_{ij}=0\) then gives the diagonal.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Edge-energy form]\label{prop:vii41-edge-energy}
The Dirichlet energy can be written
\[
\frac12 f^T Kf
=
\frac12\sum_{ij\in E} w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2,
\]
with each unoriented edge counted once.
\end{proposition}

\section{Why a mass matrix is needed}

The stiffness matrix represents the bilinear form for \(-\Delta\), not yet a
pointwise operator.  The \(L^2\) inner product of finite-element functions is
represented by the mass matrix
\[
M_{ij}=\int_M\varphi_i\varphi_j\,dA.
\]
For a triangle of area \(A\), its consistent local mass matrix is
\[
M_T=\frac{A}{12}
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1&1\\
1&2&1\\
1&1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

\begin{definition}[Lumped barycentric mass]\label{def:vii41-lumped}
A common diagonal approximation assigns
\[
M_{ii}^{\mathrm{lump}}
=
\frac13\sum_{T\ni i}\operatorname{Area}(T).
\]
\end{definition}

Diagonal masses make application of \(M^{-1}\) cheap.  Consistent masses more
faithfully represent the finite-element \(L^2\) product.  Mixed or Voronoi
areas are also common in geometry processing; the choice must be stated because
it changes the discrete pointwise operator and the generalized spectrum.

\section{Three related matrices and a sign convention}

Under the convention of this volume, \(K\) discretizes \(-\Delta\) weakly.  A
pointwise-like positive Laplacian is
\[
L_+=M^{-1}K,
\]
whereas the approximation to the smooth nonpositive operator is
\[
\Delta_h=-M^{-1}K.
\]
The generalized formulation
\[
Kf=Mg
\]
is often preferable to explicitly forming \(M^{-1}K\).

\begin{warning}[Library sign conventions]\label{warn:vii41-sign}
Many graphics libraries call either \(K\), \(M^{-1}K\), or its negative simply
``the Laplacian.''  Before combining formulas, determine whether eigenvalues are
expected to be nonnegative or nonpositive and whether the mass matrix has
already been absorbed.
\end{warning}

\section{Harmonic functions and Poisson equations}

A discrete harmonic function satisfies the interior equation
\[
(Kf)_i=0.
\]
Equivalently, when the relevant weights are positive,
\[
f_i=
\frac{\sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij}f_j}{\sum_{j\sim i}w_{ij}}.
\]
A finite-element Poisson problem takes the weak form
\[
Kf=b,
\]
with boundary conditions or a gauge condition supplying uniqueness.

\begin{proposition}[Variational characterization]\label{prop:vii41-variational}
With Dirichlet values fixed on the boundary, the discrete harmonic extension is
the unique minimizer of the discrete Dirichlet energy among all functions with
those boundary values, provided the free system has no remaining null mode.
\end{proposition}

\section{Boundary conditions}

Dirichlet conditions are imposed by fixing boundary degrees of freedom and
solving for the interior unknowns.  Natural finite-element boundary conditions
are Neumann-type: boundary flux enters through the weak-form boundary term.
On a closed connected mesh, the pure Poisson equation requires compatibility
with the constant null space; for example, a load must have zero total integral
in the consistent weak sense.

\section{Delaunay geometry and weight signs}

For an interior edge,
\[
\cot\alpha+\cot\beta
=
\frac{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}{\sin\alpha\sin\beta}.
\]
Hence an intrinsic Delaunay edge, characterized by
\(\alpha+\beta\le\pi\), has \(w_{ij}\ge0\).

\begin{theorem}[Delaunay positivity of cotangent edge weights]\label{thm:vii41-delaunay}
If every interior edge is intrinsically Delaunay, then all interior cotangent
weights are nonnegative.
\end{theorem}

Positive off-diagonal weights support a graph-like averaging interpretation and
are important for discrete maximum-principle behavior.  On a non-Delaunay mesh,
individual cotangent weights may be negative even though the assembled
finite-element stiffness matrix still represents a nonnegative Dirichlet energy.

\begin{warning}[Maximum principle is not automatic]\label{warn:vii41-max}
Positive semidefiniteness of \(K\) does not by itself imply that every interior
harmonic value is a convex combination of its neighbors.  That stronger local
property depends on the weight signs and boundary configuration.
\end{warning}

\section{Intrinsic Delaunay triangulations}

Because the cotangent operator depends on intrinsic angles, one can flip edges
intrinsically until the Delaunay condition is satisfied, without changing the
underlying piecewise-flat metric.  This can improve weight signs and numerical
behavior while preserving intrinsic distances.  Degenerate cocircular cases
may admit more than one Delaunay connectivity but produce compatible intrinsic
geometry.

\section{The generalized spectrum}

For a closed mesh, solve
\[
K\phi_k=\lambda_k M\phi_k.
\]
With positive-definite \(M\),
\[
0=\lambda_0\le\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots.
\]
The constant function spans the first eigenspace on a connected mesh.
The eigenvectors can be chosen \(M\)-orthonormal:
\[
\phi_i^T M\phi_j=\delta_{ij}.
\]
These modes drive spectral filtering, diffusion, shape signatures, and many
model-reduction techniques.

\section{Connection with the heat method}

VII/40 solved
\[
(M+tK)u=M\delta_s
\]
under the present positive-stiffness convention, followed by a Poisson solve
with \(K\).  The generalized eigenmode \(\phi_k\) is damped by
\[
\frac{1}{1+t\lambda_k}.
\]
Thus the mass and stiffness choices made here directly control heat-method
accuracy.

\section{The Laplacian of an embedding}

Let \(X=(x,y,z)\) collect the vertex coordinates of a triangulated surface.
Applying the operator coordinatewise gives a discrete mean-curvature-normal
quantity.  With the smooth convention
\[
\Delta X=-2H\mathbf n,
\]
one expects
\[
M^{-1}KX\approx 2H\mathbf n,
\]
up to discretization, mass choice, orientation, and the convention for scalar
mean curvature.

\begin{remark}[Curvature is more delicate than scalar diffusion]\label{rem:vii41-curvature}
The cotangent Laplacian is robust for many scalar PDEs, but pointwise curvature
estimates amplify mesh noise and irregularity.  VII/42 therefore combines
principal-direction geometry with explicit estimation and scale controls rather
than treating a single raw Laplacian value as a complete feature detector.
\end{remark}

\section{Assembly and numerical diagnostics}

A production implementation should assemble face contributions locally, sum
into sparse global matrices, verify symmetry, check row sums of \(K\), check
positive vertex masses, and test the constant null mode.  Meshes with zero-area
faces or nearly degenerate angles require preprocessing because cotangents can
become extremely large.

Under a uniform geometric scaling by \(c\), triangle areas and \(M\) scale by
\(c^2\), while cotangent weights and \(K\) are dimensionless.  Therefore
\(M^{-1}K\) scales by \(c^{-2}\), exactly like a second-order differential
operator.

\section{Solved problem dossiers}

\begin{problem}[Single triangle cotangent sign]\label{prob:vii41-01}
A triangle has an angle \(120^\circ\) opposite edge \(ij\).  What sign does that face's contribution \(\tfrac12\cot120^\circ\) have, and what does this illustrate?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Since \(\cot120^\circ=-1/\sqrt3\), the contribution is negative.  Obtuse geometry can therefore create negative cotangent contributions; weight positivity is not automatic on an arbitrary triangulation.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Row-sum diagnostic]\label{prob:vii41-02}
A code assembles a closed-mesh stiffness matrix but obtains \(\|K\mathbf1\|\) comparable to ordinary matrix entries.  What does this indicate?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
It indicates an assembly or orientation/indexing error, or a degenerate/preprocessing issue.  The finite-element partition of unity requires exact zero row sums in exact arithmetic.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Two-triangle Delaunay test]\label{prob:vii41-03}
An interior edge has opposite angles \(70^\circ\) and \(80^\circ\).  Determine the sign of its cotangent weight without evaluating either cotangent numerically.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Their sum is \(150^\circ<180^\circ\).  Since \(\cot\alpha+\cot\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta)/(\sin\alpha\sin\beta)\) and the sines in the denominator are positive, the weight is positive.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Non-Delaunay test]\label{prob:vii41-04}
Opposite angles are \(100^\circ\) and \(100^\circ\).  What does the intrinsic Delaunay criterion say about the edge weight?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The angle sum is \(200^\circ>180^\circ\), so the edge is non-Delaunay and \(\sin(200^\circ)<0\); hence the cotangent sum and the edge weight are negative.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Mass of a single triangle]\label{prob:vii41-05}
For a single triangle of area \(A\), what total mass is obtained by summing all entries of the consistent local mass matrix?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The three diagonal entries contribute \(6\) and the six off-diagonal entries contribute \(6\), so the coefficient matrix has total sum \(12\).  Multiplying by \(A/12\) gives total mass \(A\), matching \(\int_T 1\,dA\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Lumped versus consistent]\label{prob:vii41-06}
Why can replacing a consistent mass matrix by a lumped diagonal mass change eigenvalues even though both represent area?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
They define different discrete \(L^2\) inner products.  The generalized Rayleigh quotient \(f^TKf/(f^TMf)\) therefore changes, so the discrete spectrum changes even if both masses integrate constants correctly.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Dirichlet minimizer]\label{prob:vii41-07}
A mesh has fixed boundary values.  Explain why solving the interior stiffness equations finds the minimum-energy extension.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The discrete Dirichlet energy is a convex quadratic \(\tfrac12 f^TKf\).  Differentiating with respect to each free interior coefficient yields the corresponding equation \((Kf)_i=0\); with the boundary fixed and no free null mode, this stationary point is the unique minimizer.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Poisson gauge]\label{prob:vii41-08}
On a closed connected mesh, \(Kf=b\) has a solution and \(f\) is one solution.  Describe all solutions.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Because the null space is the constants, every \(f+c\mathbf1\) is also a solution.  Under the standard one-dimensional null-space assumption these are all solutions.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Heat-system positivity]\label{prob:vii41-09}
Why is \(M+tK\) nonsingular for \(t>0\) when \(M\) is positive definite and \(K\) positive semidefinite?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
For nonzero \(v\), \(v^T(M+tK)v=v^TMv+t\,v^TKv>0\).  Hence the matrix is symmetric positive definite and invertible.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Scaling a mesh]\label{prob:vii41-10}
A mesh is uniformly enlarged by factor \(5\).  How should nonzero generalized eigenvalues \(\lambda\) of \(K\phi=\lambda M\phi\) scale?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The stiffness matrix is unchanged while the mass matrix scales by \(25\), so eigenvalues scale by \(1/25\), consistent with the inverse-length-squared dimension of the Laplacian.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Embedding Laplacian on a sphere]\label{prob:vii41-11}
For a well-resolved unit sphere with outward normal and \(\Delta X=-2H\mathbf n\), what vector should \(M^{-1}KX\) approximate?
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
For the unit sphere \(H=1\), so \(M^{-1}KX\approx2\mathbf n\).  The sign follows because \(K\) approximates \(-\Delta\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Production regression]\label{prob:vii41-12}
Give four cheap algebraic checks that should accompany cotangent-matrix assembly before it is trusted by a heat-method solver.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Check symmetry of \(K\), near-zero row sums, positive diagonal masses, and nonnegative quadratic energies \(v^TKv\) on test vectors.  Also check the expected number of constant null modes against mesh connectivity.
\end{solution}


\section{Challenge problems}

\begin{problem}[Derive the Delaunay sign identity]\label{prob:vii41-ch01}
Prove \(\cot\alpha+\cot\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta)/(\sin\alpha\sin\beta)\), and use it to characterize the sign of an interior cotangent weight for \(0<\alpha,\beta<\pi\).
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Using \(\cot\theta=\cos\theta/\sin\theta\), the numerator becomes \(\cos\alpha\sin\beta+\sin\alpha\cos\beta=\sin(\alpha+\beta)\).  The denominator is positive.  Thus the weight is positive, zero, or negative according as \(\alpha+\beta\) is less than, equal to, or greater than \(\pi\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Rayleigh quotient]\label{prob:vii41-ch02}
Show that generalized eigenvalues of \(K\phi=\lambda M\phi\) are stationary values of \(R(f)=f^TKf/(f^TMf)\), and explain why they are nonnegative.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Differentiate the quotient subject to the normalization \(f^TMf=1\).  A Lagrange multiplier gives \(Kf=\lambda Mf\), with \(\lambda=f^TKf\).  Since \(K\) is positive semidefinite and \(M\) positive definite, \(R(f)\ge0\) for every nonzero \(f\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Discrete maximum principle]\label{prob:vii41-ch03}
Assume every free interior vertex has strictly positive incident weights.  Prove that a nonconstant discrete harmonic function cannot attain a strict maximum at a free interior vertex.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
At a harmonic vertex, \(f_i\) is a positive weighted average of neighboring values.  If it were a strict maximum, every neighbor would be smaller and the weighted average would be strictly smaller than \(f_i\), a contradiction.  Equality forces all positively weighted neighbors to share the same value and propagation handles connected regions.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Intrinsic flip thought experiment]\label{prob:vii41-ch04}
Two triangles form a convex intrinsic quadrilateral whose shared diagonal is non-Delaunay.  Explain why flipping to the other diagonal can improve local weight signs without changing the intrinsic metric of the quadrilateral.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
The quadrilateral is built from the same four boundary edge lengths and the same flat intrinsic region; the diagonal is only a triangulation choice.  The alternate diagonal satisfies the local Delaunay criterion, making its cotangent weight nonnegative, while geodesic distances intrinsic to the unchanged piecewise-flat quadrilateral are preserved.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Operator validation suite]\label{prob:vii41-ch05}
Design a compact validation suite for \(M\) and \(K\) that tests geometry, scaling, PDE behavior, and spectrum rather than only matrix dimensions.
\end{problem}

\begin{solution}
Use a planar patch to test linear finite-element consistency; a closed connected mesh to test one constant null mode; uniform rescaling to verify \(K\) invariance, \(M\sim c^2\), and eigenvalues \(\sim c^{-2}\); a Dirichlet harmonic problem to test averaging/maximum behavior on Delaunay meshes; and a heat solve to verify symmetric positive definiteness of \(M+tK\) and convergence under refinement.
\end{solution}
