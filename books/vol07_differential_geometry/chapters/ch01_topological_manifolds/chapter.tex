\chapter{Topological Manifolds}
\label{ch:vii01-topological-manifolds}

Volume VII begins by separating the topological notion of a manifold from the smooth structures added in VII/02.  The basic definition has three independent ingredients:
\[
\boxed{\text{locally Euclidean}\;+\;\text{Hausdorff}\;+\;\text{second countable}.}
\]
The mapped legacy manuscripts emphasize that each condition rules out a different pathology.  A cone apex can fail local Euclidean structure, a plane with two origins can be locally Euclidean but non-Hausdorff, and an uncountable disjoint union of ordinary manifolds can be Hausdorff and locally Euclidean but fail second countability.

\section*{Standing conventions}
All spaces are topological spaces.  By a topological \(n\)-manifold we mean a Hausdorff, second-countable space locally homeomorphic to open subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\).  Smooth compatibility of charts is deliberately postponed to VII/02.

\section*{Learning goals}
By the end of the chapter, the reader should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
\item state and test the three manifold axioms;
\item work with local Euclidean neighborhoods and topological charts;
\item distinguish local failures from separation and countability failures;
\item prove that open subsets, products, and disjoint unions under suitable countability hypotheses are manifolds;
\item recognize spheres, tori, projective spaces, and quotient surfaces as manifolds;
\item analyze quotient constructions such as the torus and Klein bottle;
\item use local invariants such as punctured-neighborhood connectivity to detect nonmanifold points;
\item explain why second countability implies useful global regularity such as a countable atlas;
\item prepare for smooth atlases without using differentiability prematurely.
\end{itemize}

\section{Locally Euclidean spaces}
\begin{definition}[Locally Euclidean space]\label{def:vii01-locally-euclidean}
A space \(X\) is \emph{locally Euclidean of dimension \(n\)} if every point \(p\in X\) has an open neighborhood \(U\) homeomorphic to an open subset \(V\subseteq\mathbb R^n\).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Topological chart]\label{def:vii01-chart}
A \emph{topological chart} is a pair \((U,\varphi)\), where \(U\subseteq X\) is open and
\[
\varphi:U\xrightarrow{\sim}V\subseteq\mathbb R^n
\]
is a homeomorphism onto an open set.  The functions supplied by \(\varphi\) are local coordinates.  No differentiability is required in this chapter.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Dimension is locally constant]\label{prop:vii01-dimension-local}
If \(X\) is locally Euclidean and connected, all charts have the same Euclidean dimension.  Hence one may speak of the dimension of a connected topological manifold.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
This is the invariance-of-domain/invariance-of-dimension principle: nonempty open subsets of \(\mathbb R^m\) and \(\mathbb R^n\) cannot be homeomorphic when \(m\neq n\).  Overlapping chart domains therefore have the same dimension, and connectedness propagates the equality.
\end{proof}

\section{Hausdorff and second-countable conditions}
\begin{definition}[Hausdorff]\label{def:vii01-hausdorff}
A space \(X\) is \emph{Hausdorff} if distinct points admit disjoint open neighborhoods.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Second countable]\label{def:vii01-second-countable}
A space \(X\) is \emph{second countable} if its topology has a countable basis.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Topological manifold]\label{def:vii01-topological-manifold}
A \emph{topological \(n\)-manifold} is a Hausdorff, second-countable space locally Euclidean of dimension \(n\).
A topological \(2\)-manifold is also called a \emph{topological surface}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Countable atlas]\label{prop:vii01-countable-atlas}
Every second-countable locally Euclidean space admits a countable atlas.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Choose a countable basis \(\mathcal B\).  For every point choose a chart and then a basis element contained in its chart domain.  Only countably many basis elements are needed, and restricting the corresponding charts yields a countable cover by chart domains.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why the hypotheses are separate]\label{rem:vii01-three-axioms}
Local Euclidean structure is a local condition.  Hausdorffness controls global separation.  Second countability rules out spaces that are locally ordinary but globally too large.  None of these roles should be hidden inside the others.
\end{remark}

\section{Three diagnostic counterexamples}
\begin{example}[The double cone]\label{ex:vii01-cone}
Let
\[
C=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3:x^2+y^2=z^2\}.
\]
Away from the apex, \(C\) is locally a surface.  At the apex, a sufficiently small punctured neighborhood has two connected components, whereas a punctured open disc in \(\mathbb R^2\) is connected.  Hence the apex has no surface chart.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The plane with two origins]\label{ex:vii01-two-origins}
Take two copies of \(\mathbb R^2\) and identify corresponding nonzero points, leaving the two origins distinct.  Every point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to \(\mathbb R^2\), but the two origins cannot be separated by disjoint neighborhoods.  Thus local Euclidean structure does not imply Hausdorffness.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Too many components]\label{ex:vii01-uncountable-components}
If \(\Sigma\) is a surface and \(\mathbb R_\delta\) is the real line with the discrete topology, then
\[
\Sigma\times\mathbb R_\delta
\]
is an uncountable disjoint union of copies of \(\Sigma\).  It is Hausdorff and locally Euclidean of dimension two, but not second countable.
\end{example}

\section{Basic constructions and examples}
\begin{proposition}[Open subsets]\label{prop:vii01-open-submanifold}
Every open subset of a topological \(n\)-manifold is a topological \(n\)-manifold.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Hausdorffness and second countability pass to open subspaces, and restricting charts gives the local Euclidean property.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Products]\label{prop:vii01-products}
If \(M\) is an \(m\)-manifold and \(N\) an \(n\)-manifold, then \(M\times N\) is an \((m+n)\)-manifold.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Products of Hausdorff spaces are Hausdorff, products of countable bases are countable bases, and products of charts give homeomorphisms to open subsets of \(\mathbb R^{m+n}\).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Spheres]\label{ex:vii01-sphere}
The sphere \(S^n\subset\mathbb R^{n+1}\) is an \(n\)-manifold.  One may use stereographic projection from the north and south poles as two charts.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The torus]\label{ex:vii01-torus}
The torus can be represented as \(S^1\times S^1\) or as the quotient
\[
T^2=\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2.
\]
The product description immediately shows that it is a topological surface.
\end{example}

\section{Quotients and identifications}
\begin{definition}[Quotient topology]\label{def:vii01-quotient}
If \(Y\) is a topological space and \(\sim\) an equivalence relation, the quotient \(Y/{\sim}\) has the topology in which \(U\subseteq Y/{\sim}\) is open exactly when its inverse image under the quotient map is open in \(Y\).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Free properly discontinuous quotients]\label{prop:vii01-proper-discontinuous}
If a discrete group \(G\) acts freely and properly discontinuously by homeomorphisms on an \(n\)-manifold \(M\), and the quotient is second countable, then \(M/G\) is an \(n\)-manifold and \(M\to M/G\) is a local homeomorphism.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Proper discontinuity gives, near each \(p\), an open set whose distinct translates do not meet.  The quotient map is therefore a homeomorphism from that neighborhood onto its image.  The remaining manifold axioms descend under the stated hypotheses.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Torus and Klein bottle]\label{ex:vii01-identification-surfaces}
Identifying opposite sides of a square with parallel orientations produces \(T^2\); reversing one pair produces the Klein bottle \(K\).  Both are topological surfaces.  Their quotient-square descriptions are the first important examples where global topology is encoded by edge identifications.
\end{example}

\section{Covering maps}
\begin{definition}[Covering map]\label{def:vii01-covering}
A map \(p:E\to B\) is a \emph{covering map} if every \(b\in B\) has an open neighborhood \(U\) whose inverse image is a disjoint union of opens, each mapped homeomorphically onto \(U\).
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[A double cover of the torus]\label{ex:vii01-torus-double-cover}
Writing \(T^2=S^1\times S^1\),
\[
p(z,w)=(z^2,w)
\]
is a two-sheeted covering \(T^2\to T^2\).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Torus covering the Klein bottle]\label{ex:vii01-torus-klein-cover}
The translation lattice of the torus can be realized as an index-two subgroup of the standard deck group defining the Klein bottle.  The induced quotient map \(T^2\to K\) is therefore a two-sheeted covering.
\end{example}

\section{Exercises}
\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-01}
Verify that \(\mathbb R^n\) is an \(n\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use the identity chart and the standard countable basis.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Euclidean space is Hausdorff, has the countable basis of rational balls, and the identity gives a global chart.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-02}
Show that every open subset of \(\mathbb R^n\) is an \(n\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Restrict the Euclidean basis.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Hausdorffness and second countability are inherited, and the inclusion coordinates are local Euclidean charts.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-03}
Show that \(S^1\) is a \(1\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use two stereographic or angle charts.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Deleting one point from the circle gives an open arc homeomorphic to \(\mathbb R\); two such opens cover \(S^1\).\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-04}
Show that \(S^n\) is an \(n\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use stereographic projection.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}North- and south-pole stereographic projections give two charts onto \(\mathbb R^n\); the sphere is Hausdorff and second countable as a subspace of Euclidean space.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-05}
Prove that the product of an \(m\)-manifold and an \(n\)-manifold is an \((m+n)\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Take products of charts and bases.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Chart products map to open subsets of \(\mathbb R^m	imes\mathbb R^n\cong\mathbb R^{m+n}\); the separation and countability axioms are preserved.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-06}
Explain why the double cone fails to be a surface at its apex.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Remove the apex from a small neighborhood.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}The punctured cone neighborhood has two components, whereas a sufficiently small punctured planar disc is connected, so no local homeomorphism exists.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-07}
Prove that the plane with two origins is locally Euclidean.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use either original plane copy near each origin.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Each origin has a neighborhood represented by one copy of a Euclidean disc, while every nonzero point has the usual Euclidean neighborhood.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-08}
Prove that the plane with two origins is not Hausdorff.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Try to separate the two origins.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Any neighborhoods of the two origins contain common nonzero points after quotienting, so they cannot be disjoint.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-09}
Show that an uncountable discrete space is not second countable.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Each singleton is open.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}A basis must contain, for each point, a basis element contained in its singleton; hence it must contain all singletons and is uncountable.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-10}
Show that \(\Sigma	imes\mathbb R_\delta\) is not second countable.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Each slice is open and the slices are disjoint.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}A countable basis cannot meet an uncountable family of pairwise disjoint nonempty open components with distinct basis elements.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-11}
Show that a second-countable space is separable.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Choose one point from each nonempty basis element.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}The chosen countable set meets every nonempty open set, hence is dense.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-12}
Construct a countable atlas from a countable basis.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Refine chart neighborhoods by basis elements.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}For each needed basis element choose one chart containing it and restrict; only countably many domains occur.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-13}
Show that a countable disjoint union of \(n\)-manifolds is an \(n\)-manifold.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Combine countable bases and atlases.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}The union is Hausdorff, a countable union of countable bases is countable, and each point retains its original chart.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-14}
Explain why an uncountable disjoint union of nonempty \(n\)-manifolds is not a manifold under the adopted definition.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use pairwise disjoint open components.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Second countability fails because each open component needs a distinct basis element.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-15}
Verify that \(T^2=S^1	imes S^1\) is a surface.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Apply the product theorem.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}The product of two \(1\)-manifolds is a \(2\)-manifold.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-16}
Show that \(\mathbb{RP}^n\) is locally Euclidean.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use the standard projective coordinate opens.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}On the open where \(x_i
eq0\), normalize \(x_i=1\); the remaining \(n\) coordinates give a homeomorphism to \(\mathbb R^n\).\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-17}
Check that the quotient map \(\mathbb R^2	o\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2\) is a local homeomorphism.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use a disc smaller than half the lattice spacing.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Such a disc has disjoint integer translates, so quotienting is injective and open on it.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-18}
Verify that \(p(z,w)=(z^2,w)\) has two points in every fiber.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Solve \(z^2=u\) on \(S^1\).\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Each \(u\in S^1\) has exactly two square roots \(z,-z\), while \(w\) is fixed.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-19}
Prove that a covering map is a local homeomorphism.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Read the definition on an evenly covered neighborhood.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Each sheet over an evenly covered neighborhood is mapped homeomorphically onto the base open.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-20}
Show that a local homeomorphism preserves local manifold dimension.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Compose a chart with the local inverse.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}A chart on the source transports across the local homeomorphism to a chart of the same Euclidean dimension on the target.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-21}
Give a topological reason the cone with its apex removed is a surface.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use the two graph descriptions \(z=\pm\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Every remaining point belongs to one smooth graph sheet and has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open planar disc.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-22}
Explain why Hausdorffness is not a local-Euclidean consequence.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use the two-origin example.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}The plane with two origins is locally Euclidean everywhere but fails to separate its two origins.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-23}
Explain why second countability is not a local-Euclidean consequence.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Use the uncountable disjoint union example.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}Each component is locally Euclidean, but the uncountable family of open components forces every basis to be uncountable.\end{solution}

\begin{exercise}\label{exr:vii01-24}
State exactly what new condition VII/02 will add to the present charts.
\end{exercise}
\begin{hint}Do not change the topological manifold definition.\end{hint}
\begin{solution}VII/02 imposes smooth compatibility on chart transition maps and organizes charts into smooth atlases; no such differentiability condition is part of VII/01.\end{solution}

\section{Solved problem dossiers}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 1(a): the cone]\label{prob:vii01-01}
Prove that
\[
C=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3:x^2+y^2=z^2\}
\]
is not a topological surface.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
All non-apex points lie on one of the two graph sheets and are locally Euclidean.  Let \(o\) be the apex.  A sufficiently small punctured neighborhood of \(o\) in \(C\) has exactly two connected components, the upper and lower cones.  A sufficiently small punctured disc in \(\mathbb R^2\) is connected.  A homeomorphism would preserve connected components, so no neighborhood of \(o\) can be homeomorphic to an open subset of \(\mathbb R^2\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 1(b): plane with two origins]\label{prob:vii01-02}
Form a quotient of two copies of \(\mathbb R^2\) by identifying corresponding nonzero points and leaving the origins distinct.  Show that every point is locally Euclidean but the space is not a surface.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Each origin has a neighborhood coming from its own copy of a Euclidean disc, and nonzero points have their ordinary disc neighborhoods, so the quotient is locally Euclidean.  However any neighborhood of the first origin and any neighborhood of the second contain identified nonzero points.  Hence the two origins cannot be separated by disjoint opens.  The quotient is non-Hausdorff and therefore is not a topological manifold under the standing definition.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 1(c): uncountably many surface components]\label{prob:vii01-03}
Let \(\Sigma\) be a surface and \(\mathbb R_\delta\) the real line with the discrete topology.  Show that \(\Sigma\times\mathbb R_\delta\) is locally a surface but is not a topological surface.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Each point lies in \(U\times\{t\}\), where \(U\) is a surface chart and \(\{t\}\) is open, so the product is locally Euclidean of dimension two.  It is an uncountable disjoint union of nonempty open copies of \(\Sigma\).  In a second-countable space, every family of pairwise disjoint nonempty opens is countable: assign to each one a distinct basis element contained in it.  Thus second countability fails.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 2(a): a double cover of the torus]\label{prob:vii01-04}
Construct a two-sheeted covering \(p:T^2\to T^2\).
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Use \(T^2=S^1\times S^1\) and define
\[
p(z,w)=(z^2,w).
\]
Every \((u,v)\) has exactly the two preimages \((z,v)\) and \((-z,v)\).  On a sufficiently short arc \(A\subset S^1\), the squaring map is a homeomorphism onto its image; hence on \(A\times B\) the product map is a homeomorphism.  Therefore \(p\) is a two-sheeted covering.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 2(b): torus to Klein bottle]\label{prob:vii01-05}
Construct a two-sheeted covering \(T^2\to K\) of the Klein bottle.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Write the torus as \(\mathbb R^2/\Lambda\) for the translation lattice and the Klein bottle as \(\mathbb R^2/\Gamma\), where \(\Gamma\) is generated by a horizontal translation and a glide/reflection generator.  The translation subgroup defining the torus has index two in \(\Gamma\).  The induced quotient
\[
\mathbb R^2/\Lambda\longrightarrow\mathbb R^2/\Gamma
\]
is therefore a two-sheeted covering and local homeomorphism.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Legacy Problem 2(c): nonconjugate involutions of \(T^2\)]\label{prob:vii01-06}
Give three pairwise nonconjugate subgroups \(\mathbb Z/2\le\operatorname{Homeo}(T^2)\).
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
In coordinates \([x,y]\in\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2\), take
\[
\tau[x,y]=[x+\tfrac12,y],\qquad
\rho[x,y]=[-x,y],\qquad
\sigma[x,y]=[-x,-y].
\]
Their fixed sets are respectively empty, two circles, and four isolated points.  Conjugate homeomorphisms have homeomorphic fixed-point sets, so the three generated order-two subgroups are pairwise nonconjugate.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Local dimension test]\label{prob:vii01-07}
Why can a connected space not be simultaneously a topological \(m\)-manifold and a topological \(n\)-manifold with \(m\neq n\)?
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Choose overlapping charts of the two alleged dimensions.  Restrict to a nonempty overlap; composing one chart with the inverse of the other would produce a homeomorphism between nonempty open subsets of \(\mathbb R^m\) and \(\mathbb R^n\).  Invariance of dimension forbids this when \(m\neq n\).  Connectedness propagates one dimension throughout the space.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Projective space charts]\label{prob:vii01-08}
Prove that \(\mathbb{RP}^n\) is an \(n\)-manifold.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Let \(U_i\) be the set of projective points with \(x_i\neq0\).  Normalize \(x_i=1\); the remaining coordinates give a homeomorphism \(U_i\cong\mathbb R^n\).  The \(U_i\) cover \(\mathbb{RP}^n\).  As a quotient of the compact Hausdorff sphere by the finite antipodal action, \(\mathbb{RP}^n\) is Hausdorff and second countable, completing the manifold axioms.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[A quotient manifold criterion]\label{prob:vii01-09}
Let a discrete group act freely and properly discontinuously on an \(n\)-manifold.  Explain why the quotient is locally Euclidean.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
For each \(p\), proper discontinuity provides an open neighborhood \(U\) whose nontrivial translates are disjoint from \(U\).  The quotient map is injective on \(U\), and because quotient maps are open for group actions, \(U\) maps homeomorphically to its image.  Composing with a chart on \(U\) gives a quotient chart of dimension \(n\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Countability versus components]\label{prob:vii01-10}
Prove that a second-countable space has at most countably many pairwise disjoint nonempty open subsets.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Let \(\mathcal B\) be a countable basis.  For each nonempty open \(U_\alpha\), choose \(x_\alpha\in U_\alpha\) and a basis element \(B_\alpha\) with \(x_\alpha\in B_\alpha\subseteq U_\alpha\).  Pairwise disjointness forces the \(B_\alpha\) to be distinct.  Thus the index set injects into the countable set \(\mathcal B\).
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Why the two-origin quotient is the right Hausdorff counterexample]\label{prob:vii01-11}
Show explicitly that the two-origin quotient has all the desired local charts even though no Hausdorff separation exists.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Around either origin, take a disc in the corresponding source copy small enough to serve as the chosen representative; its quotient image is open and the quotient map restricts to a homeomorphism.  Around any nonzero point, choose a disc avoiding the origin; the two copies have been identified there, producing one ordinary disc chart.  Thus local charts do not detect the failure: only the impossibility of separating the two origins violates the manifold definition.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Boundary of this chapter]\label{prob:vii01-12}
Separate the topological data needed in VII/01 from the smooth-atlas data reserved for VII/02.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
VII/01 needs only open chart domains and homeomorphisms to Euclidean opens, together with Hausdorffness and second countability.  It may discuss overlap homeomorphisms topologically, but does not demand differentiability.  VII/02 adds smooth compatibility of transition maps, maximal smooth atlases, and equivalence of smooth structures.  This boundary prevents the legacy smooth-atlas material from being migrated one chapter too early.
\end{solution}

\section{Challenges}
\begin{problem}[Challenge]\label{chal:vii01-01}
Give a rigorous proof that the long line is locally Euclidean and Hausdorff but not second countable.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Construct the long ray from \(\omega_1\times[0,1)\) with lexicographic order topology and double it to obtain the long line.  Order intervals give local coordinates homeomorphic to ordinary real intervals, and linearly ordered topological spaces are Hausdorff.  An uncountable cofinal family of ordinal stages prevents any countable basis from reaching all local neighborhoods, so second countability fails.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Challenge]\label{chal:vii01-02}
Classify which of the three manifold axioms fail for the cone, plane with two origins, and \(\Sigma\times\mathbb R_\delta\).
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
The cone is Hausdorff and second countable as a Euclidean subspace but fails local Euclidean structure at the apex.  The plane with two origins is locally Euclidean and second countable but fails Hausdorffness.  The product with the uncountable discrete line is locally Euclidean and Hausdorff but fails second countability.  Together the examples show the logical independence and geometric purpose of the three axioms.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Challenge]\label{chal:vii01-03}
Develop a polygon-identification criterion sufficient to produce a compact topological surface.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
Require edge pairings by homeomorphisms with each edge paired exactly once, verify that interior points have disc neighborhoods, paired edge interiors have two half-discs glued to a disc, and analyze each vertex equivalence class to ensure the cyclic collection of sectors glues to a disc rather than a cone or branching point.  The finite quotient is compact and second countable; Hausdorffness follows from the standard closed-equivalence-relation criterion in this finite polygon setting.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Challenge]\label{chal:vii01-04}
Relate finite-sheeted covering maps of manifolds to subgroup inclusions in the torus/Klein-bottle examples without developing the full fundamental-group classification.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
When a common simply connected model carries properly discontinuous deck-group actions, an inclusion \(\Lambda\le\Gamma\) induces \(M/\Lambda\to M/\Gamma\).  If the index is finite, each target fiber has that many cosets and the quotient map is a finite covering.  The torus-to-Klein-bottle map is the index-two case, illustrating the algebraic shadow of covering theory before the later algebraic-topology volume.
\end{solution}

\begin{problem}[Challenge]\label{chal:vii01-05}
Audit a candidate ``manifold'' by designing a decision tree based on local Euclidean structure, Hausdorffness, and second countability.
\end{problem}
\begin{solution}
First test local models: singular/branch points already rule out a manifold.  If all local models pass, test separation of suspicious duplicated or quotient points.  Finally test countability by seeking a countable atlas/basis or an uncountable family of disjoint opens.  The order is efficient because each stage isolates a distinct failure mode and produces a concrete certificate of nonmanifold behavior.
\end{solution}

\section*{Bridge to VII/02}
A topological manifold comes with many possible charts, but so far chart changes are only homeomorphisms.  VII/02 adds the differentiable compatibility condition, defines smooth atlases and maximal smooth structures, and asks when two topological charts belong to the same smooth geometry.
