\section{Flabby Sheaves and Vanishing of \v{C}ech Cohomology}

\subsection{Problem 3}

Let $X$ be a topological space. A sheaf $\mathcal F$ on $X$ is called
\emph{flabby} if for every inclusion of open sets $V \subseteq U$, the
restriction map
\[
\mathcal F(U) \longrightarrow \mathcal F(V)
\]
is surjective.

We prove that if $\mathcal U=\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ is an open covering of $X$
and $\mathcal F$ is flabby, then
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
\qquad \text{for all } p>0.
\]

\subsection*{(a)}

Assume that
\[
0 \longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3
\longrightarrow 0
\]
is an exact sequence of sheaves on $X$, and assume that $\mathcal F_1$ is
flabby. We show that for every open set $U\subseteq X$, the map
\[
\mathcal F_2(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(U)
\]
is surjective.

Let $s\in \mathcal F_3(U)$. Since the sequence is exact as a sequence of
sheaves, locally on $U$ the section $s$ lifts to sections of $\mathcal F_2$.
Thus there exists an open covering $\{U_i\}$ of $U$ and sections
\[
t_i\in \mathcal F_2(U_i)
\]
such that the image of $t_i$ in $\mathcal F_3(U_i)$ is $s|_{U_i}$.

On overlaps $U_i\cap U_j$, we have
\[
t_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}-t_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}
\in \mathcal F_1(U_i\cap U_j).
\]

We now glue the local liftings. Consider the set of pairs $(V,t)$, where
$V\subseteq U$ is open and $t\in \mathcal F_2(V)$ lifts $s|_V$.
Order these pairs by extension:
\[
(V,t)\leq (W,t')
\]
if $V\subseteq W$ and $t'|_V=t$.

By Zorn's Lemma, there exists a maximal such pair $(V,t)$. We claim that
$V=U$.

Suppose not. Choose $x\in U\setminus V$. Since $s$ is locally liftable,
there exists an open neighbourhood $W$ of $x$ and a section
\[
u\in \mathcal F_2(W)
\]
lifting $s|_W$.

On $V\cap W$, both $t$ and $u$ lift the same section of $\mathcal F_3$.
Therefore
\[
t|_{V\cap W}-u|_{V\cap W}\in \mathcal F_1(V\cap W).
\]
Since $\mathcal F_1$ is flabby, this section extends to a section
\[
a\in \mathcal F_1(W).
\]
Replace $u$ by $u+a$. Then on $V\cap W$,
\[
(u+a)|_{V\cap W}=t|_{V\cap W}.
\]
Hence $t$ and $u+a$ glue to a section of $\mathcal F_2(V\cup W)$ lifting
$s|_{V\cup W}$, contradicting maximality.

Thus $V=U$, so $s$ lifts globally. Therefore
\[
\mathcal F_2(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(U)
\]
is surjective.

\subsection*{(b)}

Assume
\[
0 \longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3
\longrightarrow 0
\]
is exact, and suppose that $\mathcal F_1$ and $\mathcal F_2$ are flabby.
We prove that $\mathcal F_3$ is flabby.

Let $V\subseteq U$ be open. We must prove that
\[
\mathcal F_3(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(V)
\]
is surjective.

Take $s\in \mathcal F_3(V)$. By part (a), because $\mathcal F_1$ is flabby,
the map
\[
\mathcal F_2(V)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(V)
\]
is surjective. Hence there exists
\[
t\in \mathcal F_2(V)
\]
mapping to $s$.

Since $\mathcal F_2$ is flabby, $t$ extends to a section
\[
\widetilde t\in \mathcal F_2(U).
\]
Let $\widetilde s$ be the image of $\widetilde t$ in $\mathcal F_3(U)$.
Then
\[
\widetilde s|_V=s.
\]
Therefore the restriction map
\[
\mathcal F_3(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(V)
\]
is surjective, so $\mathcal F_3$ is flabby.

\subsection*{(c)}

Let
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_0
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \cdots
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p-1}
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p+1}
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
be a long exact sequence of flabby sheaves.

We prove that for every open set $U\subseteq X$,
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_0(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1(U)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p-1}(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p+1}(U)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
is exact.

Exactness is automatic except possibly at the level of surjectivity onto
kernels. Let
\[
Z_p=\ker(\mathcal F_p\to \mathcal F_{p+1}).
\]
Since the original sequence is exact as a sequence of sheaves, we have short
exact sequences
\[
0\longrightarrow Z_p
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p
\longrightarrow Z_{p+1}
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

We prove by induction that each $Z_p$ is flabby. Since $\mathcal F_0$ is
flabby, $Z_1$ is a quotient of flabby sheaves in the sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow Z_0
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_0
\longrightarrow Z_1
\longrightarrow 0.
\]
Using part (b), the flabbiness propagates from one kernel sheaf to the next.

Now apply part (a) to each short exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow Z_p
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p
\longrightarrow Z_{p+1}
\longrightarrow 0.
\]
Since $Z_p$ is flabby, the map
\[
\mathcal F_p(U)\longrightarrow Z_{p+1}(U)
\]
is surjective.

But $Z_{p+1}(U)$ is precisely the kernel of
\[
\mathcal F_{p+1}(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p+2}(U).
\]
Therefore the sequence of global sections over $U$ is exact.

\subsection*{(d)}

Let $\mathcal U=\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ be an open covering of $X$. Define
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i_0,\ldots,i_p\in I}
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}.
\]
Then
\[
C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\Gamma(X,\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)).
\]

The boundary maps
\[
d^p:\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^{p+1}(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\]
are defined by the usual alternating formula:
\[
(d^p s)_{i_0,\ldots,i_{p+1}}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{p+1}
(-1)^k
s_{i_0,\ldots,\widehat{i_k},\ldots,i_{p+1}}
\big|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_{p+1}}}.
\]

We must show that
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\]
is exact.

It is enough to check exactness on stalks. Let $x\in X$. Choose an index
$i\in I$ such that $x\in U_i$.

On the stalk at $x$, the complex becomes the augmented \v{C}ech complex
of the covering of a point. This complex is exact because it has a
contracting homotopy.

Explicitly, define
\[
h:C^p_x\longrightarrow C^{p-1}_x
\]
by
\[
(hs)_{i_0,\ldots,i_{p-1}}
=
s_{i,i_0,\ldots,i_{p-1}}.
\]
Then one checks that
\[
dh+hd=\operatorname{id}.
\]
Therefore the stalk complex is exact at every point $x\in X$.

Hence the sequence of sheaves
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\]
is exact.

\subsection*{(e)}

Assume now that $\mathcal F$ is flabby. We prove that each
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\]
is flabby.

For every multi-index $(i_0,\ldots,i_p)$, the sheaf
\[
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}
\]
is flabby. Indeed, if $V\subseteq W$ are open in $X$, then
\[
\mathcal F(W\cap U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p})
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F(V\cap U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p})
\]
is surjective because $\mathcal F$ is flabby.

A product of flabby sheaves is flabby. Therefore
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i_0,\ldots,i_p}
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}
\]
is flabby.

By part (d), we have an exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
\cdots.
\]
Since all the sheaves $\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)$ are flabby,
part (c) implies that taking global sections preserves exactness. Hence
\[
0\longrightarrow \Gamma(X,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
is exact.

But the cohomology of this complex is precisely the \v{C}ech cohomology
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F).
\]
Therefore
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
\qquad
\text{for all }p>0.
\]

\subsection{Conclusion}

For a flabby sheaf $\mathcal F$ on a topological space $X$, every section
over an open subset extends to every larger open subset. This extension
property forces the \v{C}ech complex associated to any open covering
$\mathcal U$ to be exact in positive degrees. Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
	\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
	\quad\text{for all }p>0.
}
\]
\section{Theory: Flabby Sheaves and Vanishing of \v{C}ech Cohomology}

A sheaf $\mathcal F$ on a topological space $X$ is called \emph{flabby}
if for every inclusion of open sets $V\subseteq U$, the restriction map
\[
\mathcal F(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F(V)
\]
is surjective.

Equivalently, every section over a smaller open set $V$ extends to every
larger open set $U$ containing it. Thus flabbiness is a strong extension
property.

\subsection*{Basic meaning of flabbiness}

For an arbitrary sheaf, a section over an open set $V$ does not necessarily
extend to a larger open set $U$. In other words, the restriction map
\[
\mathcal F(U)\to \mathcal F(V)
\]
need not be surjective.

A flabby sheaf is one for which this obstruction never appears. Every local
section can be extended to a larger open set. This makes flabby sheaves very
useful in cohomology, because cohomology measures obstructions to gluing and
extending local data.

\subsection*{Flabby sheaves and exact sequences}

Suppose
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3
\longrightarrow 0
\]
is an exact sequence of sheaves.

In general, taking sections over an open set $U$ gives a left exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(U),
\]
but the last map need not be surjective.

However, if $\mathcal F_1$ is flabby, then local liftings of sections of
$\mathcal F_3$ can be modified and glued globally. Therefore the map
\[
\mathcal F_2(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(U)
\]
is surjective for every open set $U\subseteq X$.

Hence, if $\mathcal F_1$ is flabby, then
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(U)
\longrightarrow 0
\]
is exact for every open set $U$.

\subsection*{Quotients of flabby sheaves}

Suppose again that
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_2
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3
\longrightarrow 0
\]
is exact, and assume that $\mathcal F_1$ and $\mathcal F_2$ are flabby.

Then $\mathcal F_3$ is also flabby.

Indeed, let $V\subseteq U$ be open and take
\[
s\in \mathcal F_3(V).
\]
Since $\mathcal F_1$ is flabby, the map
\[
\mathcal F_2(V)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(V)
\]
is surjective. Therefore $s$ lifts to some
\[
t\in \mathcal F_2(V).
\]
Since $\mathcal F_2$ is flabby, $t$ extends to a section
\[
\widetilde t\in \mathcal F_2(U).
\]
Its image in $\mathcal F_3(U)$ extends $s$. Hence
\[
\mathcal F_3(U)\longrightarrow \mathcal F_3(V)
\]
is surjective, so $\mathcal F_3$ is flabby.

\subsection*{Exactness of global sections for long exact sequences}

Let
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_0
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1
\longrightarrow \cdots
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p-1}
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p+1}
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
be a long exact sequence of flabby sheaves.

For each $p$, define
\[
Z_p=\ker(\mathcal F_p\to \mathcal F_{p+1}).
\]
Then there are short exact sequences
\[
0\longrightarrow Z_p
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p
\longrightarrow Z_{p+1}
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

Using the fact that quotients of flabby sheaves by flabby subsheaves are
flabby, one proves inductively that all the sheaves $Z_p$ are flabby.

Then, since $Z_p$ is flabby, the map
\[
\mathcal F_p(U)\longrightarrow Z_{p+1}(U)
\]
is surjective for every open set $U$.

But
\[
Z_{p+1}(U)
=
\ker\bigl(\mathcal F_{p+1}(U)\to \mathcal F_{p+2}(U)\bigr).
\]
Therefore
\[
\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\mathcal F_p(U)\to \mathcal F_{p+1}(U)\bigr)
=
\ker\bigl(\mathcal F_{p+1}(U)\to \mathcal F_{p+2}(U)\bigr).
\]

Hence the sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F_0(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_1(U)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_p(U)
\longrightarrow \mathcal F_{p+1}(U)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
is exact.

Thus long exact sequences of flabby sheaves remain exact after taking
sections over any open set.

\subsection*{The \v{C}ech complex}

Let $\mathcal U=\{U_i\}_{i\in I}$ be an open covering of $X$.

The \v{C}ech cochains of $\mathcal F$ with respect to $\mathcal U$ are
\[
C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i_0,\ldots,i_p\in I}
\mathcal F(U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}).
\]

Thus
\[
C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_i \mathcal F(U_i),
\]
\[
C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i,j}\mathcal F(U_i\cap U_j),
\]
and
\[
C^2(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i,j,k}\mathcal F(U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k).
\]

The \v{C}ech differential
\[
\delta:C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow
C^{p+1}(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\]
is defined by
\[
(\delta s)_{i_0,\ldots,i_{p+1}}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{p+1}
(-1)^k
s_{i_0,\ldots,\widehat{i_k},\ldots,i_{p+1}}
\big|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_{p+1}}}.
\]

For example, if $s=(s_i)\in C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)$, then
\[
(\delta s)_{ij}
=
s_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}
-
s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}.
\]

Thus $\delta s=0$ means that the local sections $s_i$ agree on overlaps,
so they glue to a global section.

For a $1$-cochain $(s_{ij})$, one has
\[
(\delta s)_{ijk}
=
s_{jk}|_{U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k}
-
s_{ik}|_{U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k}
+
s_{ij}|_{U_i\cap U_j\cap U_k}.
\]

The \v{C}ech cohomology groups are
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\frac{\ker(\delta:C^p\to C^{p+1})}
{\operatorname{im}(\delta:C^{p-1}\to C^p)}.
\]

Thus \v{C}ech cohomology measures whether compatible data on intersections
comes from data on smaller intersections.

\subsection*{The sheaf \v{C}ech resolution}

Instead of only using groups of sections, we may define sheaves
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i_0,\ldots,i_p\in I}
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}.
\]

Then
\[
C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\Gamma(X,\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)).
\]

There is an augmented sequence of sheaves
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^2(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \cdots.
\]

This sequence is exact.

The reason is local. Exactness of sheaves can be checked on stalks. Fix a
point $x\in X$. Choose $i\in I$ such that $x\in U_i$.

On the stalk at $x$, the covering has a distinguished member $U_i$
containing $x$. The resulting stalk complex is contractible. A contracting
homotopy is given by inserting the fixed index $i$:
\[
h(s)_{i_0,\ldots,i_{p-1}}
=
s_{i,i_0,\ldots,i_{p-1}}.
\]

One checks that
\[
\delta h+h\delta=\operatorname{id}.
\]

Therefore the stalk complex is exact at every point $x$, and hence the
sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \cdots
\]
is exact as a sequence of sheaves.

\subsection*{Why the sheaves $\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)$ are flabby}

Assume that $\mathcal F$ is flabby.

For every multi-index $(i_0,\ldots,i_p)$, the sheaf
\[
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}
\]
is flabby. Indeed, if $V\subseteq W$ are open subsets of $X$, then the
restriction map
\[
\mathcal F(W\cap U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p})
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F(V\cap U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p})
\]
is surjective because $\mathcal F$ is flabby.

Also, a product of flabby sheaves is flabby, because restriction maps are
taken componentwise, and products of surjective maps are surjective.

Therefore
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
=
\prod_{i_0,\ldots,i_p}
\mathcal F|_{U_{i_0}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_p}}
\]
is flabby for every $p\geq 0$.

\subsection*{Vanishing of higher \v{C}ech cohomology}

We have an exact sequence of sheaves
\[
0\longrightarrow \mathcal F
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \mathcal C^2(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \cdots.
\]

If $\mathcal F$ is flabby, then every sheaf
\[
\mathcal C^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\]
is also flabby.

Since global sections preserve exactness for long exact sequences of flabby
sheaves, taking global sections gives an exact sequence
\[
0\longrightarrow \Gamma(X,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow C^0(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow C^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow C^2(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)
\longrightarrow \cdots.
\]

The cohomology of this complex in degree $p$ is precisely
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F).
\]

Since the complex is exact in all positive degrees, we obtain
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
\qquad
\text{for all }p>0.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\mathcal F \text{ flabby}
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
	\text{ for all }p>0.
}
\]

\subsection{Conceptual summary}

The central idea is that flabby sheaves have no extension obstruction.
Every local section over a smaller open set extends to every larger open
set. Because of this, local lifting and gluing problems disappear.

Since higher \v{C}ech cohomology measures precisely the failure of local
data on intersections to come from lower-degree data, flabby sheaves have no
positive-degree \v{C}ech cohomology.

In short,
\[
\boxed{
	\text{flabby sheaves are acyclic.}
}
\]

\section{Examples of Flabby Sheaves and Vanishing of \v{C}ech Cohomology}

\subsection{Example 1: The Constant Sheaf is Usually Not Flabby}

Let $X=\mathbb R$ and consider the constant sheaf $\mathbb Z_X$.

Take
\[
U=\mathbb R,
\qquad
V=(0,1)\cup(2,3).
\]

A section of $\mathbb Z_X$ over $V$ is a locally constant function.
Since $V$ has two connected components, we may define
\[
s|_{(0,1)}=1,
\qquad
s|_{(2,3)}=2.
\]

Suppose $s$ extended to a global section on $\mathbb R$.
Since $\mathbb R$ is connected, every global section of $\mathbb Z_X$
must be constant.

However, a constant function cannot simultaneously take the values
$1$ and $2$ on different components.

Therefore $s$ cannot be extended to $\mathbb R$.

Hence the restriction map
\[
\mathbb Z_X(\mathbb R)
\longrightarrow
\mathbb Z_X(V)
\]
is not surjective.

Thus
\[
\boxed{\mathbb Z_X \text{ is not flabby.}}
\]

\subsection{Example 2: A Skyscraper Sheaf is Flabby}

Fix a point $x\in X$ and an abelian group $A$.

The skyscraper sheaf $i_{x,*}A$ is defined by
\[
(i_{x,*}A)(U)=
\begin{cases}
	A, & x\in U,\\
	0, & x\notin U.
\end{cases}
\]

Let $V\subseteq U$.

If $x\notin V$, then
\[
(i_{x,*}A)(V)=0,
\]
so the restriction map is automatically surjective.

If $x\in V$, then also $x\in U$, and
\[
(i_{x,*}A)(U)=A,
\qquad
(i_{x,*}A)(V)=A.
\]

The restriction map is simply
\[
A\longrightarrow A,
\]
which is the identity map.

Therefore every restriction map is surjective.

Hence
\[
\boxed{i_{x,*}A \text{ is flabby.}}
\]

Consequently,
\[
\check H^p(\mathcal U,i_{x,*}A)=0
\qquad (p>0).
\]

\subsection{Example 3: The Sheaf of All Functions}

Let $A$ be an abelian group and define
\[
\mathcal F(U)
=
\{f:U\to A\}.
\]

Take open sets
\[
V\subseteq U.
\]

Given a function
\[
f:V\to A,
\]
choose a fixed element $a_0\in A$ and define
\[
\widetilde f(x)=
\begin{cases}
	f(x), & x\in V,\\
	a_0, & x\in U\setminus V.
\end{cases}
\]

Then
\[
\widetilde f|_V=f.
\]

Thus every section extends.

Therefore
\[
\boxed{\mathcal F \text{ is flabby.}}
\]

\subsection{Example 4: Continuous Functions are Not Flabby}

Consider the sheaf
\[
\mathcal C^0(U)
=
\{\text{continuous functions }U\to\mathbb R\}.
\]

Let
\[
U=\mathbb R,
\qquad
V=(0,1).
\]

Define
\[
f(x)=\frac1x.
\]

The function is continuous on $(0,1)$.

Suppose $f$ extended to a continuous function
\[
\widetilde f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R.
\]

Near $0$ we have
\[
\frac1x\to+\infty.
\]

Therefore no continuous extension exists.

Hence
\[
\mathcal C^0(\mathbb R)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C^0((0,1))
\]
is not surjective.

Thus
\[
\boxed{\mathcal C^0 \text{ is not flabby.}}
\]

\subsection{Example 5: Vanishing of \v{C}ech $H^1$ for a Flabby Sheaf}

Suppose
\[
X=U_1\cup U_2
\]
and $\mathcal F$ is flabby.

The \v{C}ech complex begins as
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F(X)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F(U_1)\times\mathcal F(U_2)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F(U_1\cap U_2)
\longrightarrow0.
\]

The differential is
\[
\delta(s_1,s_2)
=
s_2|_{U_1\cap U_2}
-
s_1|_{U_1\cap U_2}.
\]

Take
\[
a\in\mathcal F(U_1\cap U_2).
\]

Since $\mathcal F$ is flabby, $a$ extends to a section
\[
s_2\in\mathcal F(U_2).
\]

Let
\[
s_1=0.
\]

Then
\[
\delta(s_1,s_2)=a.
\]

Therefore every $1$-cocycle is a coboundary.

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\check H^1(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0.
}
\]

\subsection{Example 6: Understanding the Cocycle Condition}

Let
\[
X=U_1\cup U_2\cup U_3.
\]

A \v{C}ech $1$-cochain consists of sections
\[
a_{12},\ a_{13},\ a_{23}.
\]

These form a cocycle when
\[
a_{23}
-
a_{13}
+
a_{12}
=
0
\]
on
\[
U_1\cap U_2\cap U_3.
\]

For an arbitrary sheaf, such overlap data may not come from sections on the
individual open sets.

For a flabby sheaf, each section on an intersection can be extended to a
larger open set.

Using these extensions one constructs sections
\[
s_i\in\mathcal F(U_i)
\]
such that
\[
a_{ij}
=
s_j|_{U_i\cap U_j}
-
s_i|_{U_i\cap U_j}.
\]

Hence
\[
a=\delta(s).
\]

Thus every cocycle is a coboundary.

This explains geometrically why higher \v{C}ech cohomology vanishes.

\subsection{Example 7: Flabby Resolutions}

The constant sheaf $\mathbb Z_X$ is generally not flabby.

However, it can be embedded into a flabby sheaf.

Let
\[
\mathcal G^0(U)
=
\{f:U\to\mathbb Z\},
\]
the sheaf of all integer-valued functions.

Then
\[
\mathbb Z_X
\hookrightarrow
\mathcal G^0.
\]

The quotient
\[
\mathcal G^1
=
\mathcal G^0/\mathbb Z_X
\]
is again used to continue the construction.

One obtains a flabby resolution
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathbb Z_X
\longrightarrow
\mathcal G^0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal G^1
\longrightarrow
\mathcal G^2
\longrightarrow
\cdots.
\]

Since all $\mathcal G^i$ are flabby, one computes cohomology using global
sections:
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal G^0)
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal G^1)
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal G^2)
\longrightarrow
\cdots.
\]

This is one of the fundamental methods of sheaf cohomology.

\subsection{Example 8: Computing Cohomology via a Flabby Resolution}

Suppose $\mathcal F$ is any sheaf.

Choose a flabby resolution
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal F
\longrightarrow
\mathcal I^0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal I^1
\longrightarrow
\mathcal I^2
\longrightarrow
\cdots.
\]

Because each $\mathcal I^p$ is flabby,
\[
H^q(X,\mathcal I^p)=0
\qquad(q>0).
\]

Therefore all cohomological information of $\mathcal F$ is contained in the
complex of global sections
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal I^0)
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal I^1)
\longrightarrow
\Gamma(X,\mathcal I^2)
\longrightarrow
\cdots.
\]

The cohomology of this complex is
\[
H^p(X,\mathcal F).
\]

This illustrates why flabby sheaves play such a central role in modern
algebraic geometry.

\subsection{Summary Table}

\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{|l|c|l|}
		\hline
		Sheaf & Flabby? & Reason \\
		\hline
		All functions $U\to A$ & Yes &
		Arbitrary extensions exist \\
		\hline
		Skyscraper sheaf $i_{x,*}A$ & Yes &
		Restrictions are identities or zero maps \\
		\hline
		Constant sheaf $\mathbb Z_X$ & Usually No &
		Locally constant sections may not extend \\
		\hline
		Continuous functions $\mathcal C^0$ & No &
		Continuous extension may fail \\
		\hline
		Holomorphic functions $\mathcal O_X$ & No &
		Analytic continuation creates obstructions \\
		\hline
		Terms in a flabby resolution & Yes &
		Constructed to have surjective restrictions \\
		\hline
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection{Conceptual Conclusion}

Flabby sheaves possess an extremely strong extension property:

\[
\boxed{
	\text{Every local section extends to every larger open set.}
}
\]

Because local sections can always be extended, every \v{C}ech cocycle can be
resolved into a coboundary.

Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
	\check H^p(\mathcal U,\mathcal F)=0
	\qquad (p>0).
}
\]

This is the fundamental reason that flabby sheaves are called
\emph{acyclic sheaves} and are used throughout sheaf cohomology and
algebraic geometry.


\section{Problem 4: The Punctured Affine Plane is Not Affine}

Let
\[
X=\mathbb A_k^2=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y],
\]
and let
\[
U=X\setminus\{(x,y)\}
\]
be the affine plane with the origin removed.

We cover \(U\) by the two standard affine open subsets
\[
U=D(x)\cup D(y).
\]

Indeed, a point of \(\mathbb A_k^2\) is not the origin precisely when either
\(x\neq 0\) or \(y\neq 0\).

Thus
\[
D(x)=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]_x,
\qquad
D(y)=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]_y,
\]
and
\[
D(x)\cap D(y)=D(xy)=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y]_{xy}.
\]

The \v{C}ech complex for \(\mathcal O_U\) with respect to this cover is
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_U(U)
\longrightarrow
k[x,y]_x\oplus k[x,y]_y
\longrightarrow
k[x,y]_{xy}
\longrightarrow
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

The middle map is
\[
(f,g)\longmapsto g-f.
\]

Therefore
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong
\frac{k[x,y]_{xy}}{k[x,y]_x+k[x,y]_y}.
\]

Now
\[
k[x,y]_{xy}=k[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}].
\]

Every element of this ring is a finite \(k\)-linear combination of monomials
\[
x^iy^j,
\qquad i,j\in\mathbb Z.
\]

The subring \(k[x,y]_x\) contains all monomials
\[
x^iy^j,
\qquad i\in\mathbb Z,\ j\geq0.
\]

Similarly, \(k[x,y]_y\) contains all monomials
\[
x^iy^j,
\qquad i\geq0,\ j\in\mathbb Z.
\]

Therefore, in the quotient
\[
\frac{k[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}]}
{k[x,y,x^{-1}]+k[x,y,y^{-1}]},
\]
all monomials disappear except those with both exponents negative.

Hence
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong
\bigoplus_{i<0,\ j<0} kx^iy^j.
\]

Thus \(H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)\) has \(k\)-basis
\[
\{x^iy^j\mid i<0,\ j<0\}.
\]

In particular,
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\]
is infinite-dimensional over \(k\).

If \(U\) were affine, then for the quasi-coherent sheaf \(\mathcal O_U\),
we would have
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)=0.
\]

But we have just shown that
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)\neq0.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	U=\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{0\}
	\text{ is not affine.}
}
\]

\section{Problem 5: A Plane Curve and Its \v{C}ech Cohomology}

Let \(X\subseteq\mathbb P_k^2\) be the closed subscheme defined by one
homogeneous polynomial
\[
f(x_0,x_1,x_2)=0
\]
of degree \(d\).

Assume
\[
(1,0,0)\notin X.
\]

We first show that \(X\) is covered by the two affine open subsets
\[
U=X\cap D_+(x_1),
\qquad
V=X\cap D_+(x_2).
\]

The complement of
\[
D_+(x_1)\cup D_+(x_2)
\]
inside \(\mathbb P_k^2\) is the single point
\[
(1,0,0).
\]

Since
\[
(1,0,0)\notin X,
\]
every point of \(X\) lies in either \(D_+(x_1)\) or \(D_+(x_2)\).

Hence
\[
X=U\cup V.
\]

Both \(U\) and \(V\) are affine.

On \(D_+(x_1)\), set
\[
t=\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\qquad
z=\frac{x_2}{x_1}.
\]

Then
\[
U\simeq \operatorname{Spec} k[t,z]/(f(t,1,z)).
\]

Similarly, on \(D_+(x_2)\), set
\[
s=\frac{x_0}{x_2},
\qquad
w=\frac{x_1}{x_2}.
\]

Then
\[
V\simeq \operatorname{Spec} k[s,w]/(f(s,w,1)).
\]

Their intersection is
\[
U\cap V=X\cap D_+(x_1x_2).
\]

This is affine.

Since
\[
(1,0,0)\notin X,
\]
we have
\[
f(1,0,0)\neq0.
\]

Therefore the coefficient of \(x_0^d\) in \(f\) is nonzero. After scaling,
we may assume that this coefficient is \(1\).

On the intersection \(U\cap V\), use coordinates
\[
u=\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\qquad
v=\frac{x_2}{x_1}.
\]

Since \(x_2\neq0\) on \(U\cap V\), the coordinate \(v\) is invertible.

Thus
\[
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
=
k[u,v,v^{-1}]/(f(u,1,v)).
\]

Because \(f(u,1,v)\) is monic of degree \(d\) in \(u\), every element of
\(\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)\) can be represented uniquely as
\[
\sum_{r=0}^{d-1} a_r(v)u^r,
\qquad
a_r(v)\in k[v,v^{-1}].
\]

The \v{C}ech complex for the cover \(X=U\cup V\) is
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(X)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\longrightarrow
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
\longrightarrow0.
\]

Since \(U\), \(V\), and \(U\cap V\) are affine, this complex computes
\(H^0\) and \(H^1\).

The global sections are the elements of
\[
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\]
which agree on \(U\cap V\). For a projective plane curve, the only global
regular functions are constants. Hence
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\cong k,
\]
so
\[
\boxed{
	\dim_k H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=1.
}
\]

Now we compute \(H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)\).

The cokernel of
\[
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\]
is represented by the principal parts on the overlap that cannot come from
sections on \(U\) or on \(V\).

A basis for the quotient is given by the monomials
\[
u^r v^{-m}
\]
with
\[
1\leq r,
\qquad
1\leq m,
\qquad
r+m\leq d-1.
\]

Equivalently,
\[
1\leq r\leq d-2,
\qquad
1\leq m\leq d-1-r.
\]

For each \(r=1,\ldots,d-2\), the integer \(m\) can take
\[
d-1-r
\]
values.

Therefore the number of such monomials is
\[
\sum_{r=1}^{d-2}(d-1-r)
=
(d-2)+(d-3)+\cdots+1.
\]

Hence
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
1+2+\cdots+(d-2).
\]

Therefore
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\dim_k H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=1
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
	\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
}
\]

If \(X\) is nonsingular, then
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
\]
is the genus of the plane curve. Hence a nonsingular plane curve of degree
\(d\) has genus
\[
\boxed{
	g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
}
\]

\section{Problem 6: Relative Differentials}

\subsection*{(a) Construction and Universal Property of \(\Omega_{B/A}\)}

Let \(A\) be a ring, let \(B\) be an \(A\)-algebra, and let \(M\) be a
\(B\)-module.

An \(A\)-derivation of \(B\) into \(M\) is a map
\[
d:B\to M
\]
such that:
\[
d(b+b')=d(b)+d(b'),
\]
\[
d(bb')=b\,d(b')+b'\,d(b),
\]
and
\[
d(a)=0
\qquad
\text{for all }a\in A.
\]

Let
\[
\mu:B\otimes_A B\longrightarrow B
\]
be the multiplication map
\[
\mu(b\otimes b')=bb'.
\]

Let
\[
I=\ker(\mu).
\]

Define
\[
\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2.
\]

For \(b\in B\), define
\[
db=1\otimes b-b\otimes1.
\]

Since
\[
\mu(1\otimes b-b\otimes1)=b-b=0,
\]
we have
\[
db\in I.
\]

Thus \(db\) defines an element of \(I/I^2\).

We check that
\[
d:B\to I/I^2
\]
is an \(A\)-derivation.

First,
\[
d(b+b')
=
1\otimes(b+b')-(b+b')\otimes1.
\]

Therefore
\[
d(b+b')=db+db'.
\]

Second,
\[
d(bb')
=
1\otimes bb'-bb'\otimes1.
\]

Modulo \(I^2\), one has
\[
d(bb')=b\,db'+b'\,db.
\]

Indeed, this identity is exactly the image in \(I/I^2\) of the standard
identity expressing the Leibniz rule.

Finally, if \(a\in A\), then
\[
da=1\otimes a-a\otimes1=0
\]
because the tensor product is taken over \(A\).

So \(d\) is an \(A\)-derivation.

Now let
\[
d':B\to M
\]
be any \(A\)-derivation.

Define a map
\[
\varphi:B\otimes_A B\to M
\]
by
\[
\varphi(b\otimes b')=b\,d'(b').
\]

The derivation rule implies that \(\varphi\) sends \(I^2\) to zero after
restriction to \(I\). Hence \(\varphi|_I\) factors through \(I/I^2\).

Therefore there exists a \(B\)-linear map
\[
f:I/I^2\to M
\]
such that
\[
f(db)=d'(b).
\]

Thus
\[
d'=f\circ d.
\]

The map \(f\) is unique because \(\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2\) is generated by the
elements \(db\).

Therefore \(\Omega_{B/A}\), together with \(d:B\to\Omega_{B/A}\), satisfies
the universal property:

for every \(A\)-derivation \(d':B\to M\), there exists a unique \(B\)-linear
map
\[
f:\Omega_{B/A}\to M
\]
such that
\[
d'=f\circ d.
\]

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2.
}
\]

\subsection*{(b) Computation of \(\Omega_{k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/k}\)}

Let
\[
A=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n].
\]

We claim that
\[
\Omega_{A/k}
\cong
A\,dx_1\oplus\cdots\oplus A\,dx_n.
\]

Define
\[
d:A\to \bigoplus_{i=1}^n A\,dx_i
\]
by
\[
d(f)=\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i.
\]

This map is \(k\)-linear and satisfies
\[
d(fg)=f\,dg+g\,df
\]
because ordinary partial differentiation satisfies the product rule.

Also,
\[
d(c)=0
\qquad
(c\in k).
\]

Therefore \(d\) is a \(k\)-derivation.

Now let
\[
D:A\to M
\]
be any \(k\)-derivation into an \(A\)-module \(M\).

Since \(A\) is a polynomial ring, \(D\) is determined by the values
\[
D(x_1),\ldots,D(x_n).
\]

Define an \(A\)-linear map
\[
\varphi:
A\,dx_1\oplus\cdots\oplus A\,dx_n
\longrightarrow M
\]
by
\[
\varphi(dx_i)=D(x_i).
\]

Then for every polynomial \(f\),
\[
\varphi(df)
=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}D(x_i).
\]

By the usual formula for derivations on a polynomial ring,
\[
D(f)
=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}D(x_i).
\]

Thus
\[
D=\varphi\circ d.
\]

The map \(\varphi\) is unique because \(dx_1,\ldots,dx_n\) form a basis.

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\Omega_{k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/k}
	\cong
	\bigoplus_{i=1}^n k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\,dx_i.
}
\]

So \(\Omega_{A/k}\) is the free \(A\)-module generated by
\[
dx_1,\ldots,dx_n.
\]

Moreover, for every polynomial \(f\in A\),
\[
\boxed{
	df=\sum_{i=1}^n
	\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i.
}
\]

\subsection*{(c) The Sheaf of Differentials on Affine Schemes}

Let
\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}B,
\qquad
Y=\operatorname{Spec}A,
\]
where \(B\) is an \(A\)-algebra.

Then
\[
X\times_Y X
=
\operatorname{Spec}(B\otimes_A B).
\]

The diagonal morphism
\[
\Delta:X\to X\times_Y X
\]
corresponds to the multiplication map
\[
B\otimes_A B\to B,
\qquad
b\otimes b'\mapsto bb'.
\]

Let
\[
I=\ker(B\otimes_A B\to B).
\]

The conormal sheaf of the diagonal closed immersion is
\[
I/I^2
\]
sheafified on \(X\).

By part (a),
\[
I/I^2=\Omega_{B/A}.
\]

Therefore
\[
\Omega_{X/Y}
\cong
\widetilde{\Omega_{B/A}}.
\]

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\Omega_{\operatorname{Spec}B/\operatorname{Spec}A}
	\cong
	\widetilde{\Omega_{B/A}}.
}
\]

\subsection*{(d) Differentials on Affine Space}

Let
\[
X=\mathbb A_k^n
=
\operatorname{Spec}k[x_1,\ldots,x_n].
\]

By part (b),
\[
\Omega_{k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/k}
\cong
\bigoplus_{i=1}^n k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\,dx_i.
\]

Sheafifying this module on \(\mathbb A_k^n\), we obtain
\[
\Omega_{\mathbb A_k^n/\operatorname{Spec}k}
\cong
\bigoplus_{i=1}^n
\mathcal O_{\mathbb A_k^n}\,dx_i.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	\Omega_{\mathbb A_k^n/\operatorname{Spec}k}
	\cong
	\mathcal O_{\mathbb A_k^n}^{\oplus n}.
}
\]

Thus \(\Omega_{\mathbb A_k^n/\operatorname{Spec}k}\) is a free
\(\mathcal O_{\mathbb A_k^n}\)-module of rank \(n\), generated by
\[
dx_1,\ldots,dx_n.
\]

\section{Theory: Affine Covers, \v{C}ech Cohomology, Plane Curves, and Relative Differentials}

\subsection{Affine Schemes and Vanishing of Higher Cohomology}

A fundamental theorem in algebraic geometry says that if $X$ is affine and
$\mathcal F$ is quasi-coherent, then
\[
H^p(X,\mathcal F)=0
\qquad
\text{for all }p>0.
\]

In particular, for the structure sheaf,
\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=0
\]
whenever $X$ is affine.

Therefore, to prove that a scheme $U$ is not affine, it is enough to find
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)\neq 0.
\]

For example, for the punctured affine plane
\[
U=\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{0\},
\]
we use the cover
\[
U=D(x)\cup D(y).
\]

The overlap is
\[
D(x)\cap D(y)=D(xy).
\]

The \v{C}ech computation gives
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong
\frac{k[x,y]_{xy}}{k[x,y]_x+k[x,y]_y}.
\]

Now
\[
k[x,y]_{xy}=k[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}].
\]

The monomials which survive in the quotient are exactly those of the form
\[
x^iy^j
\qquad
(i<0,\ j<0).
\]

Hence
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong
\bigoplus_{i<0,\ j<0}kx^iy^j.
\]

This is infinite-dimensional over $k$, and in particular nonzero. Therefore
\[
\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{0\}
\]
is not affine.

The principle is:
\[
\boxed{
	H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)\neq0
	\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
	U\text{ is not affine}.
}
\]

\subsection{\v{C}ech Cohomology on a Two-Open Affine Cover}

Suppose
\[
X=U\cup V
\]
where $U$, $V$, and $U\cap V$ are affine.

Then the \v{C}ech complex for $\mathcal O_X$ is
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(X)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

The map is
\[
(s_U,s_V)
\longmapsto
s_V|_{U\cap V}-s_U|_{U\cap V}.
\]

Thus
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\ker\left(
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\to
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\right),
\]
and
\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\operatorname{coker}\left(
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\to
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\right).
\]

Therefore $H^0$ consists of pairs of regular functions on $U$ and $V$ that
agree on the overlap, while $H^1$ measures regular functions on the overlap
which cannot be written as a difference of functions coming from $U$ and $V$.

This is the reason why quotients such as
\[
\frac{k[x,y]_{xy}}{k[x,y]_x+k[x,y]_y}
\]
naturally appear in \v{C}ech cohomology.

\subsection{Projective Plane Curves and the Genus Formula}

Let
\[
X\subseteq \mathbb P_k^2
\]
be a plane curve defined by one homogeneous polynomial
\[
f(x_0,x_1,x_2)=0
\]
of degree $d$.

Assume
\[
(1,0,0)\notin X.
\]

Then $X$ is covered by
\[
U=X\cap D_+(x_1),
\qquad
V=X\cap D_+(x_2).
\]

Indeed, the complement of
\[
D_+(x_1)\cup D_+(x_2)
\]
in $\mathbb P_k^2$ is the single point
\[
(1,0,0),
\]
which does not belong to $X$.

Thus
\[
X=U\cup V.
\]

The sets $U$, $V$, and $U\cap V$ are affine. Therefore the two-open
\v{C}ech complex computes $H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)$ and
$H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)$.

For a projective plane curve, global regular functions are constants, so
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\cong k.
\]

Hence
\[
\dim_k H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=1.
\]

The first cohomology group $H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)$ measures the failure of
regular functions on the overlap $U\cap V$ to be represented by functions on
$U$ and $V$.

For a plane curve of degree $d$, the explicit \v{C}ech computation gives
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
\]

If $X$ is nonsingular, then this dimension equals the genus:
\[
g(X)=\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X).
\]

Therefore a nonsingular plane curve of degree $d$ has genus
\[
\boxed{
	g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
}
\]

For example:
\[
d=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=0,
\]
\[
d=2\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=0,
\]
\[
d=3\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=1,
\]
\[
d=4\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=3.
\]

Thus lines and conics have genus $0$, smooth cubics have genus $1$, and
smooth quartics have genus $3$.

\subsection{K\"ahler Differentials}

Let
\[
A\to B
\]
be a ring homomorphism.

The module of relative differentials
\[
\Omega_{B/A}
\]
is the universal object representing $A$-derivations from $B$.

An $A$-derivation into a $B$-module $M$ is a map
\[
D:B\to M
\]
such that
\[
D(b+b')=D(b)+D(b'),
\]
\[
D(bb')=bD(b')+b'D(b),
\]
and
\[
D(a)=0
\qquad
(a\in A).
\]

The universal property of $\Omega_{B/A}$ says that there exists an
$A$-derivation
\[
d:B\to\Omega_{B/A}
\]
such that every $A$-derivation
\[
D:B\to M
\]
factors uniquely through $d$.

That is, there is a unique $B$-linear map
\[
\varphi:\Omega_{B/A}\to M
\]
such that
\[
D=\varphi\circ d.
\]

Equivalently,
\[
\operatorname{Der}_A(B,M)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_B(\Omega_{B/A},M).
\]

Thus $\Omega_{B/A}$ represents the functor of $A$-derivations.

\subsection{Construction of Differentials Using the Diagonal}

The module $\Omega_{B/A}$ can be constructed as
\[
\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2,
\]
where
\[
I=\ker(B\otimes_A B\to B),
\]
and the map
\[
B\otimes_A B\to B
\]
is multiplication:
\[
b\otimes b'\longmapsto bb'.
\]

The universal differential is
\[
db=1\otimes b-b\otimes1.
\]

Indeed,
\[
1\otimes b-b\otimes1\in I
\]
because multiplication sends it to
\[
b-b=0.
\]

The quotient $I/I^2$ keeps only first-order information. Products of elements
of $I$ are second-order infinitesimals, and these are killed in $I/I^2$.

Geometrically, this construction comes from the diagonal morphism
\[
\Delta:X\to X\times_Y X.
\]

When
\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}B,
\qquad
Y=\operatorname{Spec}A,
\]
we have
\[
X\times_Y X=\operatorname{Spec}(B\otimes_A B).
\]

The diagonal corresponds to the multiplication map
\[
B\otimes_A B\to B.
\]

The ideal $I$ defines the diagonal, and
\[
I/I^2
\]
is the conormal module of the diagonal.

Thus differentials measure first-order infinitesimal displacement away from
the diagonal.

\subsection{Differentials on Affine Space}

Let
\[
A=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n].
\]

Then
\[
\Omega_{A/k}
\cong
A\,dx_1\oplus\cdots\oplus A\,dx_n.
\]

Thus $\Omega_{A/k}$ is a free $A$-module of rank $n$.

For every polynomial $f\in A$,
\[
df
=
\sum_{i=1}^n
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}dx_i.
\]

This is the algebraic analogue of the total differential from calculus.

After sheafifying on
\[
\mathbb A_k^n=\operatorname{Spec}k[x_1,\ldots,x_n],
\]
we get
\[
\Omega_{\mathbb A_k^n/\operatorname{Spec}k}
\cong
\mathcal O_{\mathbb A_k^n}^{\oplus n}.
\]

Therefore
\[
\Omega_{\mathbb A_k^n/\operatorname{Spec}k}
\]
is a free $\mathcal O_{\mathbb A_k^n}$-module of rank $n$, generated by
\[
dx_1,\ldots,dx_n.
\]

\subsection{Conceptual Summary}

The problems in this group are linked by the same principle:

\[
\boxed{
	\text{local algebra controls global geometry.}
}
\]

For the punctured affine plane, the localizations
\[
k[x,y]_x,
\qquad
k[x,y]_y,
\qquad
k[x,y]_{xy}
\]
detect non-affineness through the nonzero cohomology group
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U).
\]

For plane curves, affine charts and their overlap compute
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X),
\]
leading to the genus formula
\[
g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
\]

For relative differentials, the algebra
\[
B\otimes_A B
\]
and the diagonal ideal
\[
I=\ker(B\otimes_A B\to B)
\]
encode infinitesimal geometry through
\[
\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2.
\]

Thus these problems introduce three central tools:
\[
\boxed{
	\text{\v{C}ech cohomology, affine covers, and K\"ahler differentials.}
}
\]
\section{Examples: Affine Covers, \v{C}ech Cohomology, Plane Curves, and Differentials}

\subsection{Example 1: A Basic \v{C}ech Computation on Two Opens}

Let
\[
X=U\cup V.
\]

For the structure sheaf, the \v{C}ech complex is
\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(X)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus\mathcal O_X(V)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

The middle map is
\[
(f,g)
\longmapsto
g|_{U\cap V}-f|_{U\cap V}.
\]

Therefore
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\{(f,g)\mid f|_{U\cap V}=g|_{U\cap V}\},
\]
and
\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)}
{\mathcal O_X(U)+\mathcal O_X(V)}.
\]

Thus $H^1$ measures functions on the overlap which cannot be produced from
functions on the two pieces.

\subsection{Example 2: The Punctured Affine Line is Affine}

Let
\[
X=\mathbb A_k^1=\operatorname{Spec}k[x],
\qquad
U=X\setminus\{0\}.
\]

Then
\[
U=D(x).
\]

But
\[
D(x)=\operatorname{Spec}k[x]_x
=
\operatorname{Spec}k[x,x^{-1}].
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	\mathbb A_k^1\setminus\{0\}
	\text{ is affine.}
}
\]

This contrasts with
\[
\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{0\},
\]
which is not affine.

The difference is that in dimension $1$, removing the origin gives one
principal open set. In dimension $2$, removing the origin gives
\[
D(x)\cup D(y),
\]
and the overlap creates nonzero $H^1$.

\subsection{Example 3: The Punctured Affine Plane and Surviving Monomials}

Let
\[
U=\mathbb A_k^2\setminus\{0\}=D(x)\cup D(y).
\]

Then
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\cong
\frac{k[x,y,x^{-1},y^{-1}]}
{k[x,y,x^{-1}]+k[x,y,y^{-1}]}.
\]

We examine monomials
\[
x^iy^j.
\]

A monomial belongs to $k[x,y,x^{-1}]$ if
\[
j\geq0.
\]

A monomial belongs to $k[x,y,y^{-1}]$ if
\[
i\geq0.
\]

Therefore a monomial survives in the quotient only when
\[
i<0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
j<0.
\]

Thus examples of nonzero cohomology classes are
\[
x^{-1}y^{-1},
\qquad
x^{-2}y^{-1},
\qquad
x^{-1}y^{-3}.
\]

Hence
\[
H^1(U,\mathcal O_U)
\]
has basis
\[
\{x^iy^j\mid i<0,\ j<0\}.
\]

This gives infinitely many independent cohomology classes.

\subsection{Example 4: A Degree One Plane Curve}

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb P_k^2$ be a line.

Then $d=1$. The genus formula gives
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2
=
\frac{0\cdot(-1)}2
=
0.
\]

So
\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=0.
\]

Also,
\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\cong k.
\]

A line in $\mathbb P^2$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb P^1$, and $\mathbb P^1$
has genus $0$.

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\text{A line has genus }0.
}
\]

\subsection{Example 5: A Degree Two Plane Curve}

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb P_k^2$ be a smooth conic.

Then $d=2$. The formula gives
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(2-1)(2-2)}2
=
0.
\]

So
\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=0.
\]

A smooth conic is also isomorphic to $\mathbb P^1$, so it has genus $0$.

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\text{A smooth conic has genus }0.
}
\]

For example,
\[
X=V(x_0x_2-x_1^2)\subseteq\mathbb P^2
\]
is a conic, and its genus is $0$.

\subsection{Example 6: A Degree Three Plane Curve}

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb P_k^2$ be a smooth cubic.

Then $d=3$. The formula gives
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(3-1)(3-2)}2
=
1.
\]

Thus
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=1.
\]

Therefore the genus is
\[
g=1.
\]

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\text{A smooth plane cubic is an elliptic curve.}
}
\]

For example,
\[
X=V(y^2z-x^3-axz^2-bz^3)
\subseteq\mathbb P^2
\]
is a smooth cubic when the discriminant is nonzero.

\subsection{Example 7: A Degree Four Plane Curve}

Let $X\subseteq\mathbb P_k^2$ be a smooth quartic.

Then $d=4$. Hence
\[
\dim_k H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(4-1)(4-2)}2
=
\frac{3\cdot2}{2}
=
3.
\]

So a smooth quartic has genus
\[
g=3.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\text{A smooth plane quartic has genus }3.
}
\]

\subsection{Example 8: A Simple K\"ahler Differential Computation}

Let
\[
A=k[x].
\]

Then
\[
\Omega_{A/k}=A\,dx.
\]

For
\[
f=x^5+3x^2,
\]
we compute
\[
df=d(x^5+3x^2).
\]

Using the usual derivative rule,
\[
df=(5x^4+6x)\,dx.
\]

Thus, in algebraic geometry, the differential behaves like ordinary
calculus:
\[
\boxed{
	d(f)=f'(x)\,dx.
}
\]

\subsection{Example 9: K\"ahler Differentials in Two Variables}

Let
\[
A=k[x,y].
\]

Then
\[
\Omega_{A/k}=A\,dx\oplus A\,dy.
\]

For
\[
f=x^3y^2+y^5,
\]
we have
\[
df
=
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx
+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy.
\]

Now
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
=
3x^2y^2,
\]
and
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
=
2x^3y+5y^4.
\]

Therefore
\[
\boxed{
	df=
	3x^2y^2\,dx
	+
	(2x^3y+5y^4)\,dy.
}
\]

\subsection{Example 10: Differentials Detect Singular Points}

Let
\[
X=V(f)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2,
\]
where
\[
f=x^2-y^3.
\]

Then
\[
\mathcal O_X
=
k[x,y]/(x^2-y^3).
\]

The module of differentials is
\[
\Omega_{\mathcal O_X/k}
=
\frac{\mathcal O_X\,dx\oplus\mathcal O_X\,dy}
{(df)}.
\]

Compute
\[
df=2x\,dx-3y^2\,dy.
\]

Thus
\[
\Omega_{\mathcal O_X/k}
=
\frac{\mathcal O_X\,dx\oplus\mathcal O_X\,dy}
{(2x\,dx-3y^2\,dy)}.
\]

At the origin $(0,0)$, the relation becomes
\[
0=0.
\]

So there is no linear relation between $dx$ and $dy$ at the origin.

The cotangent space has dimension $2$, but the curve should have dimension
$1$.

This indicates that the origin is singular.

Thus differentials detect the cusp singularity of
\[
x^2=y^3.
\]

\subsection{Example 11: Smooth Hypersurface Differential Relation}

Let
\[
X=V(f)\subseteq\mathbb A_k^2
\]
with
\[
f=x^2+y^2-1.
\]

Then
\[
df=2x\,dx+2y\,dy.
\]

The module of differentials is
\[
\Omega_{\mathcal O_X/k}
=
\frac{\mathcal O_X\,dx\oplus\mathcal O_X\,dy}
{(2x\,dx+2y\,dy)}.
\]

At a point $(a,b)\in X$, not both partial derivatives vanish, because
\[
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
\]
on $x^2+y^2=1$.

Thus the relation
\[
2a\,dx+2b\,dy=0
\]
is nontrivial at every point of $X$.

This reflects that
\[
x^2+y^2-1=0
\]
is smooth.

\subsection{Example 12: The Diagonal and First-Order Differences}

Let
\[
B=k[x].
\]

Then
\[
B\otimes_k B
\cong
k[x_1,x_2].
\]

The multiplication map
\[
B\otimes_k B\to B
\]
corresponds to
\[
x_1\mapsto x,
\qquad
x_2\mapsto x.
\]

Its kernel is
\[
I=(x_2-x_1).
\]

Therefore
\[
\Omega_{B/k}=I/I^2.
\]

The class of
\[
x_2-x_1
\]
corresponds to
\[
dx.
\]

Hence
\[
\Omega_{k[x]/k}
\cong
k[x]\,dx.
\]

Geometrically, the diagonal in
\[
\mathbb A^1\times\mathbb A^1
\]
is defined by
\[
x_2-x_1=0.
\]

The quotient $I/I^2$ remembers the first-order displacement normal to the
diagonal.

\subsection{Final Intuition}

These examples show three recurring ideas.

First,
\[
\boxed{
	\text{cohomology detects non-affineness.}
}
\]

Second,
\[
\boxed{
	\text{\v{C}ech cohomology computes global geometry from affine charts.}
}
\]

Third,
\[
\boxed{
	\text{K\"ahler differentials encode infinitesimal geometry.}
}
\]

Therefore affine covers, \v{C}ech cohomology, and differentials are three
different ways of turning local algebra into geometric information.

\section{Explicit Čech Cohomology Computation for a Smooth Plane Curve}

Let

\[
X = V_+(f) \subset \mathbb{P}^2_k
\]

be a projective plane curve defined by the homogeneous polynomial

\[
f(x_0,x_1,x_2)=x_0^d+x_1^d+x_2^d,
\]

where \(d\geq 2\) and \(\operatorname{char}(k)\nmid d\).

Since

\[
f(1,0,0)=1\neq 0,
\]

the point \((1,0,0)\) does not belong to \(X\). Therefore every point of \(X\) satisfies either \(x_1\neq 0\) or \(x_2\neq 0\). Hence

\[
X = U \cup V,
\]

where

\[
U=X\cap D_+(x_1),
\qquad
V=X\cap D_+(x_2).
\]

We shall compute the Čech complex associated with this cover and determine
\(H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\) and \(H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)\).

\subsection{The affine chart \(U\)}

Inside \(D_+(x_1)\) introduce affine coordinates

\[
u=\frac{x_0}{x_1},
\qquad
v=\frac{x_2}{x_1}.
\]

Substituting into the defining equation gives

\[
f=x_1^d(u^d+1+v^d).
\]

Therefore

\[
U
\simeq
\operatorname{Spec}
\left(
k[u,v]/(u^d+v^d+1)
\right).
\]

Define

\[
A=
k[u,v]/(u^d+v^d+1).
\]

The defining ideal is

\[
I_U=(u^d+v^d+1)\subset k[u,v].
\]

Hence

\[
U=\operatorname{Spec}(A)
\]

is a closed affine subscheme of \(\mathbb A^2\).

\subsection{The affine chart \(V\)}

Inside \(D_+(x_2)\) introduce coordinates

\[
s=\frac{x_0}{x_2},
\qquad
t=\frac{x_1}{x_2}.
\]

Then

\[
f=x_2^d(s^d+t^d+1).
\]

Hence

\[
V
\simeq
\operatorname{Spec}
\left(
k[s,t]/(s^d+t^d+1)
\right).
\]

Set

\[
B=
k[s,t]/(s^d+t^d+1).
\]

Its defining ideal is

\[
I_V=(s^d+t^d+1)\subset k[s,t].
\]

Thus

\[
V=\operatorname{Spec}(B).
\]

\subsection{The intersection \(U\cap V\)}

The intersection consists of points where

\[
x_1\neq 0,
\qquad
x_2\neq 0.
\]

In the \(U\)-coordinates this means

\[
v=\frac{x_2}{x_1}\neq 0.
\]

Therefore

\[
U\cap V=D(v)\subset U.
\]

Consequently

\[
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
=
A_v
=
k[u,v,v^{-1}]
/
(u^d+v^d+1).
\]

The coordinate systems are related by

\[
s=\frac{x_0}{x_2}
=
\frac{x_0/x_1}{x_2/x_1}
=
\frac{u}{v},
\]

and

\[
t=\frac{x_1}{x_2}
=
v^{-1}.
\]

Hence

\[
s=\frac{u}{v},
\qquad
t=v^{-1}.
\]

\subsection{The Čech complex}

For the cover

\[
X=U\cup V
\]

the Čech complex of the structure sheaf is

\[
0
\longrightarrow
\mathcal O_X(U)\oplus \mathcal O_X(V)
\overset{\delta}{\longrightarrow}
\mathcal O_X(U\cap V)
\longrightarrow 0.
\]

Substituting the coordinate rings gives

\[
0
\longrightarrow
A\oplus B
\overset{\delta}{\longrightarrow}
A_v
\longrightarrow 0,
\]

where

\[
\delta(a,b)=a-b.
\]

Therefore

\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\ker(\delta),
\]

and

\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\operatorname{coker}(\delta)
=
A_v/(A+B).
\]

\subsection{Computation of \(H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)\)}

A regular function on a projective irreducible variety is constant.

Thus

\[
H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=k.
\]

Hence

\[
\boxed{
	\dim H^0(X,\mathcal O_X)=1.
}
\]

\subsection{Computation of \(H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)\)}

Elements of \(A_v\) may contain negative powers of \(v\).

The image of \(A\) contains all expressions with nonnegative powers of \(v\).

The image of \(B\) eliminates many additional negative-power terms via the relations

\[
s=\frac{u}{v},
\qquad
t=v^{-1}.
\]

After quotienting by \(A+B\), the surviving classes are represented by monomials

\[
u^i v^{-j}
\]

satisfying

\[
i\ge 0,
\qquad
j\ge 1,
\qquad
i+j\le d-2.
\]

Therefore a basis of \(H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)\) is

\[
\Bigl\{
u^i v^{-j}
\; ; \;
i\ge0,\,
j\ge1,\,
i+j\le d-2
\Bigr\}.
\]

Counting these monomials gives

\[
\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\sum_{i=0}^{d-3}
(d-2-i).
\]

Hence

\[
\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
1+2+\cdots +(d-2).
\]

Using

\[
1+2+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}2,
\]

we obtain

\[
\boxed{
	\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
	=
	\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}2.
}
\]

\subsection{Example: a smooth cubic curve}

Consider

\[
X=
V_+(x_0^3+x_1^3+x_2^3).
\]

Here

\[
d=3.
\]

Therefore

\[
\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(3-1)(3-2)}2
=
1.
\]

The condition

\[
i+j\le 1
\]

gives only one basis element:

\[
v^{-1}.
\]

Hence

\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
k\cdot v^{-1}.
\]

Thus

\[
\boxed{
	\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=1.
}
\]

This agrees with the fact that a smooth cubic curve has genus \(1\).

\subsection{Example: a smooth quartic curve}

Consider

\[
X=
V_+(x_0^4+x_1^4+x_2^4).
\]

Here

\[
d=4.
\]

Hence

\[
\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\frac{(4-1)(4-2)}2
=
3.
\]

The condition

\[
i+j\le 2
\]

gives the basis elements

\[
v^{-1},
\qquad
v^{-2},
\qquad
uv^{-1}.
\]

Therefore

\[
H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)
=
\operatorname{span}_k
\left\{
v^{-1},
v^{-2},
uv^{-1}
\right\}.
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
	\dim H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=3.
}
\]

This agrees with the genus formula for smooth plane quartics.

\section{Kähler Differentials: Explicit Examples}

Let \(A\) be a ring, \(B\) an \(A\)-algebra, and \(M\) a \(B\)-module. An \(A\)-derivation is a map

\[
D:B\to M
\]

such that

\[
D(b+b')=D(b)+D(b'),
\]

\[
D(bb')=bD(b')+b'D(b),
\]

and

\[
D(a)=0
\qquad
\text{for all }a\in A.
\]

The module of relative differentials \(\Omega_{B/A}\) is the universal object for such derivations.

\subsection{Example 1: The affine parabola}

Let

\[
A=k,
\qquad
B=k[x,y]/(y-x^2).
\]

Geometrically,

\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}B
\]

is the affine parabola

\[
y=x^2
\]

inside

\[
\mathbb A^2_k=\operatorname{Spec}k[x,y].
\]

The ideal defining this closed subscheme is

\[
I=(y-x^2)\subset k[x,y].
\]

Start with

\[
R=k[x,y].
\]

For affine space,

\[
\Omega_{R/k}=R\,dx\oplus R\,dy.
\]

Now pass to

\[
B=R/I.
\]

The relation

\[
y-x^2=0
\]

must also satisfy

\[
d(y-x^2)=0.
\]

Compute:

\[
d(y-x^2)=dy-2x\,dx.
\]

Therefore, in \(\Omega_{B/k}\),

\[
dy=2x\,dx.
\]

Hence

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}{(dy-2x\,dx)}.
\]

Since \(dy\) is determined by \(dx\), we get

\[
\Omega_{B/k}\cong B\,dx.
\]

The universal derivation

\[
d:B\to \Omega_{B/k}
\]

is given by

\[
d(x)=dx,
\qquad
d(y)=dy=2x\,dx.
\]

For example,

\[
d(x^3+y)=3x^2dx+dy.
\]

Using \(dy=2x\,dx\), this becomes

\[
d(x^3+y)=(3x^2+2x)dx.
\]

Now let

\[
M=B.
\]

Define a \(k\)-derivation

\[
D:B\to B
\]

by

\[
D(x)=1.
\]

Since \(y=x^2\), we must have

\[
D(y)=D(x^2)=2x.
\]

Thus

\[
D(x)=1,
\qquad
D(y)=2x.
\]

The universal property says that there exists a unique \(B\)-module homomorphism

\[
f:\Omega_{B/k}\to B
\]

such that

\[
D=f\circ d.
\]

Since

\[
\Omega_{B/k}\cong B\,dx,
\]

the map \(f\) is determined by

\[
f(dx)=1.
\]

Then

\[
f(dy)=f(2x\,dx)=2x.
\]

So

\[
D(x)=f(dx)=1,
\qquad
D(y)=f(dy)=2x.
\]

This confirms the universal property in this concrete case.

\subsection{Example 2: The nodal curve}

Let

\[
A=k,
\qquad
B=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^2(x+1)).
\]

Geometrically,

\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}B
\]

is the affine curve

\[
y^2=x^2(x+1)
\]

inside

\[
\mathbb A^2_k.
\]

It is defined by the ideal

\[
I=(y^2-x^2(x+1))\subset k[x,y].
\]

Let

\[
R=k[x,y],
\qquad
g=y^2-x^2(x+1).
\]

Then

\[
B=R/(g).
\]

As before,

\[
\Omega_{R/k}=R\,dx\oplus R\,dy.
\]

Passing to the quotient means that the differential of the defining equation must vanish:

\[
dg=0.
\]

Compute:

\[
g=y^2-x^3-x^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
dg=2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx.
\]

Hence in \(\Omega_{B/k}\),

\[
2y\,dy=(3x^2+2x)\,dx.
\]

So

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}
{\bigl(2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx\bigr)}.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}
{\bigl(-(3x^2+2x)\,dx+2y\,dy\bigr)}.
\]

The universal derivation is

\[
d:B\to \Omega_{B/k},
\]

with

\[
d(x)=dx,
\qquad
d(y)=dy.
\]

For example,

\[
d(xy)=x\,dy+y\,dx.
\]

Also,

\[
d(y^2)=2y\,dy,
\]

and

\[
d(x^2(x+1))=d(x^3+x^2)=(3x^2+2x)\,dx.
\]

Thus the relation

\[
y^2=x^2(x+1)
\]

forces

\[
2y\,dy=(3x^2+2x)\,dx.
\]

Now let

\[
M=B.
\]

A \(k\)-derivation

\[
D:B\to B
\]

is determined by its values

\[
D(x)=p,
\qquad
D(y)=q,
\]

where \(p,q\in B\), but these values must respect the relation.

Applying \(D\) to

\[
y^2-x^2(x+1)=0
\]

gives

\[
D(y^2-x^2(x+1))=0.
\]

Therefore

\[
2yq-(3x^2+2x)p=0.
\]

So derivations \(D:B\to B\) correspond to pairs

\[
(p,q)\in B^2
\]

satisfying

\[
2yq=(3x^2+2x)p.
\]

This is exactly the same relation as in the module of differentials.

The universal property says that each such derivation corresponds to a unique \(B\)-module map

\[
f:\Omega_{B/k}\to B.
\]

Indeed, define

\[
f(dx)=p,
\qquad
f(dy)=q.
\]

This is well-defined precisely when the relation in \(\Omega_{B/k}\) is preserved:

\[
f\bigl(2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx\bigr)=0.
\]

That condition is

\[
2yq-(3x^2+2x)p=0.
\]

Thus

\[
D=f\circ d.
\]

\subsection{Construction using \(I/I^2\)}

Now we explain the abstract construction.

Let

\[
\mu:B\otimes_A B\to B
\]

be the multiplication map

\[
\mu(b\otimes b')=bb'.
\]

Let

\[
I=\ker(\mu).
\]

Then

\[
\Omega_{B/A}=I/I^2.
\]

The universal derivation is

\[
d:B\to I/I^2
\]

defined by

\[
db=1\otimes b-b\otimes 1.
\]

This satisfies

\[
d(b+b')=db+db',
\]

and

\[
d(bb')=b\,db'+b'\,db.
\]

Therefore it is an \(A\)-derivation.

For the parabola,

\[
B=k[x,y]/(y-x^2).
\]

We have

\[
dx=1\otimes x-x\otimes 1,
\]

\[
dy=1\otimes y-y\otimes 1.
\]

Because

\[
y=x^2,
\]

we get

\[
dy=d(x^2)=2x\,dx.
\]

Thus

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}{(dy-2x\,dx)}.
\]

For the nodal curve,

\[
B=k[x,y]/(y^2-x^2(x+1)).
\]

The defining relation gives

\[
d(y^2-x^2(x+1))=0.
\]

Hence

\[
2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx=0.
\]

So

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}
{\bigl(2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx\bigr)}.
\]

\subsection{Geometric interpretation}

For the parabola,

\[
X=V(y-x^2)\subset \mathbb A^2,
\]

the module of differentials is generated by one element:

\[
\Omega_{B/k}\cong B\,dx.
\]

This reflects the fact that the parabola is smooth and one-dimensional.

The relation

\[
dy=2x\,dx
\]

means that the infinitesimal change of \(y\) is determined by the infinitesimal change of \(x\).

For the nodal curve,

\[
X=V(y^2-x^2(x+1))\subset \mathbb A^2,
\]

we have

\[
\Omega_{B/k}
=
\frac{B\,dx\oplus B\,dy}
{\bigl(2y\,dy-(3x^2+2x)\,dx\bigr)}.
\]

At the point

\[
(0,0),
\]

the coefficients of the relation vanish:

\[
2y=0,
\qquad
3x^2+2x=0.
\]

Thus the relation imposes no linear condition on \(dx\) and \(dy\) at the origin. This indicates that the tangent space at the origin is too large, which is the algebraic sign of a singular point.

Away from the singular point, the relation removes one tangent direction, so the curve behaves like a smooth one-dimensional curve.

\section{Problem 8: Closed Immersion Criterion via Sections}

Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field, let
\[
X\subseteq \mathbb P_k^m
\]
be a closed subscheme, and let
\[
\varphi:X\to \mathbb P_k^n
\]
be a morphism over $\operatorname{Spec}k$ induced by a line bundle
$\mathcal L$ and global sections
\[
s_0,\ldots,s_n\in \Gamma(X,\mathcal L).
\]

Let
\[
V\subseteq \Gamma(X,\mathcal L)
\]
be the $k$-vector subspace spanned by these sections.

Assume that $\mathcal L$ is generated by the sections $s_0,\ldots,s_n$,
so that $\varphi$ is defined everywhere.

We also assume:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Elements of $V$ separate points.
	That is, for any two distinct closed points $P,Q\in X$, there exists
	$s\in V$ such that
	\[
	s_P\in \mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P
	\]
	but
	\[
	s_Q\notin \mathfrak m_Q\mathcal L_Q,
	\]
	or vice versa.
	
	\item Elements of $V$ separate tangent vectors.
	That is, for every closed point $P\in X$, the set
	\[
	\{s\in V\mid s_P\in \mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P\}
	\]
	spans the $k$-vector space
	\[
	\mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P/\mathfrak m_P^2\mathcal L_P.
	\]
\end{enumerate}

We prove that
\[
\varphi:X\to \mathbb P_k^n
\]
is a closed immersion.

\subsection*{Step 1: Local form of the morphism}

Since the sections $s_0,\ldots,s_n$ generate $\mathcal L$, for every point
$P\in X$ there is at least one index $i$ such that
\[
s_i(P)\neq0.
\]

Let
\[
X_i=\{P\in X\mid s_i(P)\neq0\}.
\]

On $X_i$, the section $s_i$ trivializes $\mathcal L$. Therefore each ratio
\[
\frac{s_j}{s_i}
\]
is a regular function on $X_i$.

On this open set, the morphism $\varphi$ is given by
\[
P\longmapsto
\left[
\frac{s_0}{s_i}(P):
\cdots:
1:
\cdots:
\frac{s_n}{s_i}(P)
\right].
\]

Thus locally the morphism is described by the regular functions
\[
\frac{s_j}{s_i}.
\]

These functions are precisely the pullbacks of affine coordinates on the
standard chart
\[
D_+(y_i)\subseteq \mathbb P_k^n.
\]

\subsection*{Step 2: Point separation implies injectivity}

We show that condition (a) implies that $\varphi$ is injective on closed
points.

Let $P,Q\in X$ be distinct closed points.

By condition (a), there exists $s\in V$ such that, after possibly swapping
$P$ and $Q$,
\[
s_P\in \mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P
\]
and
\[
s_Q\notin \mathfrak m_Q\mathcal L_Q.
\]

This means that
\[
s(P)=0
\]
but
\[
s(Q)\neq0.
\]

Suppose, for contradiction, that
\[
\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q).
\]

Since $\varphi$ is defined by the projective coordinates
\[
[s_0:\cdots:s_n],
\]
the values of all sections in $V$ at $P$ and $Q$ must have the same
projective vanishing behavior.

In particular, if a section in $V$ vanishes at $P$, then the corresponding
linear form vanishes at $\varphi(P)$, hence also at $\varphi(Q)$, and so the
section vanishes at $Q$.

This contradicts the choice of $s$.

Therefore
\[
\varphi(P)\neq \varphi(Q).
\]

Hence $\varphi$ is injective on closed points.

\subsection*{Step 3: The image is closed}

Since
\[
X\subseteq \mathbb P_k^m
\]
is a closed subscheme of projective space, $X$ is projective over $k$.
In particular, $X$ is proper over $k$.

The target
\[
\mathbb P_k^n
\]
is separated over $k$.

A morphism from a proper scheme to a separated scheme has closed image.
Equivalently, using the fact allowed in the hint, since $X$ is projective,
the image
\[
\varphi(X)
\]
is closed in $\mathbb P_k^n$.

Let
\[
Y=\varphi(X)
\]
with its scheme-theoretic image structure.

Then $\varphi$ factors as
\[
X\xrightarrow{\psi}Y\xhookrightarrow{} \mathbb P_k^n.
\]

To prove that $\varphi$ is a closed immersion, it is enough to show that
\[
\psi:X\to Y
\]
is an isomorphism.

\subsection*{Step 4: Reduction to the structure sheaf}

The map $\psi:X\to Y$ is surjective by definition of $Y$.

By Step 2, it is injective on closed points.

Since $\psi$ is proper and quasi-finite on closed points, and since we are
allowed to use the hint that condition (a) implies that $\varphi$ is a closed
map onto its image, we obtain that $\psi$ is a homeomorphism on underlying
topological spaces.

Thus it remains to show that the morphism of structure sheaves
\[
\mathcal O_Y\longrightarrow \psi_*\mathcal O_X
\]
is an isomorphism.

Since $\psi$ is already a homeomorphism, it is enough to prove that for every
closed point $P\in X$, with
\[
Q=\psi(P)\in Y,
\]
the induced local ring map
\[
\mathcal O_{Y,Q}\longrightarrow \mathcal O_{X,P}
\]
is surjective.

Condition (b) is precisely what gives this surjectivity.

\subsection*{Step 5: Tangent separation and local coordinates}

Fix a closed point
\[
P\in X.
\]

Choose an index $i$ such that
\[
s_i(P)\neq0.
\]

Then $s_i$ trivializes $\mathcal L$ near $P$.

For every section $s\in V$, the quotient
\[
\frac{s}{s_i}
\]
is a regular function near $P$.

Moreover,
\[
s_P\in \mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P
\]
if and only if
\[
\frac{s}{s_i}\in \mathfrak m_P.
\]

Since $s_i(P)\neq0$, multiplication by $s_i^{-1}$ gives an isomorphism
\[
\mathfrak m_P\mathcal L_P/\mathfrak m_P^2\mathcal L_P
\cong
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

Condition (b) therefore says that the classes of the functions
\[
\frac{s}{s_i},
\qquad
s\in V,\quad s(P)=0,
\]
span the cotangent space
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

But the functions
\[
\frac{s_j}{s_i}
\]
are exactly the pullbacks of affine coordinate functions from the chart
\[
D_+(y_i)\subseteq \mathbb P_k^n.
\]

Hence the image of the local ring
\[
\mathcal O_{Y,Q}
\]
inside
\[
\mathcal O_{X,P}
\]
contains enough functions to generate
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

Equivalently, the induced map on cotangent spaces
\[
\mathfrak m_Q/\mathfrak m_Q^2
\longrightarrow
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2
\]
is surjective.

Dually, the tangent map
\[
T_PX\longrightarrow T_QY
\]
is injective.

Thus condition (b) says that $\varphi$ does not collapse infinitesimal
directions.

\subsection*{Step 6: Surjectivity of the local ring maps}

Let
\[
A=\mathcal O_{Y,Q},
\qquad
B=\mathcal O_{X,P}.
\]

We have a local homomorphism
\[
A\longrightarrow B.
\]

Its image contains constants and maps the maximal ideal of $A$ into the
maximal ideal of $B$.

From Step 5, the induced map
\[
\mathfrak m_A/\mathfrak m_A^2
\longrightarrow
\mathfrak m_B/\mathfrak m_B^2
\]
is surjective.

Equivalently, the image of $\mathfrak m_A$ generates
\[
\mathfrak m_B
\]
modulo $\mathfrak m_B^2$.

By Nakayama's lemma, the image of $\mathfrak m_A$ generates $\mathfrak m_B$
as a $B$-module.

Since the local functions coming from $Y$ contain these generators of
$\mathfrak m_B$, and since constants also come from $Y$, the local map
\[
\mathcal O_{Y,Q}\longrightarrow \mathcal O_{X,P}
\]
is surjective.

Therefore
\[
\mathcal O_Y\longrightarrow \psi_*\mathcal O_X
\]
is surjective.

Because $\psi$ is already a homeomorphism onto $Y$, this proves that
\[
\psi:X\to Y
\]
is a closed immersion whose underlying topological map is onto $Y$.

Hence $\psi$ is an isomorphism onto $Y$.

\subsection*{Step 7: Conclusion}

We have factored $\varphi$ as
\[
X\xrightarrow{\psi}Y\xhookrightarrow{} \mathbb P_k^n,
\]
where
\[
Y=\varphi(X)
\]
is a closed subscheme of $\mathbb P_k^n$.

We have shown that
\[
\psi:X\to Y
\]
is an isomorphism.

Therefore
\[
\varphi:X\to\mathbb P_k^n
\]
identifies $X$ with the closed subscheme $Y\subseteq\mathbb P_k^n$.

Hence
\[
\boxed{
	\varphi:X\to\mathbb P_k^n
	\text{ is a closed immersion.}
}
\]

\subsection*{Conceptual Summary}

The two hypotheses have clear geometric meanings.

Condition (a) says that the sections separate points:
\[
P\neq Q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\varphi(P)\neq\varphi(Q).
\]

Condition (b) says that the sections separate tangent directions:
\[
\text{no nonzero tangent vector is collapsed by }\varphi.
\]

Together, these imply that $\varphi$ embeds $X$ into projective space with
the correct scheme structure.

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\text{point separation}+\text{tangent separation}
	\Longrightarrow
	\text{closed immersion}.
}
\]
\section{Concrete Examples: Closed Immersion Criterion via Sections}

\subsection{Example 1: The Identity Embedding of \texorpdfstring{$\mathbb P^1$}{P1}}

Let
\[
X=\mathbb P^1_k
\]
with homogeneous coordinates
\[
[u:v].
\]

Take the line bundle
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1).
\]

The global sections of \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1)\) are spanned by
\[
u,\ v.
\]

Thus
\[
V=\langle u,v\rangle.
\]

These sections define the morphism
\[
\varphi:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^1
\]
given by
\[
[u:v]\longmapsto [u:v].
\]

Thus \(\varphi\) is the identity morphism.

\subsubsection*{Step 1: The sections generate \(\mathcal L\)}

At any point
\[
[u:v]\in\mathbb P^1,
\]
we cannot have both \(u=0\) and \(v=0\).

Therefore at least one of the two sections
\[
u,\ v
\]
is nonzero at every point.

Hence \(u\) and \(v\) generate
\[
\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1).
\]

Therefore \(\varphi\) is defined everywhere.

\subsubsection*{Step 2: The sections separate points}

Let
\[
P=[a:b],
\qquad
Q=[c:d]
\]
be two distinct points of \(\mathbb P^1\).

Since \(P\neq Q\), the vectors
\[
(a,b)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(c,d)
\]
are not scalar multiples.

Consider the section
\[
s=bu-av.
\]

Then
\[
s(P)=ba-ab=0.
\]

Now evaluate at \(Q\):
\[
s(Q)=bc-ad.
\]

If
\[
s(Q)=0,
\]
then
\[
bc-ad=0,
\]
which implies
\[
[a:b]=[c:d].
\]

This contradicts the assumption \(P\neq Q\).

Therefore
\[
s(P)=0,
\qquad
s(Q)\neq0.
\]

So the sections in \(V=\langle u,v\rangle\) separate points.

\subsubsection*{Step 3: The sections separate tangent vectors}

Take the point
\[
P=[1:0].
\]

On the affine chart \(u\neq0\), define the coordinate
\[
t=\frac vu.
\]

Then \(P\) corresponds to
\[
t=0.
\]

The local ring is
\[
\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1,P}=k[t]_{(t)}.
\]

Its maximal ideal is
\[
\mathfrak m_P=(t).
\]

Thus
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2
=
(t)/(t^2).
\]

This is a one-dimensional \(k\)-vector space generated by the class of \(t\).

Now we look at sections in \(V\) which vanish at \(P\). Since
\[
u(P)\neq0,
\qquad
v(P)=0,
\]
the section \(v\) vanishes at \(P\).

Trivializing \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1)\) by the nonvanishing section
\(u\), we get
\[
\frac vu=t.
\]

Modulo \((t^2)\), the class of \(t\) generates
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

Therefore the vanishing sections span the cotangent space at \(P\).

The same argument works at every point of \(\mathbb P^1\), after choosing a
chart where one of \(u\) or \(v\) is nonzero.

Hence the sections separate tangent vectors.

\subsubsection*{Conclusion}

The sections \(u,v\) generate \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(1)\), separate
points, and separate tangent vectors.

Therefore, by the closed immersion criterion,
\[
\varphi:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^1
\]
is a closed immersion.

Indeed,
\[
\boxed{
	\varphi([u:v])=[u:v]
}
\]
is simply the identity morphism.

\subsection{Example 2: The Degree Two Veronese Embedding}

Now take again
\[
X=\mathbb P^1_k,
\]
but use the line bundle
\[
\mathcal L=\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2).
\]

The global sections of \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2)\) are spanned by
\[
u^2,\ uv,\ v^2.
\]

Thus
\[
V=\langle u^2,uv,v^2\rangle.
\]

These sections define the morphism
\[
\varphi:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^2
\]
given by
\[
[u:v]\longmapsto [u^2:uv:v^2].
\]

This is the degree two Veronese embedding.

Let homogeneous coordinates on \(\mathbb P^2\) be
\[
[X:Y:Z].
\]

Then under \(\varphi\),
\[
X=u^2,
\qquad
Y=uv,
\qquad
Z=v^2.
\]

Therefore
\[
XZ-Y^2
=
u^2v^2-(uv)^2
=
0.
\]

So the image lies in the conic
\[
V(XZ-Y^2)\subseteq\mathbb P^2.
\]

\subsubsection*{Step 1: The sections generate \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2)\)}

At a point
\[
[u:v]\in\mathbb P^1,
\]
not both \(u\) and \(v\) are zero.

Therefore not all of
\[
u^2,\ uv,\ v^2
\]
vanish at the point.

Hence these sections generate
\[
\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2).
\]

Thus \(\varphi\) is defined everywhere.

\subsubsection*{Step 2: Point separation}

Let
\[
P=[a:b],
\qquad
Q=[c:d]
\]
be two points of \(\mathbb P^1\).

Their images are
\[
\varphi(P)=[a^2:ab:b^2],
\]
and
\[
\varphi(Q)=[c^2:cd:d^2].
\]

Suppose
\[
\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q).
\]

Then
\[
[a^2:ab:b^2]=[c^2:cd:d^2].
\]

We show that this implies
\[
[a:b]=[c:d].
\]

If \(a\neq0\), then on the chart \(X\neq0\),
\[
\frac{Y}{X}=\frac{ab}{a^2}=\frac ba.
\]

Thus the image determines
\[
\frac ba.
\]

Similarly, if \(c\neq0\), the equality of images gives
\[
\frac ba=\frac dc.
\]

Hence
\[
[a:b]=[c:d].
\]

If \(a=0\), then \(P=[0:1]\), and
\[
\varphi(P)=[0:0:1].
\]

Equality
\[
\varphi(Q)=[0:0:1]
\]
forces
\[
c=0,
\]
so
\[
Q=[0:1]=P.
\]

Thus in all cases,
\[
\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P=Q.
\]

Therefore \(\varphi\) is injective on points, and the sections separate
points.

\subsubsection*{Step 3: Tangent separation at \texorpdfstring{$P=[1:0]$}{P=[1:0]}}

Take
\[
P=[1:0].
\]

On the chart \(u\neq0\), use the coordinate
\[
t=\frac vu.
\]

Then
\[
[u:v]=[1:t].
\]

Under \(\varphi\),
\[
[1:t]\longmapsto [1:t:t^2].
\]

Now use the affine chart \(X\neq0\) in \(\mathbb P^2\), with affine
coordinates
\[
A=\frac YX,
\qquad
B=\frac ZX.
\]

Then the morphism becomes
\[
A=t,
\qquad
B=t^2.
\]

The local ring of \(\mathbb P^1\) at \(P\) is
\[
k[t]_{(t)}.
\]

Its maximal ideal is
\[
(t).
\]

The cotangent space is
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2=(t)/(t^2).
\]

Modulo \((t^2)\), we have
\[
A=t,
\qquad
B=t^2\equiv0.
\]

Thus the pullback of the affine coordinate \(A\) gives the generator \(t\) of
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

Therefore the induced map on cotangent spaces is surjective.

Equivalently, the tangent map is injective.

So the morphism separates tangent vectors at \(P=[1:0]\).

\subsubsection*{Step 4: Tangent separation at an arbitrary point}

The same argument works at every point.

On any affine chart of \(\mathbb P^1\), after choosing a local coordinate
\(t\), the morphism has the local form
\[
t\longmapsto (t,t^2)
\]
up to a change of coordinates.

The important feature is that the first coordinate has linear term \(t\).

Therefore, modulo \(t^2\), the pullback of a coordinate function from
\(\mathbb P^2\) gives the generator of
\[
\mathfrak m_P/\mathfrak m_P^2.
\]

Hence the induced cotangent map is surjective at every point.

So the sections
\[
u^2,\ uv,\ v^2
\]
separate tangent vectors everywhere.

\subsubsection*{Step 5: The image is the conic}

We already showed that the image is contained in
\[
V(XZ-Y^2).
\]

Conversely, let
\[
[X:Y:Z]\in V(XZ-Y^2)\subseteq \mathbb P^2.
\]

If \(X\neq0\), then write
\[
t=\frac YX.
\]

Since
\[
XZ=Y^2,
\]
we have
\[
\frac ZX=\left(\frac YX\right)^2=t^2.
\]

Thus
\[
[X:Y:Z]=[1:t:t^2]=\varphi([1:t]).
\]

If \(X=0\), then the equation
\[
XZ-Y^2=0
\]
gives
\[
Y=0.
\]

So the point is
\[
[0:0:1]=\varphi([0:1]).
\]

Therefore
\[
\varphi(\mathbb P^1)=V(XZ-Y^2).
\]

\subsubsection*{Conclusion}

The sections
\[
u^2,\ uv,\ v^2
\]
generate \(\mathcal O_{\mathbb P^1}(2)\), separate points, and separate
tangent vectors.

Therefore the morphism
\[
\varphi:\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^2,
\qquad
[u:v]\mapsto [u^2:uv:v^2],
\]
is a closed immersion.

Its image is the closed conic
\[
XZ-Y^2=0.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{
	\mathbb P^1
	\cong
	V(XZ-Y^2)
	\subseteq
	\mathbb P^2.
}
\]

This gives a concrete example of the closed immersion criterion.
